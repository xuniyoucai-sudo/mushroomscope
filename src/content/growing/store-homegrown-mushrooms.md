## The short answer: cool, clean, dated, and not overhandled

Homegrown mushrooms are perishable produce after harvest. Put a sound crop into a clean container, label the harvest date, and refrigerate it promptly. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) specifically lists mushrooms among the perishable produce that should be kept in a clean refrigerator at **40°F / 4°C or below**.

This is a storage guide for cultivated culinary mushrooms from an authenticated culture. It does not make an unidentified wild mushroom edible, validate a homegrown crop with an uncertain history, or replace a supplier's storage direction. If the crop looked suspect before harvest—unexpected mold, slime, leakage, pests, or a persistent foul odor—treat it as a safety problem, not a storage problem. See the [contamination guide](/growing/mushroom-contamination-guide/) for the isolation boundary.

## Start with a clean harvest boundary

Storage quality is set before the refrigerator door opens. Use clean hands, a visually clean knife or harvest tool, and a clean food-contact container. The North Carolina Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services notes that mushrooms move through harvesting, packing, storage, and transport as produce, and identifies sanitary tools, containers, refrigeration sanitation, and record keeping as part of the food-safety workflow.

That is why a freshly picked cluster should not be placed on a cutting board that just held raw chicken, or in a container with old produce debris. Keep mushrooms separate from raw meat, poultry, seafood, and the utensils used with them. If you sell mushrooms, local rules and commercial produce-safety obligations may be stricter than this home-oriented guide.

### Do not use a rinse to fix dirty handling

Wash hands before and after handling food. If you decide to rinse mushrooms before cooking, use clean running water shortly before preparation—not soap, detergent, or a commercial produce wash. The FDA says washing can reduce bacteria on produce but does not eliminate it, so washing is not a way to salvage a questionable crop or a substitute for clean harvesting.

For routine storage, follow the culture supplier's packaging instructions if supplied. The useful decision is not “paper versus plastic” as a universal rule; it is whether the container stays clean, avoids raw-food cross-contact, and does not leave the crop sitting in visible water or persistent condensation.

## Refrigerate promptly and verify the temperature

Move harvested mushrooms to a clean refrigerator as soon as practical. FDA food-storage guidance says to refrigerate or freeze perishables right away and not leave foods requiring refrigeration at room temperature for more than two hours—one hour when air temperature is above 90°F / 32°C. It also advises keeping the refrigerator at or below 40°F / 4°C and checking it with an appliance thermometer.

Use those values as a safety boundary, not as a promise about exact shelf life. Species, maturity, harvest cleanliness, moisture, packaging, and refrigerator performance all affect quality. Rather than relying on a generic “good for seven days” claim, write the harvest date down and use the crop promptly according to the supplier's direction and its actual condition.

## Handle moisture without creating a false sense of safety

Fresh mushrooms can lose quality when they are bruised, held warm, or kept wet. Do not soak harvested mushrooms, leave them in puddled water, or let a reusable container remain visibly dirty. The mushroom produce-safety fact sheet warns that humid mushroom environments can support pathogens and calls for preventing standing water and condensation as well as cleaning harvest utensils, shelves, and reusable packing containers.

If condensation appears, do not conclude that the food is unsafe solely from a wet surface; instead, check the storage history, clean or replace the container if appropriate, and follow the supplier's direction. If the crop is slimy, leaking, moldy, or persistently off-smelling, discard it rather than trying to dry it out or cut away a visible area.

## Freeze only a sound, promptly handled crop

Freezing is for a crop that was handled safely first—not for resetting the clock on food left warm or showing spoilage. FDA guidance states that freezing stops bacterial growth but does not kill most bacteria, and quality can decline over time. Prepare and freeze mushrooms using a reliable, species-appropriate cooking or preservation method; then label the package with the date and method.

If a refrigerator has lost power, keep the door closed as much as possible and use a thermometer or the applicable food-safety guidance to decide what to keep. Do not use smell or appearance as the deciding test. When the handling history is uncertain, the safer choice is to discard the food.

## A simple after-harvest record

One short label prevents a lot of guesswork. Record the culture or product label, harvest date and time, refrigerator temperature check, container change if any, and whether the crop was frozen or cooked. This is especially useful after an unusually warm harvest day or a power interruption.

The record also keeps storage separate from cultivation troubleshooting. If the crop formed poorly, revisit [mushroom-growing troubleshooting](/growing/mushroom-growing-troubleshooting/) and the [harvest guide](/growing/when-to-harvest-mushrooms/) with the supplier's strain-specific information. Do not change a culture's fruiting settings based on a refrigerator-storage issue.
