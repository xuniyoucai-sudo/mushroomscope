# Mushroom Yield and Biological Efficiency: Calculate Comparable Batches

## Yield needs a defined denominator

Yield is meaningful only when numerator and denominator are defined. Fresh mushrooms per bag, per square meter, per labor hour, or per kilogram of dry substrate answer different questions. Biological efficiency, commonly abbreviated BE, compares fresh mushroom mass with dry substrate mass. It is useful for cultivated saprotroph batches, but it is not a universal score of profit, nutrition, or environmental impact.

## Biological efficiency uses dry substrate mass

The usual calculation is fresh mushroom weight divided by dry substrate weight, multiplied by 100 percent. A batch producing 0.8 kilograms fresh mushrooms from 1.0 kilogram dry ingredients has 80 percent BE. Water added to hydrate the substrate is excluded from the dry denominator. State whether spawn and supplements are included, because inconsistent denominator rules make comparisons misleading.

## Measure dry matter instead of guessing

Supplier pellet moisture, wet grain spawn, and other ingredients are not perfectly dry. For serious comparisons, determine or obtain dry-matter fractions and calculate each ingredient’s dry contribution. Retain the method, sample, scale resolution, and date. A nominal ten-pound wet block cannot be converted to BE without knowing how much of that weight was water.

## Define what enters harvest weight

Set harvest boundaries before weighing. Decide whether substrate attached to bases is trimmed, whether aborted or damaged mushrooms count, and whether weight is recorded before cooling. Use a tared clean container and a checked scale. Gross biological yield, marketable grade, trim loss, samples, and discarded contaminated product belong in separate fields.

## Track flushes and time

Record each flush by harvest date and mass, plus inoculation, initiation, first pin, and room exit dates. Cumulative BE can hide a slow third flush that occupies valuable space. Yield per room-day, bag-day, or labor hour may better explain operational value. Do not terminate a batch differently merely to favor one comparison.

## Quality and saleable yield differ from gross yield

Large watery fruit bodies can raise fresh mass while having poor texture or shelf life. Track grade, cap or tooth condition, cluster form, contamination, packaging loss, and saleable mass. Refrigeration causes moisture loss, so harvest and sale weights should not be mixed. The [storage guide](/growing/store-homegrown-mushrooms/) explains postharvest handling boundaries.

Record the input stage with equal care. The [grain spawn guide](/growing/mushroom-grain-spawn-guide/) explains how spawn quality and rate enter a production run; biological efficiency is interpretable only when those batch variables are attached to the harvest record.

## Compare only like production systems

Compare the same species and strain, substrate recipe, bag size, spawn definition, environmental stage, harvest standard, and flush window. Published BE figures from optimized research or commercial systems may not transfer to a home setup. Replicate bags and report variation; one exceptional bag is not a reliable recipe result.

## Use the calculation to improve one variable

Use records to choose one next experiment: hydration, supplement rate, spawn rate, bag density, cut pattern, environment, or flush duration. Hold the rest stable and predefine the success measure. Higher BE is not worthwhile if contamination, labor, energy, poor grade, or longer room occupancy outweighs it. Cornell’s enterprise framework connects biological performance with cost and time.

## Worked calculation with moisture-corrected ingredients

Suppose a batch uses 800 grams of pellets documented at 8 percent moisture and 200 grams of supplement at 10 percent moisture. The dry contributions are 736 grams and 180 grams, for 916 grams total before any consistently defined dry spawn contribution. If the agreed harvest window produces 824 grams of fresh mushrooms, BE is 824 divided by 916, multiplied by 100: about 90 percent. Report the unrounded inputs and denominator convention, not only the result.

Now separate operational outcomes. If 40 grams were trimmed with substrate, 60 grams were damaged, and 724 grams met the chosen saleable grade, gross fresh yield, trim, discard, and saleable yield should occupy different fields. If a third flush adds 70 grams but holds the room for twelve more days, cumulative BE rises while yield per room-day may fall. Both results are true; each answers a different management question.

Scale error also affects comparisons. Verify zero and tare, use a capacity and resolution appropriate to the batch, keep containers dry, and check with suitable reference weights under the manufacturer’s procedure. A kitchen scale that rounds heavily may be adequate for harvest but not for a small dry supplement. Record units at collection rather than converting from memory later.

When publishing a result, include the species and strain, number of bags, failures, dry-matter method, spawn convention, harvest window, flush count, mean, range or other variation, and quality definition. These details let another grower decide whether the comparison applies instead of copying a percentage without its production boundary.

### What is biological efficiency?

Fresh mushroom mass divided by dry substrate mass, multiplied by 100 percent.

### Why use dry substrate mass?

It removes added water from the denominator so hydrated batches can be compared more consistently.

### Do all flushes count?

Count the flush window defined before the comparison and report it explicitly.

### Should contaminated mushrooms count as yield?

Track gross growth and discarded or saleable mass separately; never market suspect product.

### Is higher BE always better?

No. Time, labor, energy, contamination, quality, and saleable yield also matter.
