## Start with the underside, then keep going

In field-guide use, a bolete is usually recognized by a cap-and-stem form with a spongy surface of tube openings (pores) under the cap rather than thin gills. That observation is useful, but it is only a starting category. The USDA Forest Service description of king bolete, for example, distinguishes its pore-filled tube layer from gills and also relies on pore color, a thick stem, and other traits. A photograph of brown cap plus pores cannot reliably name a species.

Treat this page as a documentation protocol, not a foraging rule. Bolete names, combinations of features, and possible species vary by region. Keep a specimen unidentified when habitat, pore surface, flesh reaction, stem details, or season does not fit a current local key.

## Confirm a pore surface

Turn the mushroom over before judging the cap. Photograph the underside in even light and look for a layer of many small openings leading into tubes. Record the following without converting them into a verdict:

- **Pore color and shape:** Note fresh and mature color, whether openings are round or angular, and whether they change where touched.
- **Tube layer:** Take a side view or a clean cross-section so the depth and attachment of the layer can be seen.
- **Cap and stem:** Measure the cap and stem, then note texture, net-like reticulation, dots, or color zones.
- **Flesh reaction:** Make one fresh cut, photograph it immediately and again after a short interval, and state exactly where any color appears.

Color shifts are characters, not safety labels. USGS documentation of *Exsudoporus frostii* records blue staining when injured, while the USDA Forest Service describes king bolete pores as white when young and not blue-bruising. Those contrasting examples show why one reaction cannot settle a species name or edibility question.

## Build a comparison record

Use a repeatable record that another person can review. Montana State University recommends collecting the complete specimen and documenting features that might otherwise be missed. Where collection is legal, keep the stem base intact; photograph the mushroom in place first.

| Record | What to capture | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Habitat | Soil or wood association, nearby trees, site, date, and weather | Ecology can eliminate possibilities, but does not prove one. |
| Cap | Width, color in natural light, texture, cracks, margin, and age | Cap color alone is variable and easily misleading. |
| Pores and tubes | Close underside image, pore color, attachment, and a cross-section | This confirms the structure being described and preserves age-related changes. |
| Stem | Full length, base, surface pattern, and dimensions | Reticulation or dots may be useful only in a full regional comparison. |
| Cut and print | Timed images of a fresh cut; a spore print when safe and useful | Reactions and spore color add evidence, not a shortcut. |

The [mushroom photo checklist](/identification/mushroom-photo-checklist/) expands this into a reusable set of field images. If the underside is not genuinely pore-bearing, switch to the broader [mushroom identification guide](/identification/how-to-identify-mushrooms/) rather than forcing it into a bolete comparison.

## Use staining carefully

Pressing pores or cutting flesh can produce a reaction immediately, slowly, weakly, or not at all. Record the initial color, the location of contact, elapsed time, and light conditions. Avoid repeated bruising, which can make a subtle reaction look stronger or muddy the color.

Michigan State University Extension notes that some bolete descriptions use red-to-orange pores and blue staining as cautionary features for collectors. That is conservative field advice, not a universal identification key. It does not mean every blue-staining bolete is toxic, every non-blue bolete is edible, or that a phone image can replace a regional identification. Do not taste-test or cook a specimen to resolve uncertainty.

## Lookalikes are a comparison problem

Several mushrooms and fungal groups can have pores. A shelf-like polypore on wood, a pored mushroom without a central stem, or a gilled mushroom photographed poorly may not belong in the same comparison set as a ground-growing bolete. Start by describing the structure rather than by selecting a name.

Within boletes, similar cap colors can conceal differences in pore color, stem pattern, host association, flesh reaction, odor, and microscopic characters. A useful answer may therefore be “pore-bearing mushroom, species unresolved.” That conclusion protects the specimen record from false certainty and gives a local identifier better evidence to work with.

## Consumption and poisoning safety

Never use pores, a blue reaction, a popular rule of thumb, an app score, or a social-media consensus as an edibility test. Poison Control advises that wild mushrooms should not be eaten unless identified by an expert; lookalikes can be convincing, and cooking does not make a poisonous mushroom safe.

If someone may have eaten an unidentified mushroom, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Preserve uncooked material, leftovers, packaging, and photographs only if it is safe to do so. Do not wait for an online identification before seeking help.
