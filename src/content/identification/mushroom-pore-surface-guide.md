## Start with the tubes, not the resemblance

A pore surface is the visible end of a layer of microscopic spore-producing tubes. In a typical bolete, that layer hangs beneath a fleshy cap and can sometimes be separated from the cap flesh. In many polypores, the tubes are built into a shelf, crust, or tough stalked fruit body attached to wood. The small openings may be round, angular, stretched, split, or arranged as a maze.

That architecture is more useful than saying a mushroom “looks spongy.” Photograph the surface straight on, measure it, section through it, and record what supports the fruit body. A perforated underside narrows a field problem, but it does not establish a species or make a specimen edible. Some poroid mushrooms cause gastrointestinal poisoning; others are too tough, bitter, old, or contaminated for food. Use this page to create an evidence record, then apply a current regional key and seek in-person expertise.

The [bolete identification guide](/identification/bolete-identification-guide/) focuses on fleshy, stemmed boletes. The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) explains true blades and their attachment. This guide instead compares poroid construction across boletes and wood-decay fungi.

## Confirm that the surface is actually poroid

Look across the surface with side lighting. True pores open into tubes; a shallow honeycomb, cracked skin, insect damage, or wrinkles may imitate holes in a flat photograph. Cut a narrow vertical section through the cap or bracket where collection is lawful. A tube layer appears as many parallel channels beneath the fertile face. Record its depth and whether it peels away, changes color, or forms visibly different annual layers.

Elongated pores can resemble gills. Follow several ridges: in a daedaloid or maze-like surface, cross-walls repeatedly join them into closed cells. True gills are plates radiating through the underside and have their own attachment pattern at the stem. Tooth fungi bear individual spines rather than holes. Chanterelles have blunt folds, not tubes. These distinctions are structural and should survive changes in camera angle.

Do not force a binary label from a weathered specimen. Pores may split into teeth as tissue erodes, become clogged by spores or debris, or stretch near a point of attachment. Record mixed zones and photograph the transition.

## Measure pore shape and density

### Put a scale in the same plane

Place a millimeter rule beside the fertile surface without pressing it. Photograph at a right angle so the ruler and pores share a focal plane. Count pores along several 5 mm spans and convert to pores per millimeter if the key requests that unit. Avoid choosing only the largest, cleanest patch. Report a range and note where it was measured: margin, middle, or near the stem.

Pore density often changes as a fruit body expands. Young marginal tissue may be fine and nearly invisible while central pores are broad and angular. Drought can shrink openings; saturation can swell or distort them. Counts from one old bracket should not be generalized to every stage.

### Use precise shape words

Round pores have curved, separate openings. Angular pores form polygonal cells. Radially elongated pores stretch away from a stem or attachment. Daedaloid pores create a maze. Irpicoid surfaces break into flattened teeth. Describe the dominant form and exceptions instead of selecting the most dramatic word.

Record whether walls are thick, thin, entire, torn, or glistening. A hand lens helps, but aggressive digital sharpening can invent edges. Save an uncropped original and write down the lens or magnification used.

## Record color before and after handling

Photograph untouched pores in diffuse natural light beside a neutral reference. Then press a small labeled area with consistent pressure and make images immediately, after one minute, and after ten or fifteen minutes. Note whether the reaction is blue, green-blue, brown, red, black, or absent, and whether it occurs in pores, tubes, cap flesh, or stem.

A blue reaction is not a species name and not a universal toxicity signal. Its speed, intensity, tissue location, and chemistry vary among bolete lineages. Brown handling marks on a white polypore may reflect ordinary bruising, age, or moisture. Compare the mark with an untouched control on the same fruit body.

Cut surfaces can change differently from the pore face. Photograph the section promptly, then repeat under the same light. Do not taste an unknown specimen, and keep any chemically tested material away from food.

## Separate a fleshy bolete from a wood-decay conk

Most familiar boletes rise from soil on a central stem and are mycorrhizal partners of trees. Their fruit bodies are comparatively fleshy and seasonal. Record nearby trees, but do not assign a host from proximity alone; roots overlap, and ornamental plantings can hide the relevant partner. Dig only enough to preserve the stem base where collecting is permitted.

Many polypores grow directly from wood. Photograph the exact attachment before removal. Determine whether the wood is living or dead, standing or fallen, hardwood or conifer, and whether bark remains. A bracket emerging at ground level may be attached to a buried root. Expose the connection gently instead of describing it as terrestrial.

Texture matters. A young annual bracket can be soft and watery; a perennial conk can be woody with multiple tube layers. Cut a thin radial section and measure cap flesh and tube depth separately. Concentric tube strata suggest repeated growth seasons, while one tube layer often indicates an annual fruit body, though a regional key must interpret the combination.

## Document attachment and the sterile margin

For stemmed specimens, record whether tubes meet the stem, dip around it, run down it, or terminate in a depressed zone. Photograph the junction from several angles. A detached tube layer or insect feeding can create a false gap. Measure the stem and note reticulation, scabers, dots, glandular marks, or smooth tissue.

For brackets, record whether the base is broad, narrowed, crust-like, or supported by a lateral stem. The growing margin may be white, rounded, sharp, lobed, or differently textured from older tissue. Photograph both a profile and the attachment. Do not wrench a large perennial conk from a living tree merely to inspect its back; habitat photographs and a small lawful sample may be sufficient.

A resupinate fungus lies flat against wood with pores facing outward. Some begin as crusts and develop small shelves. Map the extent, margin, cracks, and direction of wood grain. One close-up without the colony outline can erase the growth form.

## Connect pore evidence to wood and tree condition carefully

A fruit body documents fungal reproduction at a place and time. It does not by itself measure the remaining strength of a tree. Note fruit-body position, number, recurrence, cavities, cracks, crown condition, and recent site disturbance. Arboricultural risk assessment requires the whole tree, target area, decay pattern, and professional inspection.

Host identification deserves its own evidence. Photograph bark, buds, leaves or needles, branching, and the attachment point. Dead logs can lose their easiest characters, so use cautious labels such as “deciduous log” when species cannot be supported. A wrong host can send a key toward the wrong fungus.

Do not scrape extensive pore surfaces, carve live trees, or remove rare fruit bodies for a casual record. Follow land rules and local collecting guidance. A small documented sample is more useful than an unlabelled bag of mixed brackets.

## Build a comparison that can survive review

Create one row per candidate and columns for growth form, substrate, host confidence, cap surface, pore shape, pores per millimeter, tube depth, bruising sequence, flesh texture, stem characters, season, and region. Fill cells with observations, not conclusions. Mark unknowns explicitly.

Reject candidates on contradictions rather than choosing the closest photograph. A supposed soil bolete physically attached to hardwood, or a candidate with one pore per millimeter when the specimen consistently has five, needs reconsideration. Taxonomy also changes: retain the source name, region, and date so another person can reconstruct the decision.

Microscopy may be needed for spore size, shape, ornament, cystidia, hyphal construction, or clamp connections. DNA data can resolve complexes but only when the specimen and sequence are properly documented. A database image or occurrence point is supporting context, not authentication of your collection.

## A repeatable pore-surface field workflow

1. Photograph the entire fruit body and exact substrate before touching it.
2. Make overview, profile, attachment, and straight-on pore images with a scale.
3. Count pores in several representative zones and describe their shape.
4. Mark one pressure point and photograph timed color changes.
5. Section a lawful sample to show tubes, flesh, and any layered growth.
6. Record associated trees, wood condition, date, weather, and region.
7. Keep each collection numbered and separate; make a spore deposit or microscopy preparation only when a key calls for it.
8. Compare the full bundle with a current regional key and seek expert confirmation.

This workflow is deliberately slower than image matching. It preserves the features that distinguish a useful record from a visually persuasive guess.

## Safety limits and emergency response

Never use “has pores” as an edibility rule. Do not consume an unidentified wild mushroom, a decayed specimen, or material collected from contaminated ground. Cooking does not reliably neutralize every toxin, and tolerance of one species does not validate another.

If someone may have eaten an unknown mushroom, contact the appropriate poison service or emergency medical provider promptly. Preserve uncooked material, cooked leftovers, photographs, collection location, and timing. Do not wait for online identification and do not induce vomiting unless a medical professional instructs you to do so.
