The **grooved bonnet**, *Mycena polygramma*, is a small gray-brown wood decomposer whose useful character is easy to lose in a photograph. Fine ribs run lengthwise along the slender stem. A bright flash can flatten those channels, a wet surface can turn them into glare, and a blurred stem can appear smooth. The grooves are therefore a feature to document, not a name to infer from a cap portrait.

This field guide explains how to build a reviewable record from substrate, structure, measurements, microscopy, and regional literature. It is not a food guide. No website can inspect every fruit body in a mixed collection or exclude all similar small mushrooms.

## Start with one numbered fruit body and its wood

Photograph the colony before touching it. Mark one fruit body as the reference and capture its cap, gills, full stem, and exact attachment without silently switching subjects between frames. Place a millimeter scale beside, not over, the mushroom. Record cap width and height, stem length and width, number of fruit bodies, occupied wood length, and whether the colony is scattered or tightly clustered.

Expose only enough litter to prove the substrate. A stem beside a stump may arise from buried bark, a root, a leaf petiole, or soil. Record whether the wood is hardwood or conifer, standing or fallen, barked or decorticated, and firm or cubically softened. The [habitat documentation guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) separates visible substrate evidence from a nearby tree assumption.

## Reveal the longitudinal stem channels

Use diffuse side light and brace the camera parallel to the stem. Take one frame dry-looking and another after natural surface moisture changes if the same fruit body remains intact. The relevant lines follow the stem axis; transverse cracks, adhering fibers, shadows from grass, and compression marks are different evidence. Trace several grooves continuously rather than pointing to one dark streak.

Rotate the specimen only when collection is lawful and necessary. Photograph at least three faces because ribs can be stronger on one side. Record whether the lines persist from apex to base, whether the stem is hollow or stuffed in section, and how color changes toward the base. Do not carve or scrape the surface to manufacture contrast.

## Record cap development rather than one color

Young caps are conical to bell-shaped and may broaden with age. Photograph fresh, intermediate, and older caps from one substrate unit. Note diameter, central profile, margin position, surface moisture, translucency, and the fraction of the radius occupied by visible striations. Gray, brown, and pale tones change with hydration and camera white balance, so include a neutral reference.

Cap striation is not the same as stem grooving. Translucent cap lines often reflect gills beneath thin tissue and occur across many bonnet species. A useful account states both structures independently and does not promote a strongly striped cap into proof of *M. polygramma*.

## Link the underside to the same cap

Make a low angled underside photograph before detaching anything. Count full-length gills in a defined sector, record shorter gills, and show how the gills meet the stem. The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) provides attachment vocabulary, but the image should carry the evidence. Overexposure can erase pale gill edges and make spacing impossible to judge.

Check whether neighboring caps share the same attachment pattern. A mixed stump can support more than one decomposer, and small pale-gilled mushrooms may interleave. Keep every detached cap, stem, photograph, and spore preparation under one collection number so data from separate fungi are not combined.

## Compare bonnets with a feature matrix

The [common bonnet guide](/mushrooms/common-bonnet-mushroom-guide/) and clustered bonnet account provide useful comparisons, not automatic exclusions. Build a matrix with colony density, cap profile, cap striation, stem grooves, stem color zones, gill attachment, substrate, odor, spore dimensions, cystidia, and cap-surface tissues. Mark unobserved characters as unknown rather than borrowing them from a guide.

Dense clustering can support another bonnet hypothesis, but colony form changes with available cracks in wood. Size overlaps, colors fade, and rain removes surface clues. A regional key should determine which microscopic structures and measurements separate the local species concept.

## Make a clean white spore deposit

Place a mature cap over clean black glass or half black and half transparent material, shield it from drafts, and label start time and collection number. Keep a blank control nearby. A faint white deposit is easily confused with gill fragments, mold, dust, or condensation residue. Photograph before transferring material.

Color narrows the field but does not confirm the species. For microscopy, calibrate the scale and state mount medium, magnification, number measured, full observed range, and whether spores came from the deposit or gill tissue. Record the cystidia and pileipellis characters required by the chosen key instead of collecting attractive but non-diagnostic images.

## Test wood and weather boundaries over repeat visits

Mark a short observation strip on the stump without damaging it. At each visit record rainfall interval, surface moisture, wood temperature, fresh caps, collapsed caps, and remaining stem bases. Photograph from the same direction with the same scale. This reveals how a flush occupies cracks and moisture zones without pretending the visible fruit bodies map the underground mycelium.

Compare an adjacent apparently suitable wood section that did not fruit. The contrast helps separate a simple “wet woodland” description from a specific substrate observation. It still cannot prove cause; wood chemistry, decay organisms, and internal moisture remain unmeasured.

## Use records and sequences with appropriate limits

GBIF can show where named occurrence records have been submitted, while NCBI connects names with sequence-linked taxonomy. Filter records by voucher, identifier, date, coordinate precision, and taxonomic revision. A map point is a lead to local literature, not validation of a new photograph. Sequence similarity also depends on locus quality, reference annotation, and the species concept used.

First Nature supplies a European account emphasizing the fluted stem and broadleaf wood. Outside that frame, consult local monographs, herbaria, or a mycological society. Keep the label at *Mycena* when the stem surface, substrate, or microscopic packet is incomplete.

## Quantify grooves without turning them into a species score

Choose a straight, undamaged 10 mm segment at mid-stem and photograph it at fixed magnification under light from four compass directions. On each frame, count only channels that continue through most of the segment; record interrupted ribs, adhering fibers, and glare separately. Repeat on at least three mature stems from the same numbered collection. Report the range rather than selecting the specimen with the most dramatic surface.

This method makes the central character reviewable, but groove count should not become a universal threshold. Hydration, age, compression, camera angle, and stem expansion change what is visible. The result belongs beside cap form, gill attachment, wood, spore data, cystidia, pileipellis, and regional alternatives. If channels disappear when the light direction changes, treat them as shadow artifacts. If they remain continuous on several faces and several stems, they are stronger morphological evidence while still not proving the species alone.

## Keep safety and collection claims narrow

Small gray bonnet mushrooms are not appropriate targets for casual food collection. The difficulty is not solved by finding one matching picture: several fruit bodies can be mixed, decisive structures may be microscopic, and unknown compounds or spoilage remain possible. Do not taste a specimen to complete an online checklist.

If illness follows mushroom ingestion, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Retain uncooked material, leftovers, packaging, photographs, location, and timing. Do not wait for a crowd-sourced name before seeking care. Collect only where permitted and take the minimum material needed for a documented voucher.

## Build a reviewable grooved-bonnet record

1. Photograph the colony and exact wood before disturbance.
2. Measure one numbered cap and complete stem.
3. Use side light to trace several longitudinal grooves.
4. Link cap, gills, stem, and base to the same specimen.
5. Produce a controlled white spore deposit.
6. Add calibrated microscopy required by a regional key.
7. Compare nearby bonnet species in a character matrix.
8. Preserve uncertainty when decisive evidence is missing.

The strongest record does not merely announce *Mycena polygramma*. It shows why that hypothesis is stronger than nearby alternatives and identifies exactly which unmeasured character could still change the name.
