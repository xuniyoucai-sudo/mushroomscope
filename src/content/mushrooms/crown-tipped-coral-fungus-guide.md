Crown-tipped coral fungus is one of the few coral-shaped fungi whose common name points to a genuinely useful structure. The terminal branches of *Artomyces pyxidatus* often widen into tiny cups ringed with short points, like a miniature crown. That feature is persuasive only when it repeats across intact tips and is connected to the rest of the evidence: growth from hardwood, overall branching, fresh texture, region, and microscopy when the record needs a species name.

This guide is for documenting that evidence, not for approving food use from a photograph. Coral fungi contain many convergent forms, damaged tips lose their most recognizable character, and buried wood can make a wood-decayer appear to grow from soil.

## Confirm that the colony is attached to wood

Photograph the fruit bodies before moving litter. Follow one base down to its attachment and record whether the substrate is a log, branch, stump, root, or buried processed wood. Include bark, exposed end grain, diameter, decay stage, and any features that support a hardwood identification. Do not name the tree from one loose leaf resting nearby.

The wood connection separates this hypothesis from many terrestrial *Ramaria* and *Clavulina* corals. It is not absolute proof, because other coral-like wood decomposers exist and a branch may be deeply buried. The [habitat documentation guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) provides a repeatable way to distinguish the fruiting substrate from the surrounding scenery.

Map separate clusters on the same log. A long trunk can host more than one fungus, and overlapping bases should not be merged into a single observation without evidence. Note whether the wood is suspended, soil-contacting, waterlogged, sun-exposed, or shaded because moisture and temperature shape the fruit body's color and persistence.

## Inspect the crown-shaped tips at several scales

Use diffuse natural light and photograph a complete branch system, then a close macro of at least ten undamaged terminals. A typical tip has a shallow central depression surrounded by several short pointed projections. Young growing tips may be less open, while rain, slugs, insects, and handling can erase the crown.

Count and describe rather than declaring every fork a crown. Record the proportion of clearly crowned tips, branch width below the terminal, and whether the tips are erect, flattened, toothed, or simply divided. A few damaged star-like ends in an otherwise different coral are weak evidence. Repetition across multiple fresh branches is much stronger.

Phone processing can create bright halos along pale edges. Keep an unsharpened original and include a scale. The cover image illustrates the intended architecture, but its many perfect crowns are editorial context and cannot supply measurements for a real collection.

## Read branching from the base upward

Trace one branch from the shared base to its terminal divisions. Record the number of branching levels, the angle at each junction, the degree of parallel upright growth, and whether neighboring branches fuse. Measure total height and width as well as the thickest basal and thinnest terminal branches.

Avoid treating the colony as a single decorative silhouette. Branching architecture may change between crowded and open parts of the same log. Photograph a side view and a top view so the record distinguishes genuinely cup-shaped tips from branches that only overlap in projection.

Fresh fruit bodies are usually pale cream to buff and can acquire tan, ochre, or faint pinkish tones. Color is subordinate evidence. White balance, shade, drying, and age can shift it substantially, while several unrelated coral fungi occupy the same palette.

## Check the fertile surface and fresh texture

*Artomyces* bears its fertile surface across the exposed branches. There should be no true gills, no discrete pore layer, and no ranks of pendant teeth. Use a hand lens to inspect the branch surface and junctions. Soil grains or water droplets can mimic texture in a photograph, so clean only a small study area without washing the whole specimen.

Cut a lawful representative branch and record whether the flesh is solid, chambered, brittle, fibrous, or gelatinous. Note odor without tasting. Fresh texture and bruising behavior can support a key, but neither is a safe standalone test. Label the section to the exact cluster and photograph it before discoloration or drying changes the surface.

The [coral tooth guide](/mushrooms/coral-tooth-mushroom-guide/) offers a useful structural contrast: *Hericium coralloides* also grows from hardwood and branches, but it produces conspicuous hanging teeth. Smooth crowned terminals and toothed fertile branches are different construction plans.

## Separate terrestrial coral-fungus lookalikes

Many *Ramaria* species develop substantial branched fruit bodies in soil, litter, or duff near trees. Their tips may fork repeatedly, and some change color or bruise. Several require microscopic characters and specialist regional literature; some have histories of gastrointestinal effects. A cream coral with pointed forks is not automatically crown-tipped coral fungus.

*Clavulina* species can be pale, gray, or whitish and often have crested or irregular tips. They commonly arise from soil and litter as mycorrhizal fungi rather than decomposing a hardwood log. Substrate, branch form, spores, and hyphal characters matter more than a broad resemblance.

Do not force a species name when the attachment is missing or all tips are damaged. “Coral fungus on unknown substrate” with complete photographs is a more useful record than a confident but unsupported *Artomyces* label.

## Use a white spore deposit carefully

A white deposit is compatible with crown-tipped coral fungus but not exclusive to it. If collection is lawful, place fresh branch sections over dark glass or foil in a protected humid chamber, keeping the base and source cluster labeled. A faint deposit can be difficult to distinguish from fragments of pale tissue.

For microscopy, report the mount, reagent, calibration, magnification, number measured, and observed range. Spore shape and ornament, basidia, cystidial structures, hyphal system, and clamp connections can help resolve lookalikes. Copying a published range into a record is not microscopy; keep observed values separate from reference values.

Sequence-linked and occurrence databases are starting points, not automated confirmations. NCBI can connect names with sequence records, while GBIF exposes source occurrences whose verification quality varies. A regionally unusual report deserves a voucher and expert review.

## Document hardwood decay without overstating cause

Record the log's decay class, remaining bark, wood firmness, contact with soil, moisture, canopy, and other visible decomposers. Crown-tipped coral fungus participates in wood decay, but a fruit body does not prove that it initiated a tree's decline or that every soft part of the log was decomposed by the same organism.

Repeated visits can show how quickly the branches expand, discolor, collapse, or are eaten. Mark positions on a sketch rather than altering the colony. Photograph after dry weather and after rain with comparable framing. That sequence improves ecological interpretation without turning appearance changes into false taxonomic distinctions.

Leave most of the colony intact. The fruit body is a reproductive structure and part of the log's invertebrate habitat. Protected sites may prohibit collection; a complete photographic record can still be valuable.

## Keep food claims behind a verified identity

Some regional field guides describe young crown-tipped coral fungus as edible. That statement is not permission to eat a collection identified online. The observer still needs a current local identification, clean substrate, fresh condition, lawful harvest, and a conservative assessment of lookalikes and individual tolerance.

Do not use taste as an identification test. Avoid old, insect-damaged, waterlogged, polluted, or roadside material, and do not assume cooking fixes an identification error. If illness follows mushroom ingestion, contact local emergency or poison-control services promptly and retain uncooked material, meal remnants, photographs, timing, and collection details.

## Build a reviewable field record

1. Photograph the entire colony and log before disturbing litter.
2. Establish hardwood attachment with bark, wood grain, and decay-stage evidence.
3. Record repeated crown tips across multiple undamaged branches.
4. Measure colony size, branch levels, junction angles, and terminal width.
5. Inspect the branch surface for the absence of gills, pores, and hanging teeth.
6. Compare wood-attached and terrestrial coral groups explicitly.
7. Make a labeled spore deposit and calibrated microscopic record when needed.
8. Preserve uncertainty if the substrate, tips, or regional evidence is incomplete.

The diagnostic value lies in the combination. Crowned terminals without wood attachment are incomplete; hardwood growth without repeated crowns is equally incomplete. A connected record makes those limits visible.
