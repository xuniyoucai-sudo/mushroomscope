Cloud ear is a useful example of why a market name and a species name are not interchangeable. Packages labeled cloud ear, wood ear, black fungus, or hairy wood ear may contain different *Auricularia* species depending on region and supply chain. This guide focuses on *Auricularia cornea*, not every product sold under those names.

The page's independent intent is species and market-boundary clarification. The [wood ear guide](/mushrooms/wood-ear-mushroom-guide/) covers the separate *Auricularia heimuer* concept. Neither page should be used to relabel a package whose supplier has not identified the species.

## The cloud ear field profile

Fresh *A. cornea* forms pale tan to gray-brown gelatinous lobes on hardwood. Shapes range from ears to shallow cups or irregular overlapping shelves. The outer, nonfertile surface is finely hairy or velvety, while the inner fertile face is smoother and may wrinkle.

There are no gills, teeth, or pores and usually no true stem. The fruit body attaches laterally or through a narrowed point to wood. Thin margins may look slightly translucent under natural light.

## Examine both sides of each lobe

A top-only photograph misses the surface contrast. Photograph the matte hairy exterior and the smoother fertile interior, then show the attachment. Use oblique side light to reveal fine hairs without artificial oversharpening.

Rain can make the outer face look glossy, and handling can flatten hairs. Compare protected folds or younger lobes. A magnifier may help, but microscopic hair structure and spores are more reliable when close species must be separated.

## Shape changes with water and crowding

Fresh lobes expand after rain and shrink during dry weather. Closely packed fruit bodies press into cups, waves, or overlapping tiers. A solitary specimen may be more distinctly ear-shaped.

Shape therefore describes a range rather than a silhouette. A perfectly human-ear outline is not required, and an irregular jelly mass should not be assigned to *Auricularia* without checking surface, attachment, and microscopic traits.

## Fresh, dry, and rehydrated material look different

Fresh tissue is flexible, rubbery, and gelatinous. Drying makes it thin, hard, dark, and curled. Rehydration restores volume and flexibility but does not reconstruct every original diagnostic detail.

This transformation is useful in cooking and difficult in identification. A dried retail fragment has lost ecology, attachment, fresh color, and much surface texture. Supplier traceability is more meaningful than visual confidence from one rehydrated piece.

## Hardwood attachment is central

Wild fruit bodies occur on dead or weakened hardwood, fallen branches, logs, and stumps in humid tropical and subtropical settings. Record whether wood is living or dead, standing or fallen, and identifiable to host where possible.

A jelly fungus on conifer wood or soil changes the comparison set. So does an apparently wood-free specimen whose branch is buried. Include a wide habitat photograph as well as macro images.

## Climate and moisture drive fruiting

Warmth and rainfall can produce repeated flushes through broad parts of the year. A fixed Northern Hemisphere autumn calendar is less useful for a tropical species. Local rain pattern, elevation, monsoon timing, and wood moisture matter.

Record whether fruit bodies are fully hydrated after rain, partly dried, or re-expanding. That condition explains thickness and translucency better than assuming all differences are taxonomic.

## Geographic records need verification

GBIF supports taxonomic and occurrence context, but occurrence records vary in identification method and may preserve historical names. Use verified regional checklists and specimen-backed records before stating that *A. cornea* is established in a new area.

Cultivation and trade also move products far beyond natural fruiting ranges. A package sold in Europe or North America does not show that the species was grown or collected locally.

## Cloud ear versus A. heimuer wood ear

Both are cultivated foods and share overlapping common names. *A. cornea* is often described as paler, thicker, and hairier, while *A. heimuer* may be sold as darker black wood ear. Those tendencies are not a dependable package-level key because strain, growing conditions, maturity, processing, and drying change appearance.

For a scientific determination, combine origin, fresh morphology, hair structure, spore measurements, culture records, and molecular evidence where needed. For cooking, rely on an authenticated supplier label rather than trying to reverse-engineer a species from dried pieces.

## Other regional Auricularia species

Names such as *A. auricula-judae* occur in literature and commerce, and multiple regional species may resemble one another. Historical books sometimes applied a familiar European name broadly to specimens elsewhere.

Modern revisions mean old occurrence and recipe names require interpretation. Preserve the exact scientific name and source date used by a publication instead of silently updating every “wood ear” mention to *A. cornea*.

## Exidia and other jelly fungi

Many *Exidia* species form dark, amber, or translucent jelly cushions and brain-like masses on wood. They may lack the clear distinction between a hairy outer face and smoother ear interior. Some coalesce into broad patches rather than discrete ears.

Other jelly fungi can be leaf-like, spatula-shaped, or gelatinous crusts. Color and texture alone do not establish edibility. Substrate, fruit-body architecture, microscopic structures, and region remain necessary.

## A defensible field record

1. Photograph the branch or log and surrounding habitat.
2. Record region, date, rainfall, host condition, and wood identity if known.
3. Photograph hydrated and partly dry fruit bodies when both occur.
4. Capture outer hairs, inner fertile face, thickness, margin, and attachment.
5. Measure multiple lobes without selecting only the largest.
6. Make a spore deposit or collect microscopic evidence where appropriate.
7. Compare with a current regional *Auricularia* treatment and qualified expertise.

## Market labels and traceability

A useful retail label identifies the product, country or producer, lot, dried or fresh form, storage instructions, and allergen or facility information where applicable. “Black fungus” alone does not establish a species.

Keep packaging until the product is used. Traceability matters if quality is poor or an adverse event occurs. Do not mix an unlabeled wild collection with a commercial dried product and assume cooking makes them equivalent.

## Culinary texture is the principal mechanism

Authenticated cloud ear is valued more for crisp-gel texture than strong aroma. Rehydrated pieces retain resilience in soups, stir-fries, and salads that are subsequently cooked according to product guidance. Thin margins soften faster than thick bases, so size pieces consistently.

The [cooked-versus-raw guide](/health/cooked-vs-raw-mushrooms/) explains the broader safety distinction. This species guide does not endorse raw service or a universal rehydration time; follow the package and applicable food-safety guidance.

## Rehydrating a verified dried product

Use clean potable water and a clean covered container. Keep rehydration under temperature control specified by the supplier or local food-safety guidance; do not leave a perishable soaked product at room temperature for an extended period. Drain, rinse under running water if directed, and trim hard attachment points.

Discard product with abnormal odor, colored mold, damaged packaging, pests, or unexpected slime. Rehydration water can contain debris and is not automatically suitable as stock.

## Cooking and cross-contamination

Cook thoroughly using a method appropriate to the dish and product. Keep mushrooms separate from raw meat utensils and wash hands and work surfaces. FDA advises cleaning produce with running water rather than soap and refrigerating perishable mushrooms at 40°F or below.

Texture remains firm even when cooked; firmness alone should not be mistaken for rawness or spoilage. Use time, temperature, supplier instructions, and overall condition together.

## Nutrition and health-claim limits

Nutrient composition depends on species, strain, substrate, water content, fresh versus dried form, serving size, and preparation. A value for “wood ear, dried” cannot automatically be assigned to fresh *A. cornea*.

Traditional-use or laboratory claims do not establish prevention or treatment of disease. This page makes no blood-pressure, cancer, cardiovascular, anticoagulant, or immune claim. Health decisions require product-specific evidence and qualified clinical advice.

## Cultivation context

Commercial cultivation uses authenticated cultures and prepared hardwood logs or sawdust-based substrates. Clean inoculation, substrate formula, temperature, humidity, fresh air, light, and harvest timing are controlled by strain and production system.

The species is more approachable than a mycorrhizal mushroom but is not a generic beginner block. Importing oyster-mushroom parameters without a species-specific method can produce poor form or contamination. Wild tissue also carries identity and contaminant uncertainty.

## Responsible observation

Take only enough material for a legitimate record where collection is legal. Jelly fungi support decomposer communities and invertebrates. Photograph repeated wet-dry cycles rather than stripping a branch.

Avoid damaging living trees to obtain a close-up. A telephoto or careful angle often documents the attachment without cutting bark.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is cloud ear always pale?

No. Hydration, maturity, strain, light, and processing change color. Paleness is only one supporting tendency.

### Does the hairy surface prove A. cornea?

No. Hair structure is useful, but related *Auricularia* also require geography, spores, microscopy, and sometimes DNA.

### Can a wood ear package contain mixed species?

Market naming and supply chains vary. Use producer documentation rather than assuming one species from a common name.

### Can cloud ear grow on a home fruiting block?

Commercial methods exist, but authenticated culture and species-specific substrate and climate control are required.

### Is rehydration water safe to drink?

Do not assume so. It may contain debris or contaminants; follow the product's preparation instructions.

## Separate outer hairs from the fertile inner surface

An ear-shaped lobe has two non-equivalent faces. The outer, sterile face of *Auricularia cornea* can be finely hairy or velvety, while the inward-facing fertile surface is smoother to wrinkled. Photograph both at the same magnification and include the rolled margin connecting them. Backlighting may show translucency but can hide hairs, so add an oblique-light frame that reveals surface relief.

Hair length and density can matter in species work, yet they are difficult to judge from a phone photograph and may require microscopy. Water, abrasion, maturity, and drying alter their appearance. Spore dimensions, basidia, internal hyphae, geography, and molecular evidence may be needed when cultivated or traded material lacks reliable origin. The field name should remain at genus level when the evidence does not support *A. cornea*.

Commercial traceability begins before rehydration. Retain the package, lot, country of origin, scientific name if supplied, ingredient list, best-by date, and seller. Photograph a representative dry piece, weigh the portion, note soak time and water temperature, and photograph the expanded material. These records describe product behavior and handling; they do not independently authenticate the species or support medical claims.
