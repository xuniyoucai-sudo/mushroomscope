## Start with the wood, not the cap color

The Common bonnet, *Mycena galericulata*, is a small wood-decomposing mushroom whose ordinary gray-brown palette hides a useful ecological story. Its caps are often bell-shaped when young, later becoming broadly conical or convex, and damp margins can be lined by the gills showing through the thin cap. Those traits make a sensible starting hypothesis. They do not make a secure identification. Many bonnet mushrooms occupy the same color range, change markedly as they dry, and fruit from wood that may be buried beneath leaf litter.

A good record begins before a fruit body is removed. Photograph the entire group and its attachment. Show whether stems share a crevice in one stump, emerge from separate points on a log, or appear terrestrial above buried roots. Include a scale, then photograph one intact cap, the gills, the full stem and its base. The ecological evidence should remain connected to the specimen evidence; a loose mushroom placed on a nearby log proves nothing about its substrate.

The current GBIF record accepts *Mycena galericulata* in Mycenaceae. Specialist Mycena resources also emphasize that species separation can depend on microscopic structures. This page therefore explains a field documentation process, not a shortcut around a regional key or expert examination.

## Read a developmental series instead of one mushroom

Young caps are commonly tall and bell-like, with a surface ranging from gray-brown to brown. As they expand, the profile becomes broader and may retain a low central umbo. Water content matters: a wet cap can appear darker and more translucent, while a drying cap becomes paler and may lose obvious marginal lines. Record color in open shade with a neutral reference rather than under warm artificial light.

Choose three fruit bodies at different stages and measure cap width, cap height and stem length. Note how far the radial striations extend from the margin toward the center. A pattern repeated across several fresh caps is more useful than an isolated weathered specimen. Do not treat surface gloss after rain as a permanent character, and do not confuse adhered wood fibers or soil with cap ornament.

The gills begin pale and are attached to the stem; some descriptions use an attached profile with a short descending tooth. Mature gills can acquire a pinkish or flesh-toned cast. That change is easy to overstate in photographs because brown leaf litter and warm white balance reflect into pale tissue. Compare a young and mature cap in the same light, and obtain a spore deposit if the collection is being studied. A white deposit supports placement among several pale-spored possibilities but does not name the species.

## Document the stem and attachment precisely

The stem is proportionally long, slender and tougher than the thin cap. Its color may run from pale gray to brownish, often darkening toward the base. Record whether it is hollow, whether the surface is smooth or fibrous, and whether fine basal hairs or attached wood particles are present. Avoid trimming the base: the point of attachment can distinguish a mushroom truly growing from exposed wood from one merely leaning against it.

For a stump cluster, sketch a simple plan. Mark each stem base and the visible grain or bark. For apparently terrestrial fruit bodies, gently trace only enough litter to establish whether a buried branch lies below; do not excavate living roots unnecessarily. This small map is more reproducible than a general note such as “in woods.” It also prevents a common error in which nearby standing trees are named as the substrate even though the fungus is decomposing an unrelated fallen branch.

Odor is sometimes recorded in bonnet identification, but it is subjective and must never be tasted as a substitute for analysis. If an odor is documented, use a fresh, uncrushed specimen, state the observer and conditions, and use comparisons such as farinaceous only as notes for a key. Absence of a perceived odor is weak evidence.

## Ecology: a decomposer operating inside coarse wood

*Mycena galericulata* is associated with decaying wood, particularly broadleaf stumps, logs and buried woody material. The visible mushrooms are short-lived reproductive structures. Most of the organism persists as mycelium within the substrate, contributing to the breakdown and recycling of woody material. A flush therefore records a moisture and temperature opportunity at one moment, not the full extent of the colony.

Host identification should be evidence-based. Bark pattern, cut end grain, attached leaves and a known standing trunk can support a host note. A leaf resting on an old stump cannot. Record the decay stage as well: intact hard wood, softened sapwood, loose fibers and heavily decomposed material represent different microhabitats. A sequence of observations after rainfall can show whether fruiting recurs from the same cracks and whether cap expansion tracks wood moisture.

Range labels require similar restraint. Database points can include old names, uncertain coordinates and records made under different species concepts. Use a local checklist or herbarium resource to determine whether the name is established in the region. A photograph from another continent should not be forced into a familiar European name without taxonomic support.

## Separate the closest gray-brown bonnet candidates

The practical lookalike set is not every small gray mushroom. Begin with species sharing three conditions: bonnet-like form, pale spores and a wood substrate. *Mycena inclinata* can form conspicuous tufts on stumps and may have contrasting stem colors. *Mycena maculata* can develop reddish-brown spots or stains. *Mycena polygramma* is associated with a strongly lined or fibrous stem. None of these capsule summaries is a verdict; age, rain and regional variation can obscure the named feature.

Build a comparison table for the actual collection. Use rows for cluster density, precise substrate, cap profile at three ages, wet and dry color, gill attachment, gill color change, stem surface, base, odor, spore deposit and measured microscopic features. Leave cells unknown rather than filling them from expectation. This makes the missing evidence visible and reduces the temptation to choose the closest photograph.

Other small wood-inhabiting genera may also enter the comparison. A brown spore deposit, different gill attachment or a ring zone can redirect the key away from *Mycena*. The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) explains how to photograph attachment without tearing it, while the [stem-features guide](/identification/mushroom-stem-features/) provides a record format for the complete base.

## When microscopy changes the answer

Microscopy is not an ornamental final step for difficult bonnets. It can test whether field observations fit the species concept. A documented examination records spore dimensions from multiple mature spores, shape, amyloid reaction when the key requires it, basidia, cheilocystidia, pleurocystidia and cap-surface structures. Measurements should include mounting medium, magnification, calibration and sample count.

Use a current regional key because the character combination matters more than any one measurement. A mean without the observed range hides variation, and a single unusually large spore can mislead. Link microscope slides and photographs to the same collection number used for the habitat and fruit-body series. If the microscopic result conflicts with the field name, preserve the conflict and revise the identification rather than discarding the inconvenient observation.

For observations intended for a public database or herbarium, dry representative material promptly and retain field notes. A voucher allows later workers to reassess the name when genus concepts change. Without preserved material, a confident species label may be impossible to audit.

## Safety and food boundary

Common bonnet is not presented here as a food mushroom. Small *Mycena* provide little culinary value, species-level identification can be difficult, and chemistry is not sufficiently predictable to turn an image match into a safety judgment. Never use cap color, wood growth or a white spore print to authorize eating a collection.

If an unknown mushroom has been swallowed and symptoms occur, contact the relevant poison center or emergency medical service promptly. Preserve uncooked material, cooked leftovers, photographs and timing information. Do not wait for an internet identification, induce vomiting unless directed, or assume that cooking neutralized an unknown toxin. This necessary emergency boundary is intentionally repeated across safety-oriented field pages.

## A reproducible field record

Use a specimen code and record date, coordinates at an appropriate privacy precision, habitat, substrate evidence, decay stage, weather and group pattern. Photograph the undisturbed cluster, cap profiles, gills, full stems and attachment. Measure at least three mature and immature specimens. If collecting is legal and justified, obtain a spore deposit and retain a voucher for microscopy.

The [habitat-identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps distinguish direct substrate evidence from nearby vegetation. For another wood-associated pale-gilled mushroom with a different body plan, compare the [Deer mushroom guide](/mushrooms/deer-mushroom-guide/). The useful outcome is not always a species name. “Gray-brown *Mycena* on decayed broadleaf wood, microscopy pending” can be a more accurate and valuable record than a forced label.

Before leaving the site, check that the photograph set shows one unquestioned point of wood attachment, one undamaged gill-to-stem junction, and at least two cap ages. Record what remains unknown. That final uncertainty list prevents a later observer from treating an omitted character as if it had been checked and found absent.
