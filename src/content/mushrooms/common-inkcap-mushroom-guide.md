**Common inkcap**, *Coprinopsis atramentaria*, is best documented as a timed transformation. A young cluster may consist of smooth gray-brown eggs. Hours later the caps open into grooved bells, their crowded gills darken, and the margins begin to dissolve into black fluid. That dramatic change is useful evidence, but it is not unique enough to name a specimen by itself. A defensible record connects every stage to the same patch, complete bases, buried wood, a dark deposit, microscopy, and a regional key.

This page owns the species-level common-inkcap intent. The [glistening inkcap guide](/mushrooms/glistening-inkcap-mushroom-guide/) addresses smaller honey-brown clusters that may retain mica-like veil granules. The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) explains how to record attachment before the tissues collapse. Neither comparison provides food clearance.

## Map the cluster before lifting a mushroom

Photograph the whole colony with a scale and fixed landmark. Count unopened eggs, expanding bells, mature caps, and collapsed remains separately. Record whether stems share one buried wood unit or merely crowd together in wood-rich soil. A lawn or path-edge cluster can originate from roots hidden several centimeters below the surface, so “growing from soil” is an observation, not proof that wood is absent.

Excavate one complete base with surrounding material if collection is lawful. Separate soil gently and look for attached wood, root bark, or dense mycelial cords. Photograph the layers before cleaning. Do not assign the nearest living tree as a host: the fungus is decomposing wood, and the buried material may come from an older stump or construction debris.

## Build a cap-development clock

Choose several numbered fruit bodies and photograph them from the same angle at recorded intervals. Young caps are typically ovoid, smooth to finely fibrillose, and gray-brown with a darker center. Expansion produces a bell-shaped profile and increasingly obvious radial grooves. Weather changes the pace, so report elapsed time, temperature, shade, and rainfall rather than claiming a universal schedule.

Measure cap height and width before each image. A cap that looks deeply pleated in side light may appear smooth under flat illumination; preserve diffuse-light frames and raw color. The margin should remain tied to the specimen number because blackening, curling, splitting, and tissue loss proceed unevenly across a cluster.

## Follow one gill from pale to black

Turn an intact cap while the margin still permits an underside view. The crowded gills begin pale, pass through gray or brownish gray, and become black as spores mature. Record the point of attachment to the stem and the order in which color changes. A detached black fragment without its earlier stage cannot demonstrate this sequence.

Deliquescence is enzymatic self-digestion rather than simple rainfall damage. It usually advances from the cap edge inward, freeing mature spores while destroying older tissue. Rain can dilute the fluid and insects can remove sections, so absence of a perfect “inky” drip does not exclude an inkcap. Conversely, a black liquid alone does not identify *C. atramentaria*.

## Preserve the stem and any basal collar

Common-inkcap stems are pale, hollow, and comparatively fragile. Measure length and width at the apex, middle, and base. Some collections show a subtle basal zone or ridge, but it is not a dependable ring equivalent. Soil, torn veil tissue, and compressed neighboring stems can imitate one. Section a documented stem to record its cavity and wall thickness.

Handle only the specimens needed for a record. Mature caps can disappear during transport, leaving stems that are difficult to interpret. A ventilated rigid container and separate specimen numbers preserve more evidence than a mixed plastic bag. Dry representative young and mature material promptly if a qualified herbarium or local group can accept it.

## Obtain a dark deposit before the cap disappears

Place a mature but not fully liquefied cap over white and clear nonabsorbent surfaces. Protect it from drafts, note the duration, and keep the cap code with the deposit. The expected mass color is black to very dark brown. The [spore-color guide](/identification/mushroom-spore-colors/) describes why reflected light, paper fibers, and a thin deposit can mislead.

Under calibrated microscopy, record spore length, width, profile, germ pore, sample size, and mount medium. Cap cuticle, veil remnants, basidia, and cystidia may also be required by the key. Report the measured range and outliers; one convenient spore cannot represent a collection. DNA records are useful only when the sequence remains tied to a well-documented voucher.

## Separate common inkcap from similar clusters

Glistening inkcap often forms dense clusters on buried wood and overlaps strongly in habitat. Fresh young caps may bear sparkling veil particles, but rain can remove them. Compare stature, cap color and texture, developmental series, veil anatomy, spores, and microscopic structures rather than relying on “mica” alone.

Hare's-foot inkcaps are usually more delicate and begin with conspicuous white woolly veil material. Shaggy mane has a tall cylindrical cap covered in coarse upturned scales and commonly fruits in lawns or disturbed ground. Other *Coprinopsis*, *Coprinellus*, *Parasola*, and dark-spored fragile mushrooms can converge on a gray bell silhouette. Local keys and microscopy are essential where the distinction matters.

## Treat the alcohol interaction as a real safety boundary

Common inkcap is associated with coprine, which can interfere with alcohol metabolism and produce flushing, pounding headache, nausea, vomiting, palpitations, weakness, and low blood pressure. Timing and severity vary. A folklore waiting interval is not a safe universal rule because identification, amount, individual physiology, medicines, and alcohol exposure are uncertain.

This guide does not recommend eating the mushroom with or without alcohol. It also does not diagnose a reaction. If exposure is suspected and symptoms occur, contact local poison control or medical services, preserve remnants and photographs, and record the mushroom, meal, alcohol, medicine, symptom, and time sequence. Do not wait for social-media identification before seeking care.

## Record urban and garden context without assuming cleanliness

Clusters often appear around old stumps, paths, landscaped beds, and buried roots. Record mowing, irrigation, soil replacement, pet access, pesticides, road splash, and treated timber where relevant. These observations help explain fruiting and contamination context; they do not establish chemical safety. A correctly named mushroom can still be spoiled, contaminated, or unsuitable for an individual.

Revisit the patch after rain and during a dry interval. Mark whether fruiting recurs over the same buried line, migrates along a root, or appears from separate wood fragments. Repeated mapping provides more ecological information than photographing the most dramatic cap once.

## Build a reviewable common-inkcap record

Before closing the record, reconcile the cluster map with the specimen ledger. Every photograph should retain date, local time, scale, weather, and specimen number. Note caps that vanished between visits and distinguish deliquescence from mowing, trampling, slug feeding, or collection. If multiple clusters occur along one buried root, measure the gaps rather than describing them as one colony without evidence.

Archive original images as well as edited copies. Automated phone enhancement can exaggerate radial grooves, remove shadow detail from black gills, and turn gray-brown caps into warm tan. A color reference and unchanged raw frame let a reviewer separate the mushroom from the camera pipeline. Record uncertainty explicitly when the substrate cannot be exposed, microscopy is incomplete, or a young cap was lost before maturity. A useful field record can remain at genus level; forcing a species name does not improve weak evidence.

Before submitting the observation, check that captions distinguish directly observed characters from literature expectations. State who performed microscopy, which regional key was used, and whether the voucher remains available. Those provenance fields let a later reviewer correct the name without discarding the underlying ecological record.
