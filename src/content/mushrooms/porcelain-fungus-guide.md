## Start with the beech wood, not the white cap

Porcelain fungus is memorable because rain makes its ivory cap gleam like wet ceramic. That visual shortcut is useful for noticing a cluster but unsafe for naming one. A defensible record combines a translucent, mucilaginous cap; white gills; a slender white stem with a delicate ring; a white spore deposit; and direct attachment to dead beech wood. In its best-documented European range, the host relationship is one of the highest-value clues.

Photograph the whole branch or trunk before making close-ups. Show whether it is standing, fallen, or still attached to a living beech. Record the height, orientation, bark, decay stage, and whether fruit bodies occur above, below, or around the wood. Then photograph caps, gills, rings, stems, and attachments across several ages. ## How moisture changes the cap

Fresh wet caps are convex to nearly flat, white to ivory, and coated in clear mucilage. The center may develop a pale tan or ochre wash while the margin stays translucent enough for the gill pattern to show through. Droplets and bright highlights can hide surface detail, so expose photographs for the white cap rather than the dark forest. Include one oblique view that shows cap thickness and margin translucency.

After dry weather, the same cap may look much less glossy. Dirt can adhere to residual slime, and old caps can yellow, collapse, split, or become translucent throughout. Record recent rain, apparent wetness, cap diameter, central color, margin striation, and whether the surface feels tacky using a glove or tool when handling is appropriate. Do not taste slime or use texture as a single-character verdict.

## Gills, rings, and curved stems

The gills are white and relatively broad. Photograph the underside without tearing the cap from its attachment if possible. The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) can help record spacing, attachment, forking, and edge color without reducing the observation to “white gills.” A white deposit is expected, but it overlaps with many wood-growing agarics.

Young stems carry a delicate membranous ring, which may collapse, adhere to the stem, or disappear with age and weather. Photograph several individuals because relying on one old ringless specimen creates a false exclusion. The [veils and rings guide](/identification/mushroom-veils-rings-guide/) explains how partial veils change from closed button to mature cap.

Stems can curve dramatically. When fruit bodies emerge from the underside of a fallen branch, growth responds to gravity: stems bend so the caps reorient and the gills face downward. Record that geometry with the log visible. A curved stem is ecological evidence about development, not a deformity and not a species character by itself.

## Confirm the host instead of assuming it

Porcelain fungus is closely associated with beech in Britain, Ireland, and much of northern Europe. It fruits from dead trunks, fallen branches, and dead portions of living trees. Smooth gray bark can suggest beech, but bark on old fallen wood may be missing or altered. Photograph attached leaves, buds, mast, branching pattern, and nearby living trees. A host recorded as “hardwood” is weaker evidence than a beech identification supported by multiple features.

If the cluster is on a different tree, pause. The organism may be a regional relative, the wood may have been misidentified, or the fungal name may be wrong. Use the [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) to document substrate uncertainty explicitly. Do not retrofit the host to match the expected mushroom.

## Developmental series beats the perfect portrait

A single glossy cap cannot show how the fungus changes. Build a series from small hemispherical buttons through expanded caps to collapsed old fruit bodies. For each stage, record cap width, translucency, central pigmentation, slime condition, ring visibility, gill spacing, and stem curvature. Photograph clusters again after one or two days when feasible; a wet-to-dry sequence can explain why two images of the same colony appear unrelated.

This series also helps separate damage from diagnostic structure. Slugs may remove cap margins, flies may use aging fruit bodies, wind may dislodge mushrooms from high branches, and rain can wash or redistribute mucilage. Label damage rather than turning it into a trait.

## Compare pale wood-growing mushrooms by evidence columns

White color occurs in many genera. Pale *Armillaria* can form clusters on wood and may have rings and white spores, but caps, gill attachment, stem texture, veil structure, and growth habit differ. Rooting-shank relatives may have a long pseudorhiza extending into soil or buried wood. Regional *Mucidula* relatives can require microscopic characters or sequence data.

Create a comparison record with columns for host, exact attachment, cap slime, cap translucency, gill spacing and attachment, ring structure, stem base, spore color, odor, season, and region. Exclude no dangerous or unknown taxon merely because it lacks one expected character in a poor photograph. A local key sets the relevant comparison set more reliably than a global image search.

## Taxonomy: Mucidula and Oudemansiella

The name *Oudemansiella mucida* remains widespread in field guides, conservation pages, image archives, and older observations. Many current taxonomic backbones accept *Mucidula mucida*. The change reflects genus-level classification, not a new common mushroom suddenly appearing on beech. A useful database record preserves the name originally applied, the current accepted name used by the selected backbone, and the date of that taxonomic check.

Search both combinations when reviewing literature. Do not merge every white *Oudemansiella* image into this species, because the older genus concept included fungi with different anatomy and ecology. When a precise record matters, retain a voucher under appropriate permits and consult a regional mycologist or herbarium.

## Wood-decay ecology and the strobilurin story

Porcelain fungus is a decomposer of dead beech tissue. Its fruit bodies mark reproduction from an established mycelium in wood; they do not show the complete extent of colonization. On a living tree, distinguish a dead limb from living sapwood and avoid claiming that visible mushrooms alone caused an entire tree's decline.

Compounds produced by this fungus helped inspire the strobilurin class of agricultural fungicides. That history is biochemical and agricultural context, not evidence that a wild fruit body treats disease or is appropriate for self-medication. A compound's ecological role, a purified derivative's commercial use, and eating a mushroom are three different evidence questions.

## Food claims require a separate decision chain

Some regional sources describe porcelain fungus as edible after removing slime and cooking. That statement does not verify a collection. First establish identity with local evidence. Then assess freshness, pollution, lawful collection, allergen exposure, cross-contamination, and preparation. Material from roadside trees, treated timber, contaminated sites, or advanced decay should not be reframed as food simply because a field guide uses an edible label.

This page cannot clear a wild specimen for consumption. Do not taste an unidentified mushroom, and do not assume cooking neutralizes a lookalike. For symptoms after a mushroom exposure, contact emergency services or a poison center and retain uncooked material, trimmings, photographs, packaging, and timing details.

## Field record checklist

1. Photograph the complete beech trunk or branch and its living/dead status.
2. Record fruiting height, wood orientation, bark, decay stage, and weather.
3. Measure caps across buttons, expanded specimens, and old material.
4. Photograph cap translucency and mucilage without overexposing highlights.
5. Show gill spacing, gill attachment, ring remnants, stem base, and wood connection.
6. Make a spore deposit only as one supporting character.
7. Compare the full record with a current regional key and both scientific-name combinations.
8. Preserve uncertainty when host, ring, or attachment cannot be established.

The checklist is deliberately host-heavy. A striking close-up may be aesthetically strong while being scientifically weak if the beech connection and attachment are missing.

## Responsible observation

Many clusters occur above head height or on rain-slick fallen timber. Do not climb unstable trees or pull down branches for a photograph. Leave enough fruit bodies for spore release and invertebrates, follow site rules, and avoid damaging living bark. Location sharing should respect sensitive woodland and landowner permissions.

Porcelain fungus is best understood as a beech-wood decomposer with a moisture-responsive fruit body. The combination of host, translucent slimy cap, white gills, ring, curved growth, and season is far more informative than whiteness alone.

## Archive a record that can survive a name change

Keep original images, observation date, region, host evidence, cap and stem measurements, weather, and the key or specialist used for comparison. Record both *Mucidula mucida* and the widely encountered combination *Oudemansiella mucida* in searchable notes, but do not silently replace the name supplied by a herbarium or verifier. A later taxonomic revision can reinterpret a well-documented observation; it cannot recover a missing underside or an assumed host.

If material is lawfully collected for formal study, link the voucher number and repository to the photographs rather than treating a loose dried cap as self-explanatory. Drying removes much of the gloss and translucency that attracted attention in the field. Notes made before drying—especially cap mucilage, ring condition, growth orientation, and beech attachment—therefore remain essential evidence.
