The **bleeding fairy helmet**, *Mycena haematopus*, is a small hardwood bonnet with an unusually visible tissue reaction. Freshly broken stems or caps can release dark wine-red latex. That reaction is memorable, but it is not a complete identification: specimen freshness changes the flow, other small bonnets can release colored fluid, and habitat or anatomy may contradict the first impression.

Build the record around the intact cluster before testing one proportionate specimen. Preserve cap stages, gill attachment, stem surface, exact wood, latex timing, spores and microscopy as linked observations. This page does not recommend the mushroom as food.

## Photograph the cluster before testing latex

Begin with the entire group on its log or stump. Include every visible base, the occupied length of wood and a scale. Photograph a low side view, a top view and a habitat view. Clustering matters, but count separate stems rather than treating fused bases or overlapping fruit bodies as one organism.

Label several maturity stages. Young caps are conical to bell-shaped; older caps expand and may flatten while retaining a central bump. Record cap diameter, height and margin condition. Wet caps can appear darker and more translucent, while dry caps fade and may conceal the fine radial lines.

## Map cap color under controlled light

Use diffuse daylight or a neutral gray reference. Record the darker disc, reddish-brown to burgundy middle zone and translucent-striate margin separately. Avoid increasing red saturation until the latex and cap become the same artificial color. Save the raw frame so another reviewer can inspect exposure and white balance.

Compare young, mature and weathered caps from one cluster. Color is a developmental series, not a fixed paint chip. Bruises, spore deposits, water films and reflected green light can alter the apparent surface.

## Record gill attachment and edge condition

Turn one cap without detaching it when possible. The gills are pale grayish, whitish or faintly pink and meet the stem. Photograph the junction sharply; a torn underside can imitate free gills. Count full-length and shorter gills over a defined arc and note cross-connections only when actually visible.

Inspect damaged edges for reddish staining, but do not create extensive injury across the colony. The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) provides attachment terms. Use the observation that fits the specimen rather than selecting a term from the expected species description.

## Test one fresh stem with a timed protocol

Choose one mature, hydrated specimen only after the intact record is complete. Make a clean transverse break near the lower stem and start a timer. Photograph immediately, then at fixed intervals under unchanged light. Record whether fluid beads, smears, darkens, soaks into tissue or fails to appear.

The [mushroom latex field guide](/identification/mushroom-latex-field-guide/) separates initial latex color from later staining. For *M. haematopus*, fresh fluid is described as dark reddish or wine-colored. Do not call a brown wound, crushed tissue or reflected light “blood.” Use non-graphic language and clean tools between collections.

## Interpret a negative latex result cautiously

Old, dry, frost-affected or insect-damaged fruit bodies may yield little visible fluid. A tiny stem also contains less tissue than a large milkcap. Record specimen condition, recent rain and time from collection to test before using absence as evidence.

If one fresh member is negative, do not keep breaking the cluster. Preserve another specimen for expert review and consider a genus-level identification. A clear positive reaction is useful only when cap, gills, stem, wood and region are compatible.

## Preserve the complete stem surface and base

Photograph each side of the stem. Note reddish-brown color, any pale bloom near the apex, and fine hairs or basal mycelium. Soil stuck to a wet stem is not ornamentation. The stem should be traced into the wood rather than cut at the surface.

There is no ring or volva. Absence should be documented across intact specimens, not inferred from a broken base. An unexpected membrane, bulb or cup is a reason to stop and reconsider the collection.

## Confirm hardwood rather than saying “on a log”

Record bark, exposed wood, decay class, diameter, contact with soil and visible tree evidence. The fungus is associated with decaying hardwood, but a conifer log can be difficult to distinguish after bark loss. Nearby living trees do not automatically identify fallen wood.

Use the [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) to separate direct attachment from surrounding litter. If fruit bodies emerge beside a log, expose only enough of one base to determine whether it joins the log, a buried branch or soil.

## Separate the bleeding bonnet from Mycena sanguinolenta

*Mycena sanguinolenta* can also release reddish latex. It is commonly smaller and associated with moss, soil or litter rather than a dense cluster attached to substantial hardwood. Those tendencies are not absolute, so substrate documentation and microscopy matter.

Create a matrix with cap size, wood attachment, cluster form, stem color, latex amount and color, spore measurements, cheilocystidia and pileipellis. Do not let the dramatic reaction erase contradictory ecology.

## Compare clustered and common bonnets

The [clustered bonnet guide](/mushrooms/clustered-bonnet-mushroom-guide/) covers another hardwood species that can form dense tufts. It tends toward gray-brown caps, two-toned stems and a different odor rather than wine-red latex. The [common bonnet guide](/mushrooms/common-bonnet-mushroom-guide/) shows a larger gray-brown wood bonnet, sometimes with cross-veined mature gills.

Compare numbered specimens at equivalent maturity. Cap color alone is unreliable after rain, and old clustered bonnets can acquire reddish or brown tones. Latex, stem anatomy, odor, gill details, substrate and microscopy should agree.

## Make a specimen-linked microscopy record

Obtain a white deposit from mature labelled caps over dark glass, protected from drafts. A faint deposit may require several caps from one verified cluster. Do not pool unrelated mushrooms growing on the same log.

Microscopy may examine spore length, width and shape, basidia, cheilocystidia, pleurocystidia, pileipellis and caulocystidia according to the regional key. State reagent, calibration, number measured and full range. Photograph representative structures without selecting only values that match an expected description.

## Treat distribution maps as questions

GBIF records provide geographic and voucher leads, while NCBI supplies taxonomic and sequence-linked context. Check the basis of record, identifier, date, coordinate precision and current name. A map does not confirm that a fresh photograph belongs to the same species concept.

First Nature provides a European account of the cap, latex and hardwood habitat. In another region, use local keys and expert networks because the available *Mycena* set differs. Report *Mycena* species when the specimen packet cannot support a narrower name.

## Keep safety and conservation boundaries explicit

This page does not recommend bleeding fairy helmet for food. Tiny bonnets are difficult to identify, mixed collections are easy to create, and little culinary value could justify that uncertainty. Latex color is not a safety test.

If illness follows wild mushroom ingestion, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Keep uncooked material, leftovers, photographs, location and timing. Do not delay care while seeking an online name.

Take only the material needed for a proportionate voucher where collection is permitted. Photograph the rest in place, and avoid stripping a whole log merely to repeat a latex test.

## Build a reviewable bleeding-fairy-helmet packet

1. Photograph the entire hardwood cluster before handling it.
2. Measure and label several cap stages.
3. Record cap zones and gill attachment under neutral light.
4. Trace complete stems to the wood.
5. Test one fresh specimen and time the latex response.
6. Record a negative result with hydration and age limits.
7. Compare *M. sanguinolenta*, clustered bonnet and common bonnet.
8. Add spores and microscopy when species-level confidence matters.

A good packet makes the wine-red latex one linked character rather than the whole diagnosis. The record remains reusable even when a reviewer decides the species name is too narrow.

For a return survey, map a fixed length of log and count fresh, mature, damaged and collapsed fruit bodies in separate columns. Record bark loss, contact with soil, recent rainfall and the photographed face of the wood. A later flush near the same mark demonstrates repeated fruiting at that substrate unit, not the age, size or genetic identity of the hidden mycelium. This restraint keeps an ecological note from becoming an unsupported population claim.
