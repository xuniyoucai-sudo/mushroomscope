Split gill fungus looks like a small gray fan until the underside is viewed closely. Its radiating structures are not ordinary single gill blades. Their edges are divided and can roll apart as moisture changes, helping protect the fertile surface during dry periods and reopen it when conditions improve.

That resilience contributes to the species’ broad occurrence on dead wood. It also means an old dry fruit body can look very different from a hydrated one. A reliable record includes both surfaces, wet-dry condition, substrate, scale, region, and microscopic evidence.

## Establish scale and growth pattern

Photograph the colony before selecting one fan. Caps are usually small and grow in rows, overlapping shelves, or scattered patches from wood. Record the size range and whether attachment follows a crack, cut face, or dead branch segment.

Macro framing can make a two-centimeter cap resemble an oyster mushroom. Include a scale and a wider branch view so the true proportions remain visible.

## Examine the hairy upper surface

The upper face is densely covered with pale hairs and often divided into subtle zones. Gray, white, tan, or pinkish tones change with moisture, dirt, algae, and age. The margin may be scalloped and curl when dry.

Use side light to reveal hairs. A wet flash photograph can make them disappear and falsely suggest a smooth fleshy cap.

## Turn the fan to see the split lamellae

The underside carries radiating folds that resemble gills. Under magnification, each structure has paired fertile edges with a groove between them. During drying, the edges roll and close; after moisture returns they spread again.

Photograph across several hydration states without tearing the fruit body. The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) explains conventional attachments, while split gill demonstrates why not every gill-like structure should be forced into that vocabulary.

## Record rehydration as a reversible movement

A dry fan becomes rigid and tightly curled. Rain or controlled humid conditions can restore flexibility. Measure dimensions and photograph the same marked fruit body rather than substituting different specimens.

Reopening is not proof that old tissue is healthy indefinitely. Weathering, microbes, insects, and ultraviolet exposure eventually damage the reproductive surface.

## Confirm direct wood attachment

Trace the lateral base to dead wood. Record living versus dead host tissue, hardwood versus conifer when defensible, bark, diameter, decay, fire or storm damage, and whether the material is cut lumber or natural debris.

“On a tree” is insufficient. A dead branch attached to a living tree is a different substrate condition from colonization of living sapwood, and neither alone establishes structural risk.

## Separate species identity from wood-decay diagnosis

The fungus decomposes lignocellulosic material, but visible fans do not measure the internal extent or mechanical significance of decay. On a landscape tree or building component, document moisture, defects, loading, targets, and construction context, then consult the appropriate arborist or building professional.

Removing fruit bodies does not remove established mycelium. Moisture correction and material assessment matter more than cosmetic scraping.

## Compare oyster mushrooms structurally

Oyster mushrooms are generally larger, thicker, and more succulent. Their gills are ordinary blades that often run down a short lateral stem or attachment. Split gill fans are thin, leathery, hairy, and small, with paired curling lamellae.

The [oyster lookalikes guide](/identification/oyster-mushroom-lookalikes/) provides a broader safety screen. Never treat the choice as “oyster or split gill”; many lateral wood fungi exist.

## Compare other small fan fungi

*Panellus*, *Crepidotus*, *Plicaturopsis*, and additional genera can form small lateral caps. Spore color may differ, while underside architecture, cap surface, substrate, and microscopy narrow the options.

A white print does not resolve all pale-spored fans. Record spores, basidia, hyphal clamps, and tissue where a regional key requires them.

## Use microscopy and culture provenance

Microscopy can evaluate spores and the characteristic construction of the fertile folds. If tissue is cultured, attach the isolate to a voucher and collection number. White mycelium from wood is not automatically *S. commune*.

The species has an unusually complex mating system studied in genetics, but interesting biology does not make an unidentified plate safe to handle casually. Contain cultures and avoid aerosolizing spores.

## Treat the global label carefully

*S. commune* is reported widely, but broad occurrence does not eliminate population structure, historic misidentification, or uneven sampling. Record region and the taxonomic source used. A name from an older record should not be silently transferred to a new specimen without examination.

Where clinical or genomic papers use authenticated strains, evidence applies to those strains and questions—not automatically to every field fan.

## Record active sporulation conservatively

Fresh hydrated undersides may release abundant pale spores. Do not tap colonies near the face or create spore clouds for video. Photograph settled deposits only with suitable respiratory precautions and containment.

People with asthma, chronic lung disease, immune compromise, or persistent respiratory symptoms should avoid deliberate exposure and seek clinician advice rather than using this page for self-diagnosis.

## Separate environmental presence from infection

CDC guidance explains that fungal diseases depend on organism, exposure, route, and host factors. Medical literature has reported *Schizophyllum* in respiratory and other opportunistic contexts, but a woodland sighting does not mean an observer is infected.

Conversely, symptoms cannot be diagnosed by comparing them with a species page. Clinical evaluation, sampling, and laboratory identification belong to qualified healthcare professionals.

## Build a low-exposure field record

1. Photograph the colony and full substrate without disturbance.
2. Add a scale and show cap rows or patches.
3. Record upper hairs, margin, and hydration.
4. Photograph split lamellae with macro side light.
5. Document host condition and wood type independently.
6. Avoid tapping, smelling closely, or carrying open sporulating material indoors.
7. Preserve a sealed minimal voucher only when examination is planned.

The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) supports host documentation without overclaiming living-tree infection.

## Do not promote it as a casual food

Some cultural sources may discuss culinary use, but this editorial guide does not recommend it. Tough texture, inconsistent regional naming, environmental contamination, and medical exposure concerns make a generic “edible” label unhelpful.

Do not taste an unknown fan to separate it from oyster mushrooms. Cooking does not validate identity or remove every contaminant.

## Respond appropriately to ingestion or symptoms

If illness follows wild fungus consumption, contact local emergency services or a poison center and retain the material, photographs, timing, and preparation details. If persistent cough, wheeze, fever, or other concerning symptoms follow heavy fungal exposure, seek medical care.

NAMA’s syndrome overview supports poisoning communication, while respiratory concerns require clinical evaluation rather than a mushroom-edibility chart.

## Keep cultivation contained

The species can colonize wood and may fruit under varied conditions, which makes it an interesting research organism but a poor candidate for casual open-air indoor production. Spores and tough persistent fruit bodies can escape a poorly controlled setup.

Use authenticated strains, sealed labeled cultures, appropriate ventilation and disposal, and institutional biosafety guidance where applicable. Do not deliberately inoculate landscape trees or structural wood.

## Preserve useful records without spreading spores

Dry a small voucher in contained airflow and package it after it is fully dry. Clean work surfaces and avoid shaking dried material. Link photographs, substrate notes, microscope data, and identifiers to the same collection number.

Most colonies can be documented without removal. Repeat images through a wet-dry cycle may provide more value than collecting many fans.

## Distinguish persistence from repeated new growth

Mark individual fans in a photograph using fixed bark landmarks rather than ink or pins. After rain, compare the same margins, tears, and attachment points. A reopened old cap retains its damage pattern, while genuinely new fans begin as separate small pale structures. This distinction matters when estimating how long a colony fruits.

Record bark temperature and relative humidity only with sensor location and instrument limits. A reading taken in open air a meter away does not describe the narrow boundary layer beneath a cap. Environmental logs should explain the observation, not create false precision or a universal fruiting recipe.

## Frequently asked field questions

### Are split gills actually torn gills?

No. The paired structure is developmental and functional, not merely damage from drying.

### Can one colony remain visible for months?

Yes. Tough fans can persist and cycle between dry and hydrated states, though active fertility changes over time.

### Does finding it indoors prove dangerous mold exposure?

No. It does indicate a wood-and-moisture issue that merits source control and professional assessment; health risk depends on exposure and host factors.

### Is pink coloration diagnostic?

No. Moisture, age, light, and substrate influence color. Structure and microscopy are more reliable.

### Can gloves replace respiratory precautions?

No. Gloves reduce direct contact, while spores are an inhalation issue. Avoid generating airborne material and use appropriate controls.
