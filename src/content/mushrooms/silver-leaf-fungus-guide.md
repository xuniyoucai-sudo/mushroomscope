**Silver leaf fungus**, *Chondrostereum purpureum*, joins two search intents that must not be confused. One is a thin violet wood fungus with a smooth fertile surface. The other is silver leaf disease, a plant-health problem in which affected foliage can take on a silvery appearance. A purple bracket near a tree does not by itself diagnose that tree, and a silver leaf does not by itself identify the fungus.

This guide builds a traceable record across fruit-body anatomy, branch symptoms, wound history, internal wood staining, host evidence, season, microscopy, and diagnostic support. It does not authorize treatment, pruning timing, deliberate inoculation, or food use from an image.

## Establish whether the growth is crust, shelf, or both

Photograph the whole occupied wood unit before making a macro view. *C. purpureum* may begin flat against wood and lift at its edges into thin overlapping shelves. Record the percentage that is resupinate, the maximum projection of shelves, occupied length, thickness, and whether separate patches merge.

Include the exact attachment: pruning cut, broken limb, bark wound, dead branch, stump, or detached log. State whether wood is on a living plant or already dead. A fruit body on a stack of cut branches is evidence of decomposition there; it is not proof that the fungus caused the original branch decline.

## Read the smooth violet fertile surface

Turn the view toward the downward or exposed fertile face without tearing the bracket. Fresh surfaces may be violet, lilac, purple-brown, or purplish gray and should be smooth at hand-lens scale. There are no gills and no organized pore mouths. Use diffuse daylight and a neutral color reference because automatic white balance can turn muted lilac into intense magenta or erase it into gray.

The color fades with weathering and drying. Record both the most protected and most exposed portions. Do not identify a bleached old crust from color alone. Smooth texture, growth form, margin, upper surface, substrate, microscopy, and regional alternatives must remain consistent.

## Separate upper surface, margin, and hymenium

Where a crust lifts into a shelf, its upper face can become gray-brown, zoned, and finely hairy. The advancing edge is commonly pale and wavy. Photograph a profile that keeps all three regions visible: hairy top, thin margin, and smooth purplish underside.

This anatomy prevents a common error. Turkey tail and several other thin brackets can share gray zoning above, but turkey tail has pores below. The [pore-surface guide](/identification/mushroom-pore-surface-guide/) explains how to verify real openings rather than interpreting shadows, dirt, or torn tissue as pores.

## Compare hairy curtain crust without relying on one color

Hairy curtain crust, *Stereum hirsutum*, also forms thin hairy shelves on hardwood. It more often presents yellow to orange-brown zones and a yellowish smooth fertile surface. The [hairy curtain crust guide](/mushrooms/hairy-curtain-crust-fungus-guide/) gives its own texture, colony, and wood-decay workflow.

Color overlap increases in old, wet, frozen, or algae-stained material. Compare protected undersides, fresh margins, hair distribution, shelf thickness, host, spores, hyphal characters, and current regional keys. A purple stain on an otherwise orange shelf is not enough to transfer the name.

## Distinguish a smooth surface from violet pores or teeth

Violet-toothed polypore can show purple on the fertile surface, but it begins with pores that split into tooth-like projections. Inspect several ages under magnification. A genuinely smooth hymenium supports *Chondrostereum*; repeated pore mouths or teeth redirect the determination.

Smoky bracket has a gray to smoky pore surface rather than a smooth lilac one. The [smoky bracket guide](/mushrooms/smoky-bracket-fungus-guide/) shows how to record pore geometry and cap context. These links separate structure-based intents instead of treating every thin bracket as a color variant.

## Build a branch-to-leaf symptom map

For a living tree, number affected limbs on a sketch. Record which leaves look silver, whether the change is uniform or patchy, when it appeared, and whether adjacent shoots on the same scaffold remain green. Photograph both leaf surfaces in neutral light. Surface-feeding insects and reflective residues can create a superficial silver effect that follows a different pattern.

Trace each affected branch toward the trunk. Note pruning cuts, storm wounds, cankers, dead twigs, sap, and fruit bodies. Do not jump across the canopy: a bracket on one dead limb does not explain symptoms on an unconnected branch without supporting vascular or diagnostic evidence.

## Interpret the silver symptom as a hypothesis, not a verdict

Silvering associated with the disease results from changes within leaf tissues rather than a silver coating of fungal growth. Similar-looking foliage can arise from environmental stress, roots, insects, spray injury, and other diseases. A symptom photograph is therefore a triage record, not laboratory confirmation.

Use a qualified local plant diagnostic service when a valuable orchard or landscape tree is involved. Provide host identity, cultivar if known, location, onset, weather, irrigation, pruning history, symptom map, branch cross-sections where advised, and photographs of any fruit bodies. Local advice matters because climate, host susceptibility, and recommended wound-management timing differ.

## Record wood staining without overinterpreting it

When pruning or sampling is lawful and professionally appropriate, a cut through an affected branch may reveal brown internal staining. Photograph a freshly cleaned cross-section with scale and mark its orientation. Record how staining relates to the dead tip, wound, fruiting site, and apparently healthy wood.

Staining supports a disease investigation but is not species-specific. Other fungi, bacterial problems, compartmentalization, and old injuries alter wood color. Never create unnecessary wounds solely to make a photograph; seek arboricultural or plant-pathology advice on sampling a living tree.

## Link fruit-body evidence to microscopy and culture

Number a fruit body before collection. Record which side bore the fertile tissue, whether it was fresh, and the exact host unit. A white spore deposit may be attempted by positioning the smooth surface over clean glass with a blank control, but thin crusts do not always yield an obvious deposit.

Microscopy should follow a current key and report calibrated spore measurements, basidia, cystidia or other specified structures, hyphal system, mount medium, and counts. Culture or sequence evidence requires clean chain of custody and suitable references. A top sequence match from untraceable wood cannot diagnose a whole tree.

## Keep management claims local and evidence-based

Guidance from the Royal Horticultural Society, university extension, or national forest-health services can help frame prevention and diagnosis. Apply only recommendations appropriate to the host, region, season, and current regulations. Timing advice is not universal, and severe pruning or tree removal can create safety and plant-health consequences.

Do not deliberately spread or culture the fungus from a field collection. *C. purpureum* has been studied in specialized vegetation-management contexts, but that research does not make a home inoculation safe, legal, selective, or effective. Keep research findings separate from household tree care.

## Build a symptom timeline before attributing decline

Create one row per numbered branch and record bud break, first visible silvering, shoot growth, wilt, dieback, pruning, storms, heat, irrigation changes, and appearance of fruit bodies. Use dated overview photographs from the same side of the tree. Backfilling a timeline from memory should be labeled retrospective rather than mixed with observations made on the day.

The order matters. A bracket appearing on a branch that had been dead for years supports saprotrophic use of that wood more directly than it supports a claim about the original death. Silvering that follows a recent wound may justify diagnostic attention, but timing alone still does not identify the causal organism. Keep symptom onset, fungal fruiting, and management events as separate columns.

Compare an affected branch with a similar apparently healthy branch on the same plant where safe. Record leaf reflectance in the same light, shoot length, leaf size, fruit load, and visible wounds. This paired record reduces confusion from camera exposure and whole-tree drought stress, while recognizing that branches can differ in age, orientation, and vascular connection.

## Grade host and wound evidence explicitly

Use three evidence levels. “Confirmed host” requires a reliably identified plant and a fruit body directly attached to its wood. “Probable association” covers a fruit body on a fallen branch whose origin is traceable but not continuously observed. “Nearby only” describes a fungus on separate debris beneath the canopy. Do not convert the third level into a host record.

For wounds, record type, dimensions, height, orientation, estimated or documented date, callus development, exposed wood, and relationship to staining or fruiting. A pruning stub, storm tear, animal injury, graft, and natural dead branch create different histories. Photographs should show the route from wound to symptomatic branch, not just two disconnected close-ups.

This grading is particularly important in orchards, where removed wood, chipped prunings, neighboring cultivars, and old stumps may coexist. It allows a plant diagnostician to see which link is observed and which remains hypothetical. The grade should be revised when laboratory evidence or a traced attachment changes the record; it is not a permanent label.

## Keep food and human-health claims outside the diagnosis

The thin leathery fruit bodies are not recommended as food. Purple color, smooth texture, or lack of a known poisoning report does not establish edibility. Wood may also carry sprays, paint, preservatives, road contaminants, or animal waste.

If an unknown fungus has been eaten and symptoms occur, contact local poison-control or medical services promptly. Preserve uncooked material, photographs, site details, preparation, amount, and symptom times. A plant-disease discussion should never delay care.

## Build a reviewable silver leaf record

1. Map the full colony and exact wound or dead-wood attachment.
2. Photograph crust, lifted shelf, upper hairs, pale edge, and smooth underside.
3. Use a color reference and record fresh versus weathered areas.
4. Test lookalikes by pores, teeth, and surface texture rather than color alone.
5. Map leaf symptoms to individual branches and wound history.
6. Treat silvering and internal stain as evidence classes, not proof by themselves.
7. Link microscopy, culture, or sequence data to numbered material.
8. Obtain regional plant-diagnostic advice before consequential management.

The useful conclusion is not “purple fungus equals silver leaf disease.” It is a specimen-linked account in which the smooth violet hymenium, thin hairy shelves, broadleaf wound, branch symptoms, wood evidence, and qualified diagnostic findings support the same explanation.
