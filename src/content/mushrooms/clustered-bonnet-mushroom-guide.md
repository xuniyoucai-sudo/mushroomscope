The clustered bonnet is a wood-decaying *Mycena* whose strongest field clue is not simply a grey-brown cap. It is the architecture of a whole tuft: many fragile stems rising from one decayed hardwood unit, each carrying a striate bell-shaped cap above pale gills. That combination creates a useful hypothesis, but the genus contains many small, variable species and a photograph cannot finish the identification.

This guide is designed for documentation rather than food clearance. Record the fruiting group, the actual wood attachment, several developmental stages, a spore deposit, odor under controlled conditions, and microscopic characters when an exact name matters. If the evidence stops at “small brown mushroom on a stump,” record *Mycena* rather than forcing *M. inclinata*.

## Establish whether the mushrooms share one wood unit

Begin with a wide photograph showing the entire stump, log, or buried root. Count distinct tufts and note whether stems converge into separate bases or appear scattered. Gently trace one edge of the attachment without dismantling the colony. A mushroom beside an oak can arise from leaf litter, a buried branch, or a different tree's root; proximity is not proof of substrate.

Record the wood as deciduous or coniferous only when bark, end grain, retained leaves, or a knowledgeable site record supports that conclusion. If oak is defensible, note how it was determined. The [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) provides a way to separate observed substrate from an inferred nearby host.

## Follow cap form from button to flattened bell

Young caps are conical to sharply bell-shaped. With expansion they broaden, sometimes retaining a low central bump, while the thin margin may become wavy or split. Measure several caps rather than publishing a single ideal diameter. Photograph from above and at cap level so width, profile, surface texture, and the position of the margin remain reviewable.

Moisture changes the apparent color. Wet caps can look deeper grey-brown or date-brown and show translucent radial lines over the gills. Drying often pales the margin and reduces translucency. Number two or three caps and photograph them before and after natural drying in the same neutral light; do not wet specimens to manufacture a pattern.

## Use the striate margin as supporting evidence

Radial striations arise because the thin moist cap transmits the pattern of the gills below. Record how far they extend toward the center and whether they disappear as the cap dries. Striation supports a bonnet-mushroom pathway but occurs across many *Mycena* and unrelated thin-capped fungi.

Surface particles, water droplets, and phone sharpening can create false lines. A side-lit macro image and an unprocessed reference frame are more useful than a heavily saturated close-up. Preserve the original file and write down whether the cap was wet, tacky, or already drying.

## Photograph gill attachment without bending the stem

The gills are pale and attached near the stem, with shorter gills between those running from margin to stem. Photograph a naturally tilted cap or support a fallen specimen; do not twist a living stem merely to expose the underside. Record spacing, attachment, edge color, branching, and any cross-connections.

Use the [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) to describe attachment consistently. Apparent attachment changes with camera angle and cap expansion, so obtain side-on views from more than one mature fruit body. Pale gills alone are weak evidence because numerous white-spored genera share them.

## Record the stem's color gradient and shared base

Stems are slender, hollow, and fragile. A useful collection often shows a paler upper stem and a progressively brown lower portion, sometimes with coarse-looking fibrils or pale basal growth. Capture complete stems from cap to wood. Cropped photographs lose both the color gradient and the tufted attachment that give the observation value.

Measure upper and lower stem width on several ages. Note whether stems curve away from crowding, fuse only visually, or truly share basal tissue. Do not interpret every leaning stem as an “inclined” diagnostic character; growth angle changes with light, obstacles, and collapse.

## Assess odor as a recorded observation, not a verdict

Descriptions of mature *M. inclinata* often mention a rancid, farinaceous, or otherwise disagreeable smell. Odor vocabulary is subjective and can be altered by wet wood, bacteria, decay, and the container. Smell only intact material without tasting it, outdoors first and then briefly in a clean container if needed.

Record specimen age, elapsed time after collection, temperature, and the observer's own words before consulting a guide. A missing odor does not exclude the species, and an unpleasant odor does not prove it. Avoid prompting several observers with the same comparison before they report independently.

## Make a spore deposit and keep its source numbered

A white to very pale deposit is expected. Place a mature numbered cap across dark and transparent surfaces, protect it from drafts, and record time and humidity. The [spore-print protocol](/identification/how-to-make-spore-print/) explains how to distinguish a sparse pale deposit from condensation or cap tissue.

Spore color can reject a rusty-, pink-, or dark-spored alternative, but it cannot separate the many white-spored bonnets. Keep the print tied to the same cap used for underside photographs and microscopy. Mixed tufts can occur on one stump, so combining caps destroys that chain of evidence.

## Use microscopy to test a regional key

Exact *Mycena* identification may use spore dimensions and shape, amyloid reactions, basidia, cheilocystidia, pleurocystidia, cap-surface cells, stem-surface features, and clamp connections. The relevant set depends on the key. Report mounting medium, reagent, calibration, magnification, sample count, and measured ranges rather than copying numbers from a website.

Choose mature but not collapsing tissue from a numbered specimen. A white deposit confirms that spores were released, while a gill mount shows where cells were sampled. If the characters conflict with the proposed species, retain the record at genus or section level and seek a local specialist.

## Separate clustered bonnet from common bonnet

The [common bonnet guide](/mushrooms/common-bonnet-mushroom-guide/) covers *Mycena galericulata*, another frequent wood-associated bonnet. It is often more robust, but size overlaps and weather changes both species. Compare tuft density, cap development, gill architecture, stem texture, odor, microscopic characters, and the current regional treatment.

Do not select a name because one cap resembles an online photograph. A reliable comparison table begins with specimens from the same region and preserves contradictory characters. DNA can clarify some difficult material, but a sequence still needs clean sampling, a voucher, and a defensible reference database match.

## Keep reddish spotting and decay separate

Some related bonnets develop reddish or brownish spots as they age, while damaged gills can discolor through drying, bacteria, or handling. Photograph fresh tissue before transport, then record whether marks enlarge or appear on cap, gills, or stem. A stain that appears only after hours in a sealed box is not equivalent to a fresh field character.

When comparing *Mycena maculata* or other spotted taxa, use a current regional key and microscopy. “No spots” on a young collection is incomplete evidence; “spots present” on rotten material may be equally unhelpful.

## Map decomposition rather than assigning a living host

Clustered bonnets are saprotrophs using dead woody material. Mark whether fruit bodies occur on bark, exposed sapwood, cut ends, buried roots, or soil immediately above wood. Estimate decay class: intact hard wood, softened fibers, or crumbly remains. This produces ecological information without claiming a mycorrhizal partnership.

On a large stump, map tuft positions and revisit after rain. Repeated fruiting from the same sectors may reveal moisture and remaining wood structure. Leave part of the colony intact, follow land rules, and avoid spreading fragments to new sites.

## Treat range records as evidence with different strength

Databases aggregate preserved specimens, observations, literature, and sometimes records created under older taxonomic concepts. Use GBIF to locate source records, then examine identifier, date, coordinates, media, institution, and name history. NCBI provides a taxonomic and sequence-oriented route but does not validate every photograph carrying the name.

For a new regional claim, prefer a voucher reviewed with a current local key. State uncertainty when only a photograph exists. “Recorded in Europe and North America” is not the same as “native throughout both continents,” and a map point does not prove present abundance.

## Keep the species out of the food basket

This page does not recommend clustered bonnets for food. Fruit bodies are small, fragile, difficult to identify, and liable to occur beside other small mushrooms. Culinary value is negligible, while a mixed collection can defeat later review. Never taste an unidentified bonnet as a diagnostic test.

If someone becomes ill after eating wild mushrooms, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Retain uncooked material, meal leftovers, photographs, location, timing, and packaging. Do not wait for a social-media identification before seeking help.

## Understand why routine cultivation is not the goal

As a wood decomposer, the fungus can be relevant to ecological culture work, but it is not a standard edible crop. Laboratory study requires authenticated culture, sterile transfers, controls, and containment. Fruiting an unknown stump isolate does not prove that it is *M. inclinata*.

Do not move colonized wood into parks or forests. A useful study might compare decomposition or document morphology under controlled conditions, but it should preserve strain provenance and avoid environmental release.

## Build a reviewable clustered-bonnet record

1. Photograph the entire stump or log and each separate tuft.
2. Number several developmental stages and measure caps and stems.
3. Record natural wet-to-dry color change in neutral light.
4. Capture gill attachment and complete basal wood connection.
5. Describe odor independently without tasting.
6. Make a pale spore deposit from one numbered mature cap.
7. Apply a current regional key and document microscopy when needed.
8. Preserve a lawful voucher and uncertainty level.

That workflow turns a striking cluster into evidence another observer can audit. It also preserves the most important boundary: a coherent *Mycena inclinata* hypothesis is not an edibility decision.
