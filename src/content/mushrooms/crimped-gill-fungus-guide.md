The **Crimped gill fungus** (*Plicaturopsis crispa*) is best treated as a field-evidence problem, not a photograph-matching exercise. Small, thin, overlapping cream-to-tan fans on dead hardwood, with a hairy zoned upper surface and a forked, crimped, vein-like fertile underside rather than ordinary blade gills. A useful record connects the full colony, exact wood attachment, both surfaces, scale, developmental stage, region, and—where the question requires it—microscopy. It cannot authenticate a specimen, diagnose a tree, or authorize eating.

## Begin with scale and colony shape

A dramatic macro photograph can make this fungus look much larger than it is. Photograph the whole branch first, then add a ruler beside representative fans. Record the smallest and largest caps, whether they form rows or overlapping tiers, and whether they arise from bark, exposed wood, a cut surface, or a decorticated section. The colony pattern helps distinguish a true series of thin lateral fans from one fleshy mushroom torn into pieces. Keep the branch orientation visible: fruit bodies may reorient as wood falls, so posture alone does not prove when colonization began.

## Use side light to read the upper surface

The upper face can be cream, buff, tan, or pale brown and may show subtle concentric zones. Fine hairs are easiest to see with low side light, not direct flash. Record whether the surface is dry, rain-darkened, dirty, algae-covered, or frosted. Measure the thickness at the margin and near the attachment. A useful photograph preserves the wavy outline and the transition from the hairy cap to the wood. Color is supporting evidence only; exposure, moisture, and white balance can shift pale tan fans toward gray or orange.

## Turn the fan and map the forked folds

The underside carries the feature behind the common name. Photograph it square-on and obliquely. The folds branch, reconnect, and crimp rather than forming evenly spaced, knife-like blades. Trace three folds from the attachment toward the margin and note every fork. This simple map is more informative than calling the surface “gilled.” The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) explains why fertile-surface architecture matters. Do not flatten a delicate fan merely to obtain a picture; several naturally exposed undersides usually provide enough evidence.

## Separate it from split gill structurally

Split gill is an important comparison because both fungi make small pale fans on wood. Split gill typically has conspicuously hairy gray-white caps and paired lamellar edges that roll apart and close with moisture. Crimped gill presents broader, irregular, forked folds. Compare the same-sized mature specimens, and photograph the edges under magnification. The [split gill guide](/mushrooms/split-gill-fungus-guide/) gives a dedicated wet-dry workflow. When the underside is weathered, immature, or hidden, retain a broader field label rather than forcing either name.

## Exclude oysters and other lateral wood fungi

Pleurotus fruit bodies are ordinarily thicker, more succulent, and equipped with true gills that run toward a lateral attachment. Small Crepidotus, Panellus, and related wood fungi add more possibilities, including different spore colors. Texture, scale, attachment, fold construction, and spore evidence must agree. Never reduce the decision to “oyster or not oyster.” A mixed branch can host several fungi, and detached fans lose their ecological evidence. Number specimens before collecting and keep different textures or underside patterns separate.

## Record hardwood evidence honestly

Photograph bark, buds, leaves, twig arrangement, or cut wood characters when they remain. If the host cannot be named, record “dead hardwood, identity unresolved”; that statement is more reliable than assigning the nearest standing tree. Note branch diameter, whether it is suspended or soil-contacting, decay class, and competing fungal growth. These details describe the moisture and resource context that supports the colony. The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) offers a repeatable substrate vocabulary.

## Compare wet and dry condition without staging a false test

Thin fans change posture and tone as humidity changes. Revisit the same marked colony after rain if access is lawful and safe, using the same angle and scale. Do not soak a field specimen and present the result as natural behavior without labeling the method. Record margin curl, flexibility, surface color, and fold visibility. A controlled rehydration of a lawful voucher can be useful, but it should be timed and documented. Moisture response supports a description; it does not replace anatomy, substrate, distribution, or microscopy.

## Use microscopy after the field hypothesis is coherent

Microscopy is valuable only when slides remain linked to a complete collection. Retain cap, fertile folds, and attachment tissue under one number. Record objective, calibration, mount, number of spores measured, length and width ranges, and relevant tissue structures required by a current regional key. A pale deposit alone does not distinguish the many white-spored wood fungi. If the microscopic result conflicts with the photographed fold architecture, revisit specimen mixing, maturity, and preparation quality instead of selecting the most convenient measurement.

## Treat distribution records as hypotheses, not permission

Occurrence databases help establish where named records have been reported, but a map point does not authenticate a new collection. Inspect record date, identifier, voucher, accepted name, and geographic precision. North American names applied to small pleurotoid fungi have a complicated history, so current local literature matters. At a range edge, use a conservative label and preserve evidence. The correct conclusion may remain genus-level or descriptive until regional expertise, microscopy, or sequence-quality material is available.

## Keep food and exposure claims conservative

This page makes no recommendation to eat crimped gill fungus. Small tough wood fungi are easy to mix, and the condition of an exposed branch adds contamination, mold, and debris concerns. Do not taste an unresolved specimen. People with asthma, lung disease, or immune compromise should avoid deliberate spore clouds and indoor culture experiments with unidentified fungi. If illness follows a wild mushroom exposure, contact a poison center or medical service promptly and preserve photographs, leftovers, timing, and collection details.

## Build a reproducible collection record

A checkable record contains a colony overview, scale, upper surface, several undersides, side profile, exact attachment, substrate, date, region, weather, and a unique collection number. Add a plain-language uncertainty statement. If collection is prohibited, a non-destructive record is still valuable. If a voucher is lawful, dry it promptly and store it with the same identifier used in image filenames. This chain prevents attractive photographs and microscope slides from drifting apart during later comparison.

## Audit image evidence before sharing a name

Review the image set as if the branch were no longer available. At least one frame should prove scale, one should establish the direct wood attachment, and two should show the underside from different angles. Reject frames whose sharpening invents false ridges or whose denoising erases the fine hairs. Preserve an unedited original beside every cropped or color-corrected derivative, and record any focus stacking. A stacked macro can explain fold structure, but it must not be represented as a single field exposure.

Before publishing a species label, ask whether an independent reader could distinguish forked crimped folds from split lamellae or true oyster-like gills. If not, caption the observation descriptively and invite review instead of increasing certainty. This evidence audit is especially important for AI-assisted editorial covers: the cover on this page illustrates the evidence categories, but it is not an occurrence record and cannot replace authenticated photographs or a voucher.

## Understand its role in hardwood decomposition

The fruit bodies reveal a wood-decomposing organism using dead plant material. Their presence does not by itself quantify internal decay, date the death of a branch, or diagnose the safety of a standing tree. For a branch over a path or a structural timber, document defects and consult the appropriate arborist or building professional. Removing visible fans does not remove established mycelium. Ecological observation should distinguish the fungus’s decomposition role from a separate risk assessment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are the crimped folds true gills?

They are best recorded as forked, wrinkled folds rather than ordinary single blade gills; close side lighting and magnification show the branching architecture.

### What substrate should be photographed?

Show the actual dead hardwood branch or log, its decay stage, bark, diameter, and the point where each fan attaches.

### Can it be identified from a top view?

No. A top view hides the most informative feature: the pale repeatedly forked fertile folds underneath.

### Is crimped gill fungus edible?

This guide does not recommend it as food. Remote content cannot clear a specimen, and small wood fungi have many confusing alternatives.

### What makes a useful voucher?

Link a lawful dried sample to habitat photographs, both cap surfaces, measurements, date, region, and any microscopy.

## References

When folds are difficult to interpret, photograph one undamaged underside dry and again after natural humidity rises, without soaking it. Record whether the ridges merely swell or actually split, branch, or reconnect. This paired view adds evidence about the hymenial architecture while avoiding the misleading shadows created by a single oblique macro image.
