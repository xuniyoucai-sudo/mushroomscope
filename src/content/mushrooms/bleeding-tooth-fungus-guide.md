Bleeding tooth fungus is often introduced through one extraordinary feature: ruby-red drops on a pale cap. That image is memorable, but it is a poor stopping point. The droplets are a moisture-dependent event on actively growing tissue, not a permanent badge, and several unrelated fungi can exude colored liquid. A useful record must connect the droplets to a tooth-bearing fruit body, its changing cap, tough internal context, forest floor, potential tree partners, region and microscopic evidence.

*Hydnellum peckii* is also a lesson in life-stage bias. Young fruit bodies can look pale, plush and almost soft. Mature ones become irregular, pitted and brown, neighboring caps may fuse, and litter can be swallowed by expanding tissue. An observer who searches only for a white cap dotted with red may walk past the same organism later in the season.

## Confirm teeth before interpreting the red drops

Photograph the whole colony, then use a mirror to view a cap edge without uprooting it. The fertile surface should consist of many separate downward teeth, not gills, pores or torn flesh. Make a perpendicular macro image beside a millimeter scale and record tooth length, density, color and the transition from protected margin to mature center.

The [hedgehog mushroom guide](/mushrooms/hedgehog-mushroom-guide/) explains the same broad fertile-surface plan in a different construction. *Hydnum* fruit bodies are generally fleshy and conventionally cap-and-stem shaped. *Hydnellum peckii* is tougher, frequently fused and irregular, with litter incorporated around the base. Teeth establish the correct comparison group; they do not by themselves provide the species name.

If an apparent spine is actually a split pore wall, a hanging fiber or an insect channel, redirect the record. A clean section through the fertile layer shows whether teeth arise as organized tissue. Keep top, underside and section photographs tied to the same numbered specimen.

## Record guttation as an event, not a diagnostic label

Guttation is the release of liquid from growing tissue under particular water and pressure conditions. On young bleeding tooth fungus, droplets can be clear to pink or deep red. Record their size, distribution and translucency before touching them, along with recent rain, soil wetness and temperature. Do not add water, squeeze the cap or stage droplets for a photograph.

Revisit the colony after a dry interval and after rain from the same camera position. Drops may shrink, darken, spread into the cap or disappear. New growth may produce fresh beads while older cap zones remain dry. That sequence is more informative than treating “bleeding” as a binary field character.

Red exudate does not mean blood, poison or a medical substance. Pigmented fungal liquid has no direct implication for danger or benefit. Avoid sensational captions that turn an ecological observation into a toxicity claim.

## Map the cap from pale margin to brown center

Young surfaces may be whitish or blush pink and finely velvety. With age they can become salmon, rusty brown or dark brown, and may develop pits, radial wrinkles or concentric zones. Photograph protected margins and exposed centers with the same neutral color reference. Report color as a range linked to location and age.

Measure separate caps before assuming that a broad mass is one fruit body. Adjacent fruit bodies commonly fuse. Mark boundary lines, depressions and points where litter is incorporated. A fixed overhead photograph with a scale makes later expansion or fusion visible.

Section only when collection is permitted and the population can support it. Record cap thickness, color bands, moisture and the transition into the short stem. Tough fibrous or corky tissue is an important separation from soft edible-looking tooth fungi. A field note about bitterness should come from established literature or trained taxonomic practice, not casual tasting of an unknown wild fungus.

## Prove that the fruit body comes from soil

Clear a small amount of loose litter at one edge rather than pulling the colony. The base should connect to soil or densely incorporated litter. A buried twig can create the illusion of wood growth, so photograph the attachment in place. The fungus forms ectomycorrhizal relationships with living trees and is not simply consuming a visible log.

Use the [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) to grade host evidence. Record tree species only when needles, buds, cones, bark and site knowledge support the name. Plot the distances to plausible conifers and note mixed broadleaf trees. “Under spruce” is stronger when a spruce root zone dominates the plot than when one tree stands beyond several other hosts.

Soil acidity, nutrient status, canopy history and disturbance can matter to Hydnellum communities. Do not infer those variables from moss alone. A repeatable site record includes coordinates at an appropriate privacy precision, slope, drainage, litter depth, canopy composition and management signs.

## Separate Hydnellum species with a current regional key

Several *Hydnellum* species share tough tooth-bearing fruit bodies, brown spores, fused caps and occasional guttation. *H. ferrugineum* is a particularly close photographic comparison. Color descriptions overlap across age, lighting and preservation, so a red-drop photograph cannot reliably decide between them.

Document odor without forcing a familiar analogy. Under laboratory conditions, record spore dimensions from calibrated equipment, ornamentation, hyphal characters and any reactions required by the key. State how many spores were measured and preserve the full range rather than a preferred average. A voucher connects those observations with a physical specimen and allows later re-examination when species concepts change.

GBIF maps can reveal candidate records, but each point inherits the certainty and date of its source. NCBI links taxonomic names to sequence records with their own voucher and identification histories. Use databases to find evidence, not to replace morphology or local expertise.

## Distinguish soft hedgehog mushrooms and other tooth fungi

The presence of teeth first separates the specimen from gilled agarics and most boletes. Within tooth fungi, compare texture, attachment and spore color. Edible hedgehog mushrooms are normally fleshy, with distinct caps and stems and pale spores. *Hydnellum* fruit bodies are typically tough, irregular, often fused and brown-spored.

Other genera such as *Sarcodon* can produce robust terrestrial tooth fungi associated with trees. Cap scales, flesh reactions, odor, spores and current taxonomy determine the route. The goal is not to memorize one dramatic photograph but to create a table in which each candidate must explain the same measured characters.

Do not use an image-search match as edible clearance. Even a correct genus identification leaves species, condition, contamination and individual response unresolved.

## Read conservation signals cautiously

Tooth-fungus communities can be sensitive to forest continuity, nitrogen deposition, soil change and management, and some regions monitor particular *Hydnellum* species. A single fruiting absence does not prove local loss: underground mycelium can persist without producing visible fruit bodies in a dry year.

For monitoring, establish a fixed plot and separate fruit-body count from occupied area. Photograph from fixed points, record weather and avoid trampling the colony. Do not excavate every cap. If a record may be locally important, share coordinates privately with the relevant conservation recorder rather than broadcasting a vulnerable site.

The organism's relationship with trees also means that ordinary “grow kit” assumptions do not apply. Laboratory culture is not equivalent to forming a functioning mycorrhiza or producing fruit bodies in a forest.

## Keep safety claims proportional to the evidence

Bleeding tooth fungus is generally treated as inedible because it is tough and extremely bitter. That statement is not an invitation to taste-test a specimen. Guttation color cannot diagnose toxicity, and the lack of a famous poisoning syndrome cannot establish safety.

If any wild fungus is eaten and symptoms occur, contact the appropriate poison center or medical service. Retain uncooked material, photographs, location, timing and preparation details. Online identification should not delay professional care.

## Build a reviewable field record

1. Photograph the colony, surrounding trees and undisturbed attachment.
2. Link pale margins, brown centers, droplets and toothed underside to numbered specimens.
3. Measure cap dimensions, tooth length and the occupied patch.
4. Record recent rain and revisit to document guttation change.
5. Grade host evidence rather than naming the nearest tree automatically.
6. Section one representative only when lawful and ecologically appropriate.
7. Use a current regional key, calibrated microscopy and a voucher for a consequential record.

This workflow remains useful when the red droplets are absent. It replaces a viral visual cue with evidence that another observer can test.

It also preserves negative evidence: missing teeth, an unattached base, soft flesh, or a hardwood-decay habit should remain explicit contradictions rather than being edited out of the final identification note.
