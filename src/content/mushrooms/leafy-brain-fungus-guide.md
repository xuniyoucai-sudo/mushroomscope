Leafy brain fungus is a wet-weather shape-shifter. After rain it can form a conspicuous brown rosette of thin translucent folds. During dry weather the same body shrinks, darkens, stiffens, and may become difficult to recognize. A reliable record therefore treats hydration as part of the observation rather than assuming the freshest photograph is the permanent form.

The name also carries taxonomic history. Material once grouped broadly under *Tremella foliacea* has been revised, and regional records may not all represent the same modern concept. This guide uses *Phaeotremella foliacea* cautiously and prioritizes voucher-linked characters, associated fungi, microscopy, region, and current literature.

## Photograph the whole gelatinous mass before touching it

Show the complete branch or log, the number of clusters, their spacing, and exact attachment. Add a scale at the wood surface. Macro framing can turn a three-centimeter cluster into an apparently massive object and hide whether lobes share one base.

Record whether bark remains, whether the wood is standing or fallen, and whether other crusts or fruit bodies occupy the same area. Do not move the jelly onto a cleaner log for photography.

## Describe lobes instead of calling the mass brain-like

Measure several lobe widths and thicknesses. Record whether margins are rounded, torn, crisped, or thin enough to transmit light; whether surfaces are smooth, wrinkled, or granular; and whether the center is compact or open. “Brain-like” is memorable but does not separate leafy folds from solid convolutions.

Use backlighting carefully to show translucence without clipping highlights. Keep one neutral-light image for color comparison.

## Build a wet-dry-rewet sequence

On a lawful detached voucher, record mass, dimensions, lobe flexibility, and color while hydrated. Allow it to dry in contained clean airflow, then repeat at fixed intervals. Rehydrate with a measured amount of clean water and document which folds expand and whether damage remains.

Label the sequence as controlled. Re-expansion demonstrates tissue behavior; it does not prove that an old specimen resumed spore production or that every field cycle is harmless.

## Keep color tied to lighting and moisture

Brown jelly can appear amber in transmitted light, nearly black when dry, or gray under a cool camera profile. Include a neutral gray reference outside the crop and retain the raw exposure if possible. Record rainfall and surface water.

Color is a supporting character, not a species verdict. Algae, sediment, decay, frost, and age can shift it further.

## Trace the exact wood attachment

Follow the shared base to bark, exposed wood, or a neighboring crust. Photograph before sampling and record wood decay, diameter, soil contact, and host confidence. The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps distinguish the colonized substrate from nearby leaves.

Use “dead hardwood” when tree identity is uncertain. Transported firewood and flood debris can separate a branch from its original tree.

## Search for the associated fungus

*Phaeotremella* species are mycoparasitic or otherwise closely associated with other wood fungi, often crust-forming taxa. Inspect the same branch around and beneath the jelly for crusts, pores, discoloration, or old fruit bodies. Photograph those structures under the same collection code.

Absence of a visible host fruit body is not proof of independence; fungal tissue may be inconspicuous or not fruiting. Culture claims must account for both organisms.

## Compare witch's butter by lobe construction

The [witch's butter guide](/mushrooms/witchs-butter-fungus-guide/) covers vivid yellow-orange *Tremella mesenterica*-type material with thicker convoluted lobes. Leafy brain is generally brown and more leaf-like. Weathered yellow jelly can darken, however, so color alone cannot finish the comparison.

Document associated crust fungus, lobe thickness, basidia, spores, and region when the distinction matters.

## Separate wood ears from leafy jellies

Wood ears usually form discrete ear- or cup-like fruit bodies with recognizably different upper and lower surfaces. Leafy brain forms a crowded mass of numerous thin folds. The [cloud ear guide](/mushrooms/cloud-ear-mushroom-guide/) provides an intent-specific comparison focused on *Auricularia* form and food context.

A torn wood ear can mimic one brown lobe. Confirm the whole growth pattern and attachment rather than naming a fragment.

## Compare Exidia and other brown jellies

*Exidia* species may produce brown-black buttons, cushions, or effused gelatinous patches that merge. Surface papillae, attachment, drying behavior, host and microscopy route the key. Apricot jelly and other taxa introduce still different color and structure combinations.

Do not build an identification from a generic “brown jelly fungus” image search. Preserve a voucher and list unresolved alternatives.

## Section one lobe and one attachment

Cut a narrow lawful strip through margin, center, and base. Record thickness, internal inclusions, toughness, and whether lobes arise independently or from a common gelatinous core. Photograph the section fresh because drying rapidly changes geometry.

Keep attachment tissue with the voucher. A washed lobe in a dish loses the wood and associated-fungus evidence central to the record.

## Use microscopy with a current species concept

Tremellalean basidia and spores require careful preparation and interpretation. Record mounting medium, calibration, number measured, maturity, and which lobe supplied the material. Compare with a recent regional treatment rather than transferring an old broad description automatically.

NCBI supplies a taxonomic checkpoint and the Fungal Records Database supports occurrence research. Neither converts historical records into one modern species without review.

## Treat distribution maps as hypotheses

Older observations may use *Tremella foliacea* broadly, lack associated-fungus data, or predate molecular separation. When mapping range, retain the name as originally reported and mark whether a voucher was reexamined. Do not silently relabel every brown jelly photograph.

A global-looking point map can reflect taxonomy and recorder effort as much as true biological distribution.

## Record persistence without assuming revival

Mark one cluster using bark cracks and photograph it after wet and dry periods. Separate new lobes from expansion of old tissue by tracking tears, margins, and attachment. A dried fruit body can rehydrate physically without necessarily returning to the same reproductive condition.

Pair observations with rainfall and temperature, but report sensor position and limits. Weather-station humidity does not equal the boundary layer on a log.

## Keep decay ecology modest

The jelly's appearance on dead wood does not quantify wood strength or prove it caused all decomposition present. Multiple fungi and invertebrates may share the substrate. Record bark loss, softness, cracks, moisture, and other fruit bodies independently.

On structural timber or a tree part near a target, seek the appropriate professional assessment rather than scraping away fruit bodies.

## Do not generalize food claims across jelly fungi

Some *Auricularia* species are cultivated foods, but that does not make every gelatinous wood fungus edible. This page makes no food recommendation for leafy brain fungus. Identity, species concept, substrate contamination, spoilage, and individual reaction remain relevant.

Never taste a jelly to separate it. Cooking does not authenticate an unknown fungus or remove every environmental contaminant.

## Respond conservatively to ingestion

If illness follows wild-fungus consumption, contact local emergency or poison-control services. Retain photographs, raw and cooked remnants, amount, preparation, meal time, and symptom onset. Do not wait for crowd-sourced identification before seeking care.

NAMA's syndrome resource supports emergency communication, not remote confirmation of this species.

## Preserve a two-organism voucher when possible

Collect minimally and lawfully. Retain the jelly, its exact wood attachment, and adjacent crust tissue under one code; photograph their spatial relationship before removal. Dry or preserve material according to the receiving herbarium's advice, because gelatinous tissues can collapse.

Store raw images, measurements, hydration state, host confidence, region, microscopy, and the key used. An honest genus-level record is preferable to a species name that discards contradictory evidence.

## Quantify lobe geometry without flattening the specimen

Select five intact lobes distributed from the outside to the center of one cluster. With the specimen still attached, photograph each beside a small scale and record maximum width, visible thickness at a naturally exposed edge, degree of branching, and whether its margin is entire, crisped, torn, or fused to a neighbor. Do not press the jelly beneath glass for the field measurement; compression changes both thickness and apparent branching.

On one lawful voucher, repeat measurements after blotting only free surface water, then after controlled drying. Report the change for each numbered lobe. Averaging unrelated lobes into one “typical” thickness can hide a central compact zone and thin expanding margin that are biologically and taxonomically useful.

## Map the jelly-to-crust relationship on the branch

Create a simple branch diagram with distances from the cut end or a permanent knot. Mark every jelly cluster, visible crust-fungus patch, bark boundary, and exposed wood area. Photograph overlap zones under side light. If a crust continues beneath a lobe, sample both at the same coordinate and keep their vouchers paired.

Spatial association alone does not identify the partner, and absence of a fruiting crust does not exclude fungal hyphae in wood. The map turns an impression—“growing with a crust”—into evidence another observer can revisit. It also discourages stripping the entire log in search of a hidden partner.

## Distinguish one rehydrated body from new fruiting

Before rain, record cracks, missing corners, embedded debris, and the outline of each dried cluster. After rain, align the new image to bark landmarks and ask whether those exact defects expanded with the tissue. A rehydrated old lobe preserves damage; genuinely new tissue begins with a different margin and attachment. Use transparent image overlays only as an analysis aid and retain the untouched originals.

Repeat the record across more than one rain event. If volume increases, describe the observation without claiming growth unless new tissue can be separated from water uptake. This restraint is especially important for gelatinous fungi, where a dramatic change in size can be almost entirely physical hydration.
