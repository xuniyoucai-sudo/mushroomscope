## Establish scale before naming the cup

Green elfcups are minute. A macro photograph can make a three-millimeter disc resemble a large woodland cup, so every useful record needs scale. Place a millimeter rule beside—not on—the colony and photograph the cups, stained wood, and surrounding moss in the same frame. Count cup diameters across a short transect instead of reporting only the largest specimen.

The fruit bodies often begin as deep cups and flatten into shallow saucers or irregular discs. Their margins can become wavy, split, or weathered. Record the distribution of shapes across the colony. One flat turquoise spot may be paint, algae, lichen, or exposed pigment rather than a mature apothecium; repeated cup form and a visible attachment make the interpretation stronger.

## Separate the fruit body from the stained substrate

Chlorociboria mycelium produces blue-green pigments, including xylindein, that permeate decaying wood. The colored zone may extend well beyond the visible cups and can persist when no fruit bodies are present. Photograph one frame optimized for the cups and another for the wood fibers. State separately whether you observed fruit bodies, stained wood, or both.

Do not label every turquoise board as active green elfcup. Historic decorative wood can retain pigment long after collection, and photographs may not establish provenance. In the field, document bark status, wood decay, ground contact, moisture, and direct cup attachment. The [habitat documentation guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps distinguish fungal substrate from nearby colored debris.

## Show the smooth fertile surface

The exposed inner face of each apothecium is smooth at field scale. It does not carry gills, pores, or teeth. Use diffuse light plus one gentle side-light frame to reveal cup depth and margin without creating false ridges. Photograph the outer surface and the tiny central or eccentric attachment where possible.

Avoid scraping away a colony for an underside image. Select a naturally tilted fruit body or a loose fragment already separated from the main log. Note whether the stem-like base is truly present or merely inferred from posture. These distinctions matter when a macro image compresses depth.

## Map pigment across the wood

Lay a paper grid beside the stained zone and measure its length, width, and continuity. Record whether pigment follows annual rings, vessels, cracks, or a uniform decomposed layer. Photograph transitions from unstained to stained wood under constant white balance. Do not use digital saturation to make a weak stain appear diagnostic.

Repeat the map after a wet and a dry interval. Surface color may intensify when wet even though the pigment distribution has not changed. New cups may arise only during a brief humid window, while the same wood remains green throughout the year. Keeping these processes separate prevents persistent stain from being mistaken for continuous fruiting.

## Treat close Chlorociboria names as a microscopy problem

*Chlorociboria aeruginascens* and *C. aeruginosa* overlap strongly in visible color, cup form, and stained substrate. A photograph usually supports the genus better than either species. Use current regional keys and examine mature material when a species-level record matters. Relevant characters may include spore size and septation, ascus and paraphysis features, excipular structure, and hair characters.

Report calibration, mount medium, magnification, sample size, full measured range, and how maturity was judged. Do not copy a diagnostic cutoff from a guide into field notes as though it were measured. If the characters overlap or material is immature, “*Chlorociboria* species” is a precise and defensible result.

## Compare other cup fungi by size, substrate, and contrast

The [scarlet elf cup guide](/mushrooms/scarlet-elf-cup-guide/) describes much larger red cups with a pale exterior on decaying hardwood. Their size and two-tone tissue contrast differ from minute uniformly blue-green Chlorociboria cups, and they do not normally create an extensive turquoise stain through the wood. Orange peel fungus is larger, orange, brittle, and associated with disturbed soil rather than stained hardwood.

Lichens, algae, copper compounds, paint, and weathered plastic can introduce blue-green color into a scene. Ask whether an actual three-dimensional cup is present, whether it is organically attached, and whether the pigment penetrates wood fibers. A color patch alone is insufficient.

## Record decay and host confidence

Describe the substrate as a fallen branch, log, stump, buried fragment, or worked wood, and record whether bark remains. Host identification can be difficult after advanced decay. Use wood anatomy, attached bark, nearby matching branches, and documented site history, then express confidence rather than inventing certainty. Chlorociboria occurs on hardwoods broadly; one nearby tree is not proof of host.

Assess decay by observable resistance, fiber separation, bark loss, moss cover, and ground contact. Name any formal scale used. A soft, waterlogged log and a hard suspended branch create different moisture regimes even in the same forest. Map shade and drainage so recurrence can be interpreted in context.

## Preserve a tiny voucher without losing context

Photograph before collection. If permission and a legitimate identification purpose justify a voucher, remove a minimal wood chip holding multiple mature cups and some unstained margin. Assign a field number immediately. A loose cup without stained substrate and habitat metadata loses much of its evidence.

Dry the sample promptly with gentle airflow appropriate to small ascomycetes. Store it in a packet that prevents the cups from being crushed, and retain high-resolution fresh photographs because color and shape can change during drying. Link slides, measurements, images, and any sequence to the same voucher.

## Interpret databases and pigment research carefully

GBIF and similar portals are discovery tools. Filter records by basis, institution, date, coordinate quality, image, and preserved specimen. A point near the site does not identify a colony; an apparent gap can reflect collecting effort. Nomenclatural databases organize names, while sequence databases and microscopy address different evidence questions.

Xylindein has attracted interest in historic decorative wood and materials research. That context does not make a wild colony medicinal, edible, or appropriate for unsupervised pigment extraction. Keep materials-science claims tied to the cited experiment, organism or culture, extraction method, and measured outcome rather than generalizing from the color.

## Keep food and safety claims conservative

This guide makes no food recommendation. The fruit bodies are tiny, the close species are difficult to separate visually, and a low profile in poisoning literature is not evidence of safety. Do not taste the cups or stained wood. Environmental contaminants, treated timber, and mixed colonies create additional uncertainty.

If someone has ingested an unknown fungus, contact the relevant poison center or medical service. Preserve remaining material, photographs, substrate details, estimated amount, time eaten, and symptoms. Do not delay care while attempting microscopy and do not induce vomiting unless a qualified service directs it.

## Build a repeatable colony record

Select a fixed twenty-centimeter section of wood. Record cup count, diameter classes, developmental stages, stained area, shade, wood moisture, recent rainfall, and temperature. Photograph from the same marked angle on each visit. The method can show whether cups appear in cohorts and whether visible pigment predicts fruiting at that site.

Do not infer causation from one log. A second nearby stained log without cups is valuable comparison material. Record it using the same protocol. The contrast may reveal moisture, decay, or microclimate associations worth testing, while keeping the conclusion local and observational.

## Verify that scale and color survive the image workflow

Keep one uncropped habitat frame, one scale frame, and one macro frame for every numbered colony. Phone macro modes may synthesize background blur, merge frames, or exaggerate turquoise saturation. Inspect cup margins and moss stems at full resolution for doubled edges, missing attachments, and repeated texture. Those artifacts can make separate fruit bodies appear fused or make a flat stain look raised.

Use a neutral reference and disclose white-balance or contrast adjustments. Photograph an unstained section of the same wood under identical light so the pigment contrast is observable rather than remembered. Avoid ultraviolet or unusual illumination unless the method and purpose are stated; fluorescence or shifted color should never be presented as ordinary daylight appearance.

## Separate colonization, staining, and fruiting dates

A single date cannot describe every part of the colony. Record when the wood was first found stained, when cups were first observed, and when mature cups suitable for microscopy were collected. If the log is revisited, state whether the same marked area produced new cups or whether fruiting moved along the substrate. This timeline prevents persistent pigment from being reported as a year-round crop of fruit bodies.

Where a section of wood is already naturally broken, photograph pigment depth through the fibers. Do not saw intact habitat merely to expose color. A surface-only stain, pigment penetrating the wood, and actively fruiting cups are distinct observations. Reporting them separately makes the record reusable for ecology, curation, and materials-history questions without implying that one proves the others.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does wood turn blue-green around green elfcup fungi?

Chlorociboria mycelium produces xylindein pigments that can permeate decaying wood; stained wood may remain visible when no fruit bodies are present.

### How large are green elfcup fruit bodies?

They are tiny, commonly only a few millimeters across, so a scale bar and macro photograph are essential to avoid making them look like large cup fungi.

### Can stained wood identify Chlorociboria aeruginascens?

No. Blue-green wood supports Chlorociboria activity, but species separation generally needs fruit bodies, microscopy, region, and current specialist literature.

### What is the difference between Chlorociboria aeruginascens and C. aeruginosa?

They overlap strongly in visible form and pigment; microscopic characters, including spore measurements, and regional treatments are usually needed.

### Is green elfcup edible?

This guide makes no food recommendation. The fruit bodies are minute, species-level identity is difficult, and an online page cannot assess a real collection.
