Purple jellydisc is best understood as a millimeter-scale wood record, not as a purple blob isolated from context. *Ascocoryne sarcoides* can form rounded gelatinous cushions, convoluted lobes, and shallow irregular discs on damp dead hardwood. Those forms may represent developmental or reproductive states, while close species can overlap macroscopically. Scale, wood continuity, maturity, and microscopy therefore carry the identification.

This guide covers the small *Ascocoryne* record. The [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) explains how to record substrate without overclaiming a host, while the [jelly ear guide](/mushrooms/jelly-ear-fungus-guide/) covers a much larger ear-shaped wood jelly. The editorial image illustrates morphology but is not specimen evidence.

## Establish scale before describing shape

Place a millimeter scale beside the colony without touching it and make a perpendicular overview. Individual bodies may be only a few millimeters across; macro photography can make them resemble large cups or folded ears. Record the range, not only the largest cluster, and include enough branch diameter to preserve context.

Then photograph three states: the smallest rounded cushions, intermediate lobes, and the most open discs. Note whether they connect at the base. Do not call a lobed mass “brain-like” without dimensions, because that phrase collapses small ascomycete discs and much larger basidiomycete jellies into one visual category.

## Map cushions and cups on one wood unit

Lay a paper scale along the branch and map each cluster by distance from an endpoint or knot. Record which forms share a continuous strip of wood. Rounded conidial-looking cushions and open fertile cups may coexist, but proximity alone does not prove that every purple patch is one species.

Assign specimen codes before sampling. If microscopy is planned, take a small lawful portion from a mature disc and a separate cushion, keeping their positions and photographs linked. This is more informative than mixing the whole colony into one container and losing state provenance.

## Read the fertile surface without inventing gills

An open fruit body presents a smooth to subtly uneven upper fertile surface. It has no gills, teeth, or pores. Photograph grazing light across the disc and make a clean vertical section. The cut should show gelatinous tissue and a direct or very short attachment to wood.

Rain droplets, ice, and slug damage can create false pits. Record the untouched wet surface first, then a sheltered view as free water drains. Never add “veins” or “pores” from reflections in a saturated macro image. A descriptive record should distinguish observed relief from reproductive anatomy inferred under a microscope.

## Track wet, dry, and freeze-thaw states

Mark a small group and revisit it after a dry interval and after rewetting by natural rain. Record width, thickness, translucency, and color with fixed exposure. Gelatinous bodies can shrink, darken, or become less conspicuous when dry, then expand again. That change does not by itself indicate a new flush.

In cold weather, note ice before touching the colony. Freeze-thaw damage can make margins ragged and surfaces watery. Compare protected and exposed parts of the same branch rather than attributing every texture difference to taxonomy. Do not spray a rare colony merely to create a photographic transformation.

## Prove attachment to decaying wood

Make a side view showing the fruit bodies emerging from bark, exposed wood, or a bark fissure. Record whether the branch is suspended or soil-contacting, its diameter, bark retention, decay softness, and moisture. “On a log” omits the wood condition that helps explain where the colony formed.

Host names require evidence. Follow the same wood unit to attached twigs, buds, bark, or leaves where possible, and report confidence. Beech is a well-known substrate, but *Ascocoryne sarcoides* is not restricted to a photographed beech-like branch. “Dead hardwood, host uncertain” is a valid and reusable label.

## Separate colonizer from wood-decay neighbors

One branch can carry crusts, pyrenomycetes, molds, and other jelly fungi. Photograph colony boundaries and sample them separately. A black stroma beneath a purple disc may be a neighbor rather than an old stage, while pale mycelial fans under bark may belong to another decay organism.

The [leafy brain guide](/mushrooms/leafy-brain-fungus-guide/) documents a larger, folded basidiomycete jelly with a different structure and ecological interpretation. Size, attachment, and microscopy prevent a general “purple jelly fungus” label from erasing these distinctions.

## Time maturity for microscopy

Choose a well-expanded disc that has not collapsed. Prepare thin sections through the fertile surface and underlying gelatinous tissue, and document the mounting medium, optics, calibration, and source specimen. Record asci, paraphyses, and spores only when the structures are mature enough to interpret.

For spores, report the number measured, complete range, central tendency if used, septation state, and whether measurements came from discharged spores or tissue. Immature nonseptate spores should not be compared casually with mature septate descriptions. Preserve the slide notes and a dried voucher where lawful.

## Test the Ascocoryne cylichnium boundary

*Ascocoryne cylichnium* is the critical close comparison because photographs can be nearly indistinguishable. Cup shape and purple color are insufficient. Separation depends on mature microscopic measurements and a regional key, with specimen condition and reproductive state made explicit.

If only photographs exist, use *Ascocoryne* sp. or an *A. sarcoides/cylichnium* record according to local recording practice. A cautious aggregate label preserves evidence for later review; a confident species name based on color alone makes occurrence data harder to clean.

## Exclude pale cups and larger jellies

*Neobulgaria pura* can form translucent pale pink, lilac, or whitish gelatinous cups on hardwood. Compare colony color under neutral light, individual size, cup development, tissue section, and microscopy. Weathered purple jellydisc can fade, so color is a starting observation rather than a verdict.

Species of *Exidia* and *Tremella* often form larger lobed or brain-like masses and are basidiomycetes with different microscopic fertile structures. The [jelly ear guide](/mushrooms/jelly-ear-fungus-guide/) shows how a larger ear-shaped body carries a different geometry. Keep the complete colony and attachment in every comparison.

## Quantify a branch-level colony

Choose a fixed length of wood, such as 30 centimeters, and count occupied patches rather than attempting to count fused lobes as individuals. Record the percentage of retained bark, decay class, soil contact, shade, and whether water collects on the upper or lower surface. Repeat with the same boundaries after wet weather.

This protocol tests whether new fruiting follows newly exposed wood, bark edges, cracks, or consistently damp faces. It does not estimate the full mycelium or prove causation. Report “fruit bodies detected within the marked segment” and retain zero observations from dry visits.

## Keep food and health claims outside the record

The fungus is not presented as food or medicine. Tiny gelatinous appearance does not establish edibility, and decaying wood can carry several organisms and environmental contaminants. No field taste is needed for identification.

After suspected ingestion, contact local poison or medical services for case-specific advice. Preserve photographs, substrate information, remaining material, timing, and estimated amount when safe. Do not wait for a webpage to predict symptoms or attempt treatment.

## Separate fruit-body change from branch movement

A fallen branch can rotate, settle into litter, or lose bark between visits. Mark a fixed knot and photograph the branch orientation, upper face, soil-contact face, and drainage direction at every survey. If a cluster appears to move from the top to the side, test whether the wood moved before proposing a change in fruiting preference.

Use the same 30-centimeter segment and map new, persistent, collapsed, and no-longer-detected patches. Do not equate a missing wet-weather cushion with death; it may have dried below easy visibility. Conversely, do not count a rehydrated patch as a new individual. A transparent event log makes phenology more useful than a sequence of unregistered macro photographs.

Record bark loss and fresh breaks after storms. Newly exposed wood may reveal colonies that were already present beneath loose bark, while abrasion can remove fruit bodies. The survey can describe these coincident changes but cannot assign causation without a designed study.

## Make a microscopy handoff another person can repeat

Package mature disc tissue, cushion tissue, and any neighboring fungus separately. For each preparation, record specimen code, branch coordinate, collection time, mounting medium, stain if used, objective, calibration file, and whether spores were discharged or measured within asci. Save uncropped microscope frames as well as annotated versions.

When comparing with *A. cylichnium*, state the regional key, publication date, measured sample size, spore maturity, full range, and septation distribution. A single unusually long spore should not define the collection, and an average without raw range hides overlap. If the structures are immature or crushed, request a later fertile sample rather than converting poor material into certainty.

This handoff also prevents the cushion from one coordinate and the mature disc from another being treated as one developmental proof. Another examiner should be able to reconstruct exactly which visible fruit body produced every measurement.

## Frequently asked questions in context

### Do large open cups make the identification certain?

No. They show maturity, but close *Ascocoryne* species and other gelatinous cups still require microscopic and regional comparison.

### Can the same colony look pink and purple?

Yes. Age, hydration, thickness, and light can shift apparent color. Record color with scale and condition rather than splitting names by one photograph.

### Is a conventional spore print useful?

Usually not in the agaric sense. Mature tissue sections and discharged-spore preparations with clear specimen provenance are more useful than waiting for a visible pile.
