Wrinkled club fungus replaces the ordinary mushroom cap and stem with a pale, irregular column. Its lengthwise folds can look carved, and the apex may stay blunt, flatten, or divide into a few short prongs. That restrained architecture is the useful starting point for a *Clavulina rugosa* hypothesis. It is not a species verdict: age, damage, parasites, fused neighbors, and several pale club fungi can create similar silhouettes.

A strong record connects the entire base, branching order, surface condition, flesh, habitat, spore evidence, microscopy, and a current regional key. Do not infer edibility from a white club shape. The page supports documentation and comparison; it cannot clear a real collection for food.

## Photograph the complete growth form

Begin with an undisturbed low side view that includes every apex and the point where each club enters the litter. Add a top view and a second side view at right angles. A single camera angle can hide a fork behind the main axis or make two touching fruit bodies appear to share one base.

Place a ruler beside, not on, the cluster. Measure total height, width at the lower and upper thirds, number of visible divisions, and the distance from base to the first fork. Give each club a specimen number before lifting anything. These measurements keep “small,” “slender,” and “slightly branched” from becoming subjective labels.

## Map wrinkles instead of describing a rough surface

Use soft side lighting to show the folds. Record whether grooves run continuously from base to apex, break into short ridges, twist around the axis, or become flattened at the top. Separate true tissue folds from drying cracks, slug tracks, soil impressions, and wounds.

Photograph fresh and older members of the same patch. A waterlogged club may look smooth, while drying can exaggerate every ridge. If the collection spans different conditions, record surface moisture before comparing it. Wrinkles support the name only when they belong to intact tissue rather than post-fruiting damage.

## Count branches from the base upward

Wrinkled club can remain simple or divide sparingly, but it should not be treated as a miniature coral merely because one apex forks. Trace each axis from the soil to its final tip and count branch orders. A first-order fork near the top differs from the repeated tree-like branching typical of many coral fungi.

The [violet coral guide](/mushrooms/violet-coral-fungus-guide/) shows how branch hierarchy, axils, tips, and a common base can be recorded. Use the same method for a pale collection. If only a broken terminal fragment is available, architecture is missing evidence and the identification should stay broader.

## Preserve the base and substrate boundary

Loosen litter around one representative fruit body and lift the complete attachment. Photograph adhering soil, fine roots, moss, needles, leaves, and any buried wood separately. A club emerging through litter is not automatically growing from litter; a hidden twig or root can change the ecological interpretation.

Do not write “on beech” because beech leaves dominate the frame. Record nearby trees by observed bark, leaves, cones, or buds, plus measured distances. The [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) separates the visible substrate from a proposed biological partner.

## Section one club and track change over time

When lawful and proportionate, cut one mature club lengthwise from apex through base. Record whether the flesh is solid, chambered, insect-tunneled, waterlogged, or discolored. Photograph immediately and after a fixed interval under the same light. A hollow left by larvae is not a taxonomic cavity.

Avoid taste testing. Older field traditions sometimes use taste in limited groups, but it is unnecessary for a public documentation workflow and does not establish safety. Describe odor briefly from intact fresh material and note specimen age; do not convert “earthy” or “mild” into an identity claim.

## Distinguish the giant club by measured proportions

Giant club fungus, *Clavariadelphus pistillaris*, is generally a much stouter ochre or buff pestle. Its upper portion expands, its apex remains broadly blunt, and it may reach dimensions far beyond a small pale *Clavulina*. Wrinkled club is usually narrower, paler, more twisted, and more willing to divide shallowly.

Use the [giant club fungus guide](/mushrooms/giant-club-fungus-guide/) for a matched comparison of height, width at three levels, apex shape, branch count, color, flesh, habitat, and spores. Do not use size alone: young giant clubs and unusually robust wrinkled clubs can overlap.

## Separate gray clubs and parasite-altered colonies

Names around *Clavulina cinerea*, *C. rugosa*, and related forms have been interpreted differently across regions and studies. Gray color, more abundant branching, spore characters, and molecular evidence may redirect a collection, but no single photograph resolves the entire complex.

Some *Clavulina* fruit bodies are altered by other fungi, which can darken, swell, or deform the surface and suppress normal spore production. Map unusual patches rather than averaging them into the description. Sample clean and altered zones separately if an expert requests microscopy or DNA work.

## Make a controlled spore and microscopy record

Because the fertile surface is external, lean a mature club over clean glass or dark paper without crushing it. Protect the setup from drafts, record duration and humidity, and keep tissue debris separate from a pale deposit. A white print supports the group but is not specific.

Microscopy may record spore length, width, shape, surface, oil contents, basidia, clamps, and hyphal features required by the chosen key. State reagent, calibration, number measured, and full range. Database descriptions cannot substitute for measurements from the labelled specimen.

## Treat maps and names as review tools

GBIF records can locate vouchers and institutional observations, while NCBI connects taxonomic and sequence-linked records. Both require reading the basis of record, identifier, date, geography, and name usage. A dot on a map does not authenticate a photograph or prove that the same species concept was applied.

For a record outside the familiar range, seek review from a local mycological group or herbarium and preserve material only where permitted. Report “pale *Clavulina*” when the regional species boundary is unresolved. Honest uncertainty makes the record reusable.

## Keep safety and conservation boundaries visible

This guide does not recommend wrinkled club fungus as food. A webpage cannot inspect the complete colony, microscopic features, local lookalikes, spoilage, pollutants, pesticides, or individual sensitivity. Small pale clubs are also easy to mix in a basket with unrelated fragments.

If illness follows wild mushroom ingestion, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Keep uncooked material, leftovers, photographs, location, and timing. Do not wait for an online name before seeking care.

Collect sparingly, especially in protected or repeatedly visited woodland. Photograph the colony, map a defined patch, and leave most fruit bodies to mature. Revisit with the same search boundary after comparable rainfall rather than treating one conspicuous flush as a population trend.

## Test patch persistence without assuming one organism

Mark a non-sensitive reference point and map every club within a fixed one-square-meter plot. Give each fruit-body cluster a coordinate, record its branch count and maximum height, and repeat the survey after rain, drying, and the following fruiting season. Distinguish a club that has expanded from a newly emerged neighbor by comparing the mapped base and dated overview photographs.

The exercise measures visible fruiting, not the boundary or age of an underground mycelium. Several genetic individuals may fruit close together, and one individual may extend beyond the plot. Report fresh, damaged, parasitized, and senescent clubs in separate columns so a change in condition is not misreported as disappearance. A stable method can reveal where clubs recur, how long individual fruit bodies remain legible, and which microhabitats retain moisture; it cannot prove population growth from one productive autumn.

For a management record, add trampling, litter removal, canopy work, drainage change, and search effort. These local variables give a future reviewer something testable while avoiding a universal ecological claim from a small woodland patch.

## Build a reviewable field packet

1. Photograph the full colony and each complete base.
2. Measure height, three widths, first-fork height, and branch order.
3. Map wrinkles under side light and separate damage from intact tissue.
4. Record substrate layers and nearby trees without assuming a partner.
5. Section one representative club only when justified.
6. Obtain a labelled spore deposit and microscopy if the key requires it.
7. Compare giant clubs, gray *Clavulina*, and repeatedly branched corals.
8. Preserve uncertainty and seek regional review.

The result should explain why the material follows a wrinkled-club hypothesis and what still prevents a species-level conclusion. That evidence chain is more useful than a confident label built on a pale silhouette.
