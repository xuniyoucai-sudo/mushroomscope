Brittle cinder fungus can pass unnoticed because the mature fruit body resembles a patch of burned bark. Its young stage may look entirely different: gray-white, relatively soft margins surrounding a darker center. A record that captures only the final black crust loses the developmental evidence that helps separate fungal tissue from char, paint, tar, and other black wood fungi.

The species also matters beyond naming. *Kretzschmaria deusta* is associated with decay in roots and the lower stem of broadleaf trees. That association warrants attention where a tree could strike people or property, but a photograph cannot calculate residual wall thickness, root-plate condition, loading, wind exposure, or likelihood of failure.

## Start with the entire tree and target zone

Photograph the whole tree, crown, lean, nearby structures, paths, vehicles, and occupied areas before moving to the fungus. Then show the complete root flare and all visible stromata. Record whether the tree is living, dead, a stump, or a cut log. A close black patch without tree context cannot support risk triage.

Urgent conditions—fresh soil lifting, new cracks, hanging limbs, sudden lean, or movement—should be isolated from people and assessed promptly. Do not strike, excavate, or load a suspect root to “test” it.

## Map each stroma around the root flare

Use compass direction or a fixed landmark and measure the height, width, and distance from soil for every patch. Note whether growth follows a buttress root, pruning wound, bark seam, cavity edge, or buried wood. Repeat photographs from the same positions at later visits.

The map is evidence of visible fruiting, not a map of the hidden fungus. Mycelium and decay can extend beyond stromata, while an old crust may remain after active margins change.

## Distinguish the pale young stage

Fresh growth may form flattened gray to whitish sheets with a darker central area. Photograph the surface under diffuse light, its boundary with bark, and any transition to older black tissue. Record softness only on a small lawful sample or naturally detached fragment; repeated pressing destroys the evidence.

White margins should not be treated as a universal character. Rain, age, algae, dirt, and secondary organisms alter color. A dated series provides more value than selecting the palest example online.

## Examine mature carbonaceous crusts

Mature stromata become black, hard, cracked, and brittle. Raking light can reveal low mounds and tiny surface openings associated with embedded perithecia. Add a millimeter scale and photograph intact, cracked, and weathered zones separately.

Do not scrape the entire patch to expose fresh tissue. If identification work is justified, retain one minimal section that includes surface, interior, substrate boundary, and collection code.

## Prove that it is not fire damage

Actual char usually occurs within a broader heat pattern: scorched bark, altered wood grain, ash, heat cracking, or a known fire history. Brittle cinder stromata have organized boundaries, developmental stages, and reproductive structures. A pale active margin beside black mature tissue is especially useful evidence.

Site history still matters. A fungus can fruit on previously burned wood, so “fungal” and “fire-affected” are not mutually exclusive. Record each observation independently.

## Section one voucher through a mature patch

Where lawful and safe, cut a small radial section from edge to center. Record thickness, hardness, color layers, perithecial cavities, and attachment. Photograph the section immediately because drying can alter contrast and fracture patterns.

Keep the fragment linked to tree, position, date, and in-situ images. A loose black crust collected from the ground cannot establish which root or even which tree produced it.

## Use mature microscopy, not a copied spore number

Identification within the Xylariales often depends on asci, dark ascospores, germ-slit characters, and measurements from mature perithecia. Record mounting medium, calibration, number measured, and the range actually observed. Immature pale tissue may not supply the structures needed.

NCBI provides a lineage checkpoint and First Nature a regional field account. Neither validates a reader's photograph. Results that disagree with a current regional key should remain unresolved.

## Compare dead man's fingers structurally

The related [dead man's fingers guide](/mushrooms/dead-mans-fingers-fungus-guide/) documents projecting clubs that darken into carbonaceous stromata. Brittle cinder generally spreads as an irregular crust. Crowded or weathered material can blur that distinction, so attachment, section and spores still matter.

Do not decide by the common names “fingers” and “crust.” Describe the geometry in measured terms that another observer can test.

## Separate other black stromatic fungi

Wood supports numerous black *Hypoxylon*, *Biscogniauxia*, *Xylaria*, and related fungi. Host, position on the tree, growth form, internal anatomy, perithecial layout, and spores route the key. Bark disease, slime molds, lichens, paint, and tar also belong in the first visual screen.

Collecting a black fragment without young tissue or exact attachment often makes the problem harder. Preserve the colony pattern before sampling.

## Record host confidence honestly

Photograph bark, buds, leaves, fruit, branching, and surrounding trees. A root flare can be connected confidently to the trunk, but the tree species may still be uncertain outside leaf season. Use “living broadleaf” when evidence does not support a narrower host.

The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps keep substrate and nearby plants separate. A leaf resting on a root is not host proof.

## Do not translate presence directly into failure

Fungal decay can reduce wood properties without creating an obvious hollow. The USDA Wood Handbook explains that strength also depends on species, grain, moisture, defects, geometry and load. No fixed fruit-body size corresponds to a fixed residual strength.

A qualified arborist can integrate species, vitality, targets, site exposure, root condition, sounding or drilling when appropriate, and uncertainty. The online name is only one input.

## Preserve a repeatable monitoring record

Use permanent external landmarks, not nails driven into roots. Repeat overall tree, root-flare, and macro images after major weather and at a defined interval. Record new pale margins, expansion, cracking, bark separation, crown change, soil disturbance, and construction.

Monitoring is not a substitute for assessment when consequences are high. Its value is showing change rather than relying on memory.

## Avoid harmful do-it-yourself treatment

Removing the visible crust does not remove internal mycelium or restore wood. Fungicide, paint, bleach, sealing compounds, root excavation, and unplanned pruning can damage the tree or obscure evidence. Management must follow the actual tree and site assessment.

If removal is recommended, tree work near structures and power lines belongs to insured qualified professionals. The fungus page does not specify rigging, climbing, or felling.

## Apply a conservative food and exposure boundary

The stromata are hard, brittle, wood-attached structures and are not presented as food. Do not taste black crusts. Dust from old material, treated wood, soil, animal waste, and unrelated molds add exposure concerns.

If ingestion occurs, retain material and contact a poison center or medical service as appropriate. NAMA supports poisoning communication, not identification by image.

## Preserve evidence for both mycology and arboriculture

A useful record contains tree and target photos, a root-flare map, young and mature stages, exact voucher position, host evidence, section, microscopy, dates, and observer. Keep risk-assessment conclusions separate from taxonomic notes so neither exceeds its evidence.

Corrections should identify the claim, source, and date. No named mycologist, arborist, wood scientist, or clinician reviewed this page; review status is editorial.

## Build a stage-linked surface transect

Choose a patch where pale, gray, and black tissues meet. Lay a flexible paper scale beside—not across—the stroma and define five equally spaced observation windows from the fresh outer edge toward the oldest center. In each window, record color under fixed white balance, surface height, cracking, brittleness of a naturally detached crumb, and the number of visible perithecial dots within one square centimeter. Repeat the same transect after a wet and a dry interval.

The aim is not to invent a universal maturation clock. It is to show which characters coexist on one connected stroma. Pale tissue from one root and black crust from another cannot be combined into a false life series. If the boundary retreats, becomes covered by bark debris, or is damaged by maintenance, keep that event in the record.

## Separate fungal extent from the assessment zone

An arboricultural inspection area should extend beyond the visible crust. Photograph the lower stem, all accessible buttress roots, surrounding soil, nearby paving, recent trenching, grade changes, irrigation, and signs of root cutting. Mark where visibility is blocked by soil, ivy, mulch, structures, or ownership boundaries. “No fruit bodies seen” in an inaccessible sector is not evidence that the sector is unaffected.

The assessor may choose tools or tests based on tree, target and site; this page does not prescribe drilling or excavation. Any invasive examination should have a defined question, competent operator, and documented location so the wound and result can be interpreted later.

## Record neighboring fungi without merging diagnoses

One root flare may support brittle cinder, bracket fungi, crusts, slime molds and old pruning-wound organisms. Give each growth its own code, photographs, attachment, and provisional identification. The [Artist's conk guide](/mushrooms/artists-conk-mushroom-guide/) illustrates a perennial bracket pathway with different external construction; its presence nearby would not cancel or confirm a black stromatic fungus.

This separation prevents a common reporting error: assigning every visible fruit body to one decay column and then making one unsupported risk conclusion. The final tree decision should state which observations were confirmed, which were provisional, what portions were inaccessible, and when reassessment is due.
