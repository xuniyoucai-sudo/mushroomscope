Blewits are remembered as purple woodland mushrooms, but “purple” is not a safe identification. A useful determination must explain cap aging, gill attachment, stem fibers, spore color, substrate, and the absence of a veil. This guide treats those observations as a connected test and marks the point where regional expertise or microscopy is still needed.

The accepted name used here is *Collybia nuda*. Many current field guides use *Lepista nuda*, while older sources may use *Clitocybe nuda*. These names help locate literature; they do not make every violet *Lepista*-like collection one species.

## Identification profile at a glance

A typical young blewit has a smooth lilac cap, crowded violet gills, and a stout fibrous stem colored much like the gills. Mature caps expand, develop wavy margins, and may fade almost completely to tan or gray-buff. There is no volva or durable ring. A pale pinkish-buff spore deposit supports the identification; a rusty-brown deposit contradicts it.

No item works alone. Begin with the [complete mushroom-identification workflow](/identification/how-to-identify-mushrooms/) and preserve several ages from the same troop where collecting is permitted.

## Cap development and color loss

Young caps are convex with an incurved margin. The surface is smooth, sometimes slightly greasy in damp weather, rather than scaly. Expansion produces a broadly convex or nearly flat cap; old margins may lift or ripple. Violet often fades from the center after sun, rain, or frost, leaving buff adults beside purple buttons.

Photograph that sequence before moving anything. Phone white balance can intensify mauve, and soaked specimens can appear darker than they are. Color should be described under neutral daylight and supported by structure, never treated as a password.

## Gills and their attachment

The gills are crowded and violet when fresh and young, then become dull lilac or pinkish buff. They attach to the stem or form a shallow notch; they do not run far down it. A close side view should show the junction without tearing the cap away.

Brown soil on a few gill edges differs from a uniform rusty maturation. Examine clean specimens at multiple ages. Rust spreading across mature gills is a warning that the collection may be a *Cortinarius* rather than a blewit.

## Stem, base, and internal texture

The stem is fibrous and comparatively substantial, usually lilac to pale violet and sometimes enlarged near its base. Leaf litter can cling to pale mycelial material. It should lack a sac-like volva, and it does not carry a persistent skirt-like ring.

Lift one permitted specimen with the entire base. Cutting at soil level discards useful evidence. Slice it lengthwise to record internal color, insect tunnels, hollowing, and flesh texture. Blewit flesh is more fibrous than brittle; waterlogging and decay can erase that difference.

## Making the spore deposit count

Place a mature cap over adjacent light and dark nonabsorbent surfaces, cover it against drafts, and allow enough time for a visible deposit. The expected pale pinkish-buff color may look nearly cream in poor light, so view it in neutral daylight.

A clearly rusty deposit redirects the identification. A failed print says little: the cap may be too young, old, dry, or damaged. The [identification safety checklist](/identification/mushroom-identification-safety-checklist/) explains why a print supports a complete record but cannot independently approve food use.

## Odor is secondary evidence

Fresh blewits are often described as aromatic, fruity, or perfumed. People perceive odors differently, and temperature and condition change volatile compounds. A faint smell does not exclude blewit; an agreeable smell does not establish edibility.

Waft air toward the nose rather than tasting unknown material. Strong decay, sourness, chemical contamination, or visible mold describes specimen condition, not a diagnostic blewit character.

## Leaf litter and compost ecology

Blewits decompose organic debris. They occur in deciduous litter, conifer duff, compost-rich gardens, hedgerows, park margins, and mulched beds. Troops, arcs, and rings reflect a mycelium extending through a suitable layer, not a connection among the visible stems.

Write “beech leaf litter over soil” or “aged wood-chip garden bed,” not merely “on ground.” Record nearby trees, litter depth, disturbance, and any buried wood. Ecology helps distinguish a litter decomposer from fungi tied to roots or recognizable timber.

## Season and geographic limits

Cool wet autumn weather is typical through much of the temperate Northern Hemisphere. Fruiting can continue into early winter and occur outside that window in mild maritime climates. Season supports a hypothesis but cannot overrule anatomy.

Occurrence maps provide plausibility, not proof. Records vary in verification quality, and species concepts may differ among continents. Use a current regional key rather than applying a photograph and description from another region without qualification.

## The violet Cortinarius problem

Purple *Cortinarius* species deserve first attention because the genus includes toxic fungi. Young fruit bodies may carry a cortina: fine cobweb-like threads between cap edge and stem. After rupture, rusty fibers or a dusty rusty band may remain on the stem. Mature gills and the spore deposit commonly turn rusty brown.

Weather can remove an obvious web, so its absence on one adult is weak evidence. Search young specimens and clean stems. An ambiguous rusty zone, uncertain deposit, or collection composed only of battered adults should remain unresolved.

## Amethyst deceiver and close blewit relatives

The amethyst deceiver, *Laccaria amethystina*, is normally smaller and more delicate. Its gills are thick and widely spaced rather than crowded, and the stem is slender. Rain fades both species, making proportions and gill spacing more useful than purple intensity.

*Lepista sordida* is often smaller and thinner-fleshed but can overlap closely. Other violet taxa vary by region. When macroscopic characters fail to separate plausible names, microscopy or DNA is an honest requirement rather than a detail to improvise.

## A reproducible field record

1. Photograph the intact troop and surrounding litter.
2. Record date, place, weather, trees, and exact substrate.
3. Document young, middle-aged, and mature fruit bodies.
4. Photograph cap, gill attachment, full stem base, and possible veil fibers.
5. Section one specimen and note flesh color and texture.
6. Make a spore deposit and assess it under neutral light.
7. Test the whole record with a current regional key and qualified local knowledge.

This sequence keeps contradictory evidence visible. A name must account for the rusty fibers that should not be present just as clearly as it accounts for violet gills.

## Food history without remote clearance

Blewits have a culinary history after cooking, but a reputation is not permission to eat a particular collection. Purple lookalikes, mixed collections, environmental contamination, spoilage, and individual gastrointestinal response all matter. Cooking cannot make a poisonous misidentification safe.

Avoid roadside, treated-landscape, and industrial-site specimens. If symptoms occur after a wild mushroom meal, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Retain uncooked material, trimmings, photographs, and meal timing for professional assessment.

## Handling a verified product

Only after identity and supply chain are established, refrigerate promptly in a breathable container, reject slimy or sour-smelling material, prevent cross-contamination, and cook thoroughly. A market label on cultivated product does not transfer to a similar mushroom found in a yard.

Blewits release water in the pan. Moderate batches and adequate evaporation allow browning; crowding steams them. That cooking mechanism does not answer identification or personal-suitability questions.

## Cultivation context

Outdoor blewit beds may use aged compost, leaf litter, or related organic material, but performance depends on strain, climate, moisture, substrate maturity, and competitors. This is less standardized than a common commercial fruiting block. Authenticated culture is essential; cloning an unknown purple mushroom preserves the uncertainty.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a blewit lose all purple color?

Yes. Mature, sun-exposed, rain-washed, or frosted caps may become buff. Younger companions, gills, stem fibers, spores, and substrate then carry more weight.

### Is a pale print enough to exclude every dangerous lookalike?

No. It helps reject typical rusty-spored *Cortinarius*, but other comparisons and regional species may require microscopy.

### Why preserve the stem base?

It shows attachment, swelling, mycelial material, and any volva-like structure. Trimming it removes evidence.

### Are blewits linked to tree roots?

They are generally treated as saprotrophic litter decomposers. Trees still shape the litter, moisture, and local community.

### Can an app confirm blewit?

No. It may suggest names but cannot reliably assess spores, disappearing veils, microscopy, contamination, or edibility.
