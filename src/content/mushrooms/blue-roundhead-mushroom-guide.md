The **blue roundhead**, *Stropharia caerulea*, is easy to notice when fresh and easy to misread after weathering. Its sticky blue-green cap may fade from the center toward ochre, the young veil may leave only a few white fragments, and its weak ring zone can collapse or become dark with spores. Those changes overlap strongly with the verdigris agaric, so a responsible record needs several ages and more than cap color.

This page focuses on a developmental evidence chain: cap moisture, veil distribution, gill maturation, spore deposit, stem anatomy, substrate, region, and microscopy limits. It treats the species as inedible and does not use appearance to infer psychoactive chemistry.

## Capture the blue-green surface before it dries

Photograph the undisturbed group in diffuse daylight. Include a neutral gray reference and turn off vivid processing because woodland shade and automatic white balance can push green toward cyan. Record whether the cap is sticky, merely damp, or dry, and whether bits of grass or litter adhere to it.

Young caps are convex and can be a cool pale blue-green. Expansion produces a broader cap while exposed centers often fade first through greenish yellow to ochre. Photograph protected tissue near the margin as well as the center. Repeat the same specimen after a measured drying interval; this shows color change without combining unrelated mushrooms.

## Measure a developmental series

Select a young cap, an expanding cap, and a fully sporulating cap from one coherent patch. Give each a specimen suffix. Record diameter, height, center color, margin color, surface viscosity, and visible veil remnants. Do not choose only the bluest individual, because that practice hides the very transition needed for comparison.

Keep a habitat frame, a scale frame, and macro frames connected by the collection code. A close-up without scale can make a modest roundhead resemble a much larger wine cap. State when grazing, trampling, or partial burial makes a measurement unreliable.

## Map sparse veil tissue across cap and stem

Blue roundheads may show white veil flecks when young, but the remnants are often sparse or short-lived. Photograph the closed margin before the veil breaks, then trace material to the cap edge and stem. Rain can wash cap flecks away; expansion can stretch them beyond recognition.

The stem may carry a fragile ring or only a fibrillose ring zone. Falling spores can darken its upper surface. Use the [veils and rings guide](/identification/mushroom-veils-rings-guide/) to distinguish a membrane, fibers, a spore line, and handling damage. Ring weakness on an old specimen is not proof that a veil never existed.

## Follow gill color into maturity

Turn several stages without tearing their attachment. Young gills are relatively pale; mature faces become brownish purple as spores accumulate. Photograph the gill edge and face in the same sharp frame. Blue roundhead accounts generally do not emphasize the strongly contrasting whitish mature edges used in comparison with verdigris agaric.

That distinction is a tendency, not a remote identification switch. Lighting, immature spores, drying, and image sharpening change apparent edge contrast. Record the evidence as present, absent, or unknown, then retain the other characters rather than forcing a conclusion.

## Produce a specimen-linked dark deposit

Place a mature cap over white and dark nonabsorbent surfaces, cover it against drafts, and label collection number and time. A heavy deposit should be purple-brown to very dark brown. A few dark marks beneath an old cap are insufficient because soil, moisture, and decaying gills can stain paper.

The [spore-print method](/identification/how-to-make-spore-print/) standardizes the setup. A dark deposit supports *Stropharia* but cannot separate close species. When a precise record matters, measure mature spores, document calibration and mount medium, and examine cystidia, gill edges, cap cuticle, and veil tissues with a current regional key.

## Document the full stem and its base

Lift one lawful specimen with its base intact. Photograph all sides before cleaning. The stem is pale, sometimes carrying blue-green tones or scattered fibers, and lacks a cup-like volva. Soil and white mycelium can imitate veil tissue, so retain an untouched view and brush only a small comparison area.

Measure stem length and thickness above and below the ring zone. Section it lengthwise to record internal structure and any color change, but do not convert a nonspecific bruise into a species feature. An unexpected volva, substantial skirt, rusty veil, or different spore deposit should reopen the entire determination.

## Prove the substrate instead of naming the nearest object

Blue roundheads are saprotrophic in humus-rich grass, leaf litter, gardens, park edges, and decaying organic material. Trace the stem base gently. A mushroom beside a twig may arise from soil humus; one that appears terrestrial may stand over buried chips. Describe what is physically connected and what remains inferred.

Map grass, litter depth, woody debris, drainage, canopy, and recent disturbance. Fertilized lawns, roadside verges, and treated parkland also create contamination questions distinct from identity. The habitat record is ecological evidence, not food permission.

## Compare verdigris agaric in matched stages

The [verdigris agaric guide](/mushrooms/verdigris-agaric-mushroom-guide/) covers the closest visual competitor. Fresh *S. aeruginosa* often carries more conspicuous white scales, particularly near the margin, and mature gills may retain pale edges. *S. caerulea* is often paler, loses scales readily, and lacks the same conspicuous edge contrast.

Compare young with young and mature with mature. A pristine verdigris button beside a washed old blue roundhead creates an artificial difference. Build a table of cap viscosity, scale persistence, gill-edge contrast, ring construction, spore measurements, habitat, and region. Unknown cells should remain unknown.

## Exclude aniseed funnels structurally

The aniseed funnel can carry blue-green color, but it lacks the sticky scaled cap and ring-zone pathway of *Stropharia*. It produces pale spores and often has a strong anise-like odor. Mature caps may become funnel-shaped. The [aniseed funnel guide](/mushrooms/aniseed-funnel-mushroom-guide/) treats that odor and gill structure as a separate intent.

Record odor by wafting air from a fresh section; do not taste an unresolved mushroom. An absent odor is weak evidence when material is old, the observer has a cold, or competing environmental smells dominate.

## Keep wine caps in the correct scale and habitat

Wine caps share dark spores and a ring but are much more robust. They commonly show burgundy to brown caps, thick stems, a substantial toothed or cogwheel-like ring, and managed wood-chip habitat. The [wine cap guide](/mushrooms/wine-cap-mushroom-guide/) documents that garden-bed and cultivation context.

Measure cap width, stem thickness, and ring structure rather than relying on color. Both taxa fade, and old photographs can lose their original scale. A dark print identifies neither one without the rest of the record.

## Use occurrence maps with evidence grades

GBIF search records can show where the name has been reported, but map points differ in precision and identification support. Note whether a record links to a preserved specimen, an image, or only an observation; record coordinate uncertainty and the date the database was checked.

First Nature provides a practical European account and comparison with verdigris agaric. NCBI is a lineage checkpoint. Outside a well-documented range, preserve a dried voucher and consult regional specialists rather than transferring a European photo key unchanged.

## Keep chemistry separate from appearance

Claims about compounds in blue-green *Stropharia* are inconsistent across popular sources and cannot be generalized to every specimen. A photograph cannot establish identity, concentration, dose, contaminants, or individual risk. This page therefore does not claim psychoactive content and does not interpret the absence of a verified compound as safety.

Treat the mushroom as inedible. If symptoms follow ingestion, contact emergency services or poison control promptly. Preserve uncooked material, leftovers, photographs, location, timing, and other exposure details, and do not induce vomiting unless a clinician directs it.

## Blue roundhead field checklist

1. Photograph multiple ages under fixed neutral light.
2. Record cap stickiness and center-to-margin fading.
3. Map sparse veil flecks, ring zone, and spore staining.
4. Show attached gills and their brown-purple maturation.
5. Produce a heavy, labeled dark spore deposit.
6. Photograph the complete stem base and actual substrate.
7. Compare verdigris agaric, aniseed funnel, and wine cap explicitly.
8. Preserve a voucher and state uncertainty when microscopy is missing.

The [identification safety checklist](/identification/mushroom-identification-safety-checklist/) supplies the stopping rule when the evidence chain is incomplete.
