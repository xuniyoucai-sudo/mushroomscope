Candlesnuff fungus is a small wood decomposer whose two-tone fruit bodies can look as if black twigs were dipped in white ash. The name usually refers to *Xylaria hypoxylon*, but the common name does not resolve every slender *Xylaria*. Age changes the visible surface, buried wood hides the true substrate, and several related fungi produce dark persistent clubs. A defensible record combines the entire stroma, its attachment, developmental stage, internal construction, spores, locality, and a current regional key.

## Read the fruit body from base to tip

Start with an undisturbed group and a millimeter scale. Typical stromata are upright, narrow, and tough. The lower portion is black and often cylindrical, while the upper part may flatten, split, or fork like small antlers. Young upper surfaces can be coated in pale gray or white powder. Mature specimens may become darker throughout, develop a subtly bumpy surface, or lose delicate forks.

Do not select one dramatic fork and ignore the rest of the colony. Photograph simple clubs, forked bodies, broken tips, and old black structures separately. Measure total height, width at the base, width below the first division, and the number of terminal branches. Those observations describe a population without pretending that one silhouette is fixed.

## The white coating marks a life stage

The pale material on young tips is commonly associated with an asexual state that produces conidia. It is not frost, mold, or proof of a species by itself. Rubbed, rain-washed, or old fruit bodies can lack an obvious coating. Conversely, pale debris and secondary molds can make unrelated dark wood fungi look superficially similar.

Use a hand lens. Record whether the pale layer is evenly powdery, patchy, granular, or composed of separate growth. Photograph the boundary where white tissue grades into black. Avoid wiping the surface before documentation; handling destroys precisely the age evidence needed for comparison.

## Trace the substrate before calling it terrestrial

Candlesnuff fungus decomposes wood, especially decaying broadleaf material. A cluster beside a log is not necessarily attached to the visible log, and a cluster apparently in soil may arise from a buried stick, root fragment, stump edge, or old woody fruit structure. Photograph the emergence point and gently remove loose litter only when access rules allow.

The [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) explains how to distinguish soil, litter, and buried wood without destructive excavation. Note bark if present, wood hardness, diameter, decay class, moisture, and surrounding trees. Write “decayed broadleaf wood” when the host cannot be established; an unsupported tree name adds false precision.

## Mature black surfaces contain different evidence

As sexual structures mature, embedded flask-like perithecia can give the surface a finely dotted or pimply appearance. A clean longitudinal section may show a pale interior beneath a dark rind. Make the section only after exterior photographs and use one representative specimen where collection is lawful.

Under magnification, mature spores and their dimensions help separate similar taxa. A blackish deposit may be difficult to obtain from a small upright stroma. Placing mature material over clean glass in a humid but not wet chamber can preserve any discharge. Absence of a visible deposit is not evidence for a pale-spored species; it may simply mean the structure was immature, dry, or exhausted.

## Separate dead man's fingers by architecture, not nickname

*Xylaria polymorpha*, commonly called dead man's fingers, usually forms thicker, blunt, swollen fingers. Its bodies can cluster at stump bases or buried wood and become black outside, so habitat alone does not separate it. The [dead man's fingers guide](/mushrooms/dead-mans-fingers-fungus-guide/) details the broader, club-like profile and internal evidence.

Small or immature material can overlap. Record ratios rather than adjectives: height divided by maximum width, width at the base versus tip, and distance to the first branch. Candlesnuff material is often more slender and frequently branched above, but a single unbranched body should remain provisional.

## Compare other slender Xylaria in context

Names within *Xylaria* are tied to mature anatomy, spores, and substrate. *Xylaria carpophila* is associated especially with old beech fruit structures and may appear as thin dark threads with pale tips. Other species occur on particular fruits, petioles, or wood. Buried substrate can conceal these associations.

A useful field label may therefore be “*Xylaria* cf. *hypoxylon*” until mature material is studied. “Cf.” communicates a comparison, not certainty. Retain collection date and photographs because one site can show pale asexual tips and dark mature stromata at different visits.

## Build a developmental sequence

Return to a marked cluster without disturbing it. From the same camera position, photograph it every seven to fourteen days. Record rainfall, surface moisture, loss of white coating, darkening, branching damage, and the appearance of embedded dots. Use a small fixed ruler placed beside—not on—the colony.

This sequence prevents a common error: treating pale-tipped and uniformly black stages as different species merely because they were photographed on different days. It also shows persistence. Xylaria stromata can remain visible long after soft mushrooms have collapsed, so presence does not give an exact fruiting date.

## Ecology is decomposition, not a living-tree diagnosis

The fungus participates in breaking down dead woody material. Finding it near a tree does not demonstrate that it killed the tree, nor does one fruiting establish the full decay history. Separate observations of dead substrate from claims about disease. If stromata emerge from a stump or root, document whether tissue is dead, partly dead, or connected to a living tree before seeking arboricultural advice.

Wood decay communities are layered. Mosses, slime molds, other fungi, invertebrates, and bacteria can occupy the same log. Do not assume every dark club on a piece of wood belongs to one organism. Give separate field numbers to structures that differ in texture, branching, or attachment.

## A measured microplot improves the record

Mark a 20-by-20-centimeter plot around a cluster without attaching anything to the wood. Count stromata by three categories: pale-tipped, mixed pale-and-black, and fully black. Measure five representatives from each available category. Record exposed wood area, moss cover, and moisture after a dry day and after rain.

The resulting table is more informative than “many candlesnuff fungi.” It can reveal whether surface appearance tracks size or exposure, and it documents sampling limits. It does not prove a causal relationship, but it provides a repeatable observation another recorder can check.

## Food and handling boundary

## Preserve a useful voucher without flattening the evidence

When a record genuinely warrants collection and rules permit it, take one young and one mature stroma with a small piece of attached wood. Keep them in separate paper packets labeled with the same site record and different specimen suffixes. Photograph each before drying. Gentle warm-air drying preserves the carbonaceous body better than a sealed plastic bag, where condensation can encourage secondary mold and erase the powdery surface. Include the wood orientation and emergence point in the notes. A voucher without substrate, locality, date, and developmental stage loses much of the evidence that makes this fungus distinguishable.

Do not press wet stromata flat between paper. Their three-dimensional branching and embedded surface structures matter. If microscopy is planned, reserve intact mature tissue and note which surface produced spores. A curator can decide whether the material is sufficient; collecting more after the context is lost rarely repairs a weak record.

Candlesnuff fungus is not presented here as food. The stromata are small and tough, species-level identification may require microscopy, and dark wood fungi include multiple lookalikes. Do not taste unknown fungi as an identification test. Ordinary photographic handling is not equivalent to ingestion, but wash hands after field work and keep specimens away from food preparation.

If illness follows mushroom ingestion, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Preserve uncooked material, photographs, location, timing, and any packaging. Do not delay care while seeking a crowd-sourced name.

## Evidence limits and editorial method

GBIF and NCBI help check names, lineage, and records; they do not confirm a photographed specimen. Forest Service material supports wood-decay context rather than a claim about one tree. NAMA supports conservative exposure response, not an edible classification. Regional monographs and microscopy remain necessary where a species-level record matters.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are the white tips spores?

The powdery young surface commonly produces asexual conidia. Mature sexual spores arise from embedded structures in darker tissue, so the visible white layer and a mature spore discharge represent different parts of the life cycle.

### Can it grow on conifer wood?

Records are most often associated with broadleaf wood. When the host is uncertain, document wood characters and surrounding trees without forcing a name; regional treatments may clarify local substrate breadth.

### Does touching make it turn black?

The major pale-to-black change is developmental and weather-related, not a useful instant bruising test. Rubbing can remove pale powder and expose darker tissue, which is damage rather than diagnostic chemistry.

### Is every forked black club candlesnuff fungus?

No. Other *Xylaria* and unrelated stromatic fungi overlap. Attachment, scale, maturity, surface anatomy, spores, and geography must agree.

### Should a whole colony be collected?

No. Photograph first. If collection is lawful and scientifically justified, take the smallest representative sample that preserves base and mature tissue, leaving most of the colony and substrate intact.
