Smoky bracket is a thin wood-decaying polypore whose most informative character lies underneath. The upper surface can resemble many grey or tan shelf fungi, but a fresh fruit body develops a fine smoke-grey pore layer that contrasts with its pale active margin. That contrast, combined with bracket thickness, tissue section, hardwood attachment, decay pattern, and microscopy, supports a *Bjerkandera adusta* hypothesis.

The species is ecologically important as a white-rot decomposer. It is not presented here as food or medicine. Photographing an upper surface alone loses the decisive pore evidence and encourages confusion with turkey tail, hairy brackets, and other thin polypores.

## Map the bracket colony before taking a close-up

Start with the entire log, stump, branch, or wound. Record whether brackets form separate shelves, fuse into rows, or transition into a flat pore-bearing crust. Note orientation, height above soil, bark retention, exposed sapwood, moisture, shade, and the length of the occupied wood.

Mark several zones along the colony. Old dark shelves and a pale fresh margin can coexist, and different fungi may occupy adjacent sectors. A single beauty photograph cannot show whether one organism covers the wood or several bracket species overlap.

## Inspect the smoky pore surface first

Turn naturally accessible edges toward diffuse light and use a hand lens or macro lens. The underside should resolve into minute pores rather than gill blades or smooth crust. Fresh pores may begin pale and become smoke-grey to darker grey with age, handling, or drying; record the state rather than assigning one fixed color.

The [pore-surface guide](/identification/mushroom-pore-surface-guide/) explains how to describe pore shape and density. Photograph beside a millimeter scale, count openings along a measured line in several areas, and report a range. Do not count a blurred image or treat dirt-filled pores as smaller openings.

## Preserve the pale active margin

A growing bracket often has a pale cream edge outside the darker pore field. Measure its width at several points and photograph the boundary between active margin and mature hymenium. Rain, abrasion, algae, and frost can erase or discolor that edge on persistent material.

The pale zone is supporting evidence, not a standalone test. Other brackets have white margins, while a fully weathered smoky bracket may have little active growth. Revisit after rain to distinguish a newly extending margin from exposed torn flesh.

## Read upper-surface zoning conservatively

Caps are thin and semicircular to irregular, often grey-brown, buff, or tan with subdued concentric zones. Young surfaces can be finely hairy; older ones become smoother, darkened, green with algae, or bleached. Photograph dry and damp states under neutral light because saturation can make weak zones look like turkey-tail bands.

Record radius, thickness, overlap, texture, and edge shape. Strong orange, blue, red, and black bands are not required and should not be invented through editing. Upper zoning narrows the comparison only when linked to the smoky pores below.

## Cut one lawful sample through cap and pores

Where collection is permitted and abundant, take a narrow radial section through one numbered mature bracket. Photograph the profile with a scale. Record the thickness of upper context and pore tubes, their color boundary, flexibility, and whether layers are sharply separated.

Smoky bracket is typically thin, with pale context above a distinctly darker tube layer. A section helps distinguish surface staining from tissue architecture and reveals whether several annual layers are present. Do not cut living-tree tissue simply to obtain a specimen.

## Verify lateral attachment and resupinate growth

Fruit bodies usually lack a stem and attach directly to wood. Some colonies extend flat along the substrate before forming shelf edges. Photograph the transition at both ends of a row. A flat pore patch is easily overlooked or mistaken for a different crust fungus when the shelves receive all attention.

Trace only loose bark or already exposed wood. The [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) separates observed attachment from an assumed tree species. A bracket on the ground may still be attached to a buried branch.

## Document deciduous wood without guessing the tree

The species is commonly associated with dead broadleaf wood. Record bark, wood anatomy, branching, retained leaves or buds, and site management before naming a host. “Hardwood” is a useful observation when species-level tree evidence is missing; an unsupported oak or beech label weakens the record.

Note whether the fungus occupies a fallen log, stump, cut branch, standing snag, or dead portion of a living tree. Fruiting on a wound does not by itself establish the cause of decline. Trees fail through interacting fungi, drought, roots, storms, and structural defects.

## Separate white rot from fruit-body identity

White-rot fungi degrade lignin as well as cellulose, often leaving pale fibrous wood. Photograph sound wood and decayed wood at an already broken surface, recording softness, fibers, moisture, and cubical versus stringy texture. Do not excavate a tree or strike structural wood to demonstrate decay.

Decay type supports ecology but is shared by many polypores. Conversely, dark or wet wood beneath a bracket may conceal a pale fibrous interior. Treat fruit-body characters, wood condition, and tree risk as three related but separate observations.

## Compare turkey tail by underside, not color bands

The [turkey tail guide](/mushrooms/turkey-tail-mushroom-guide/) covers *Trametes versicolor*. Turkey tail commonly has stronger multicolored zones and a white to cream pore surface. Smoky bracket is usually more subdued above and grey below. Weathering, algae, and camera color can obscure both distinctions.

Compare brackets from the same wood unit: upper texture, zone colors, pore color, pore density, thickness, context section, and microscopy. Do not name a shelf “turkey tail” because it is zoned or “smoky bracket” because it is grey.

## Keep Bjerkandera fumosa in the comparison

*Bjerkandera fumosa* can overlap in geography and wood habitat. It is often described as thicker and more buff-toned, with differences in pore surface and context, but variable and weathered collections may not separate cleanly by a single field trait.

Use a current regional key, section several ages, and document spores and hyphal characters. Preserve a voucher when the distinction matters. Database photographs are useful for generating questions, not for replacing specimen-based comparison.

## Exclude pale-pored hairy Trametes

Species such as *Trametes hirsuta* can form pale zoned hairy brackets on hardwood. Check whether the upper hairs are coarse or merely fine young tomentum, whether the underside remains white or cream, and whether pore size and context match the relevant key.

Old caps collect algae and dirt, while undersides darken through handling. Photograph a fresh margin and protected pore area before cleaning. A grey smear on a white pore surface is not equivalent to naturally smoky pore tissue.

## Make a controlled white spore deposit

A white deposit may be obtained from an actively sporulating fresh bracket. Suspend or place the pore surface over dark and transparent media without crushing it, protect from drafts, and record duration and humidity. Sparse bracket deposits can be difficult to see.

Spore color alone will not distinguish most candidate polypores. Keep released material tied to the numbered fruit body and use it for calibrated microscopy. Do not scrape tube tissue onto the surface and call the debris a print.

## Apply microscopy to close cases

Regional keys may use spore size and shape, basidia, cystidia-like elements, hyphal construction, clamp connections, and reactions in tissue. Report mounting medium, reagent, optics, calibration, sample count, and measurement ranges. Sample a clean mature pore layer rather than a mixed old colony.

Sequence evidence can help with difficult or research material, but a sequence needs a voucher, clean tissue, quality control, and appropriate reference data. A database match without specimen context does not repair a contaminated sample or incorrect locality record.

## Interpret distribution records cautiously

GBIF can reveal specimen and observation records, while NCBI connects the name to taxonomic and molecular resources. Inspect the underlying record type, identifier, institution, date, location precision, and taxonomic name history. Aggregated points are not equal in evidentiary strength.

Describe verified temperate records without claiming uniform global abundance. A persistent bracket can be observed outside its fresh-growth season, and a photograph dated in winter does not prove that fruiting began then.

## Record seasonal growth separately from persistence

Mark the margin of selected brackets in a non-damaging photograph and revisit after rainfall. Measure outward extension, new pore color, cracking, insect damage, and drying. Persistent old shelves may remain visible for months after active growth stops.

This distinction improves phenology data: “seen in December” describes observation, while a pale expanding margin or measured extension supports active growth. Avoid writing universal monthly calendars from one site.

## Keep tree-risk claims within evidence

Fruit bodies on a living or standing tree indicate fungal activity in associated wood, but they do not quantify remaining wall thickness, root stability, load, or failure probability. Do not strike, drill, or excavate a tree based on this page. Photograph location and restrict access if an immediate hazard is plausible.

For a tree near people or structures, consult a qualified local arborist who can integrate species identification with structural assessment. The fungus name and the risk decision are different conclusions.

## Do not turn environmental research into treatment advice

Wood-decay fungi are studied for enzymes, pollutant transformation, and other laboratory applications. Such research may use a defined strain, purified enzyme, controlled concentration, and measured endpoint. It does not establish that a wild bracket is safe to eat, brew, inhale, or use as medicine.

This page makes no therapeutic claim. Avoid home exposure to cultures or contaminated wood dust, and use appropriate institutional controls for laboratory work. People with respiratory sensitivity should minimize handling of actively sporulating material.

## Build a repeatable smoky-bracket record

1. Photograph the entire occupied wood unit and map separate colonies.
2. Capture upper surfaces, pale margins, and protected pore surfaces.
3. Measure bracket radius, thickness, margin width, and pore density.
4. Section one lawful abundant specimen through context and tubes.
5. Record wood type, decay texture, position, and management history.
6. Compare turkey tail, *B. fumosa*, and pale-pored *Trametes*.
7. Obtain a controlled spore deposit and microscopy when necessary.
8. Preserve a voucher and state the confidence level.

That sequence makes the smoky underside part of a complete ecological record rather than a shortcut. It also preserves the safety boundaries: a species hypothesis does not establish food use, medical value, or the structural condition of a tree.
