**Blushing bracket fungus**, *Daedaleopsis confragosa*, is a tough wood-decay polypore whose pale fertile surface often forms branching, maze-like openings. Fresh pore tissue may discolor pinkish or brown after contact, which explains the common name. Neither the blush nor a brown zoned cap is sufficient by itself: the bracket must be documented as one linked set of cap, margin, pore geometry, section, attachment, substrate, region, and age.

This page owns the thin maze-pored *Daedaleopsis* field-record intent. The [oak mazegill guide](/mushrooms/oak-mazegill-guide/) covers a thicker oak-associated bracket with deeper, coarser labyrinths. The [hairy bracket guide](/mushrooms/hairy-bracket-fungus-guide/) covers a gray zoned *Trametes* with coarse cap hairs and small round pores. Keeping those evidence chains separate prevents a dramatic underside photograph from overriding host and tissue structure.

## Begin with the complete wood unit

Photograph the branch, log, stump, or dead standing portion before approaching the fruit bodies. Record diameter, ground contact, bark retention, cut faces, decay hardness, moisture, orientation, and whether the wood is living, recently dead, or long fallen. Include enough surroundings to show whether the site is a river margin, wet scrub, managed park, woodland, or stored timber.

Willow is an important association, but a willow leaf beside a log does not identify the substrate. Trace attached twigs, buds, bark, and wood anatomy where possible; otherwise retain “unknown broadleaf.” A moved branch can lie beneath another tree. Host evidence narrows the comparison only when it belongs to the fruiting wood.

Map all colonies along a measured length. Mixed polypores frequently share a log, so number individual brackets or connected patches. The cap selected for a portrait, the underside photographed for pores, and the section retained for microscopy must carry the same number.

## Measure cap zones and active margins

For fresh and weathered brackets, record width along the wood, outward projection, thickness at the base and edge, attachment width, and spacing between shelves. Use a scale in the cap plane. A tilted ruler makes thin shelves appear thicker, while overlapping tiers can hide the attachment.

Describe concentric zones by color, texture, and position rather than reducing the cap to “brown.” Gray, buff, reddish-brown, and darker weathered bands can coexist. Note whether the growing edge is pale, rounded, sharp, cracked, algal, or insect damaged. Rain can darken the entire cap and compress visual differences.

Repeat one fixed view after a defined wet interval. New pale tissue extending beyond a stable reference mark is stronger evidence of active growth than a color change alone. An old persistent shelf may rehydrate without producing new tissue.

## Prove that the underside has pores, not gills

The fertile surface is the key structural feature. Photograph it square-on with a millimeter scale and neutral white balance. Enclosed openings should form elongated, branching, and locally angular paths. From a shallow angle, long pore walls can imitate gills; a true gill runs as a blade rather than enclosing repeated openings.

Count openings across several measured lines at the edge, middle, and near the attachment. Record where pores are rounder, more stretched, split, clogged, or eroded. The [pore-surface guide](/identification/mushroom-pore-surface-guide/) provides a method for separating pores, folds, teeth, and cracks without relying on a single vocabulary label.

Do not clean an underside aggressively. Mud, algae, and insect debris are part of the condition record, and scraping can manufacture a fresh pale patch that did not exist in the field.

## Test bruising as a timed observation

On one abundant, lawfully handled fresh bracket, choose adjacent intact pore squares. Leave one as a control and apply the same gentle pressure to the other with a clean blunt tool. Photograph both immediately and at fixed intervals under unchanged light. Record the starting moisture, temperature, pressure method, and exact area.

A pinkish, reddish, or brown response supports the observation but does not prove the species. Old, dry, frozen, or already stained tissue may respond weakly. Other fungi can discolor, and camera auto white balance can create an apparent blush. Report “no visible change under these conditions” rather than converting a negative trial into exclusion.

## Cut a specimen-linked radial section

Where collection is permitted and the colony is abundant, cut one radial slice from cap surface through context and tubes to the attachment. Photograph the fresh cut with scale. Measure total thickness, context thickness, tube depth, any dark boundary, moisture gradient, and the transition between old and active tissue.

The section distinguishes surface dirt from colored tube tissue and reveals whether a thick-looking bracket is actually several fused layers. Keep the slice with its field number and wood record. A loose fragment found beneath a multi-species log cannot safely be reassigned after removal.

Dry a small voucher with airflow rather than sealing it damp. Record drying conditions and later color change. Microscopy or sequencing is only useful when results can return to the exact bracket and attachment shown in the field photographs.

## Separate oak mazegill by host and construction

Oak mazegill is typically a heavier, thicker bracket with deep coarse labyrinths and a strong oak association. Compare measured cap thickness, pore depth, host confidence, annual-versus-persistent construction, and the fresh context. Do not decide from the word “maze”: pore geometry varies within a bracket and both surfaces weather.

If the host is confirmed oak and the body is massive with deep daedaloid tubes, follow the oak-mazegill evidence path. If the shelf is thinner on willow-like wood with a documented blush, *D. confragosa* becomes a stronger hypothesis. Ambiguous host and eroded pores justify a group-level record rather than a forced species name.

## Exclude round-pored and smooth-backed brackets

Hairy bracket and turkey tail may share zoned caps, but their fertile surfaces usually show many small round pores. Smoky bracket has a gray pore layer with fine openings. False turkey tail has a smooth to wrinkled underside rather than true tubes. Photographing only the upper side erases the most useful separation.

Old pore walls can split until they resemble teeth, while grazing insects can enlarge openings. Compare protected areas beneath overlaps with exposed edges. If different zones contradict one another, retain separate specimen numbers and investigate whether more than one colony is present.

## Record white rot without predicting tree failure

Blushing bracket participates in hardwood decomposition. Fruiting on a fallen willow branch is ordinary nutrient cycling. Fruiting on dead tissue attached to a living tree documents colonized wood, but the photograph does not measure remaining wall thickness, root condition, load, or likelihood and timing of failure.

For a tree near a path, building, vehicle, or play area, record exact bracket position, cavities, cracks, dead unions, lean, root disturbance, and changes through time. Seek a qualified local arborist or tree-risk assessor. Species identification and structural assessment answer different questions.

## Build a repeatable pore-change record

Select fresh, mid-aged, and eroded brackets on the same mapped wood unit. Photograph fixed underside squares near the margin and attachment using the same scale, distance, and light. Trace a sample of pore walls, count complete openings, and record the fraction obscured by damage instead of guessing through it.

This series can show whether pores elongate, split, or erode with age in that colony. It cannot establish a universal developmental schedule because moisture, orientation, insects, and genetics differ. Retain the original images so another observer can audit the classifications.

Pair the pore series with cap-zone photographs. A surface that looks like a different species after bleaching may still connect continuously to the same attachment and internal context.

## Keep food and health claims outside this intent

The fruit bodies are tough and not treated here as food. A correct name would not remove contamination from roadside wood, treated timber, industrial sites, animal waste, or mixed decayed material. Historical craft or laboratory research does not establish a household tea, supplement, or medical use.

If an unknown fungus was swallowed, preserve photographs and remaining material and contact local poison-control, veterinary, or medical services as appropriate. Do not wait for online identification. Wash hands after handling rotten wood and avoid inhaling dust from dry fruit bodies.

## Build an auditable blushing-bracket record

1. Photograph and measure the entire occupied wood unit.
2. Confirm or conservatively label the broadleaved host.
3. Link cap, underside, section, and voucher by specimen number.
4. Photograph pores square-on and distinguish them from gills.
5. Run one timed bruise comparison with an untouched control.
6. Compare oak mazegill, hairy bracket, smoky bracket, and false turkey tail.
7. Separate decay identification from any tree-risk conclusion.
8. State confidence and preserve contradictory evidence.
