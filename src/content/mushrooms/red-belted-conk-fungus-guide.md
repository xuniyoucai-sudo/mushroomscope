**Red-belted conk** refers to hard perennial brackets historically grouped around *Fomitopsis pinicola*. The familiar field picture combines a dark zoned upper crust, a muted red-brown resinous belt, a pale active margin, fine pores, and growth on dead or stressed wood. Modern regional work has shown that this familiar name can cover more than one lineage, so a field record should preserve evidence rather than force every red-edged bracket into one global species concept.

This page owns the complex-level documentation intent. The [tinder fungus guide](/mushrooms/tinder-fungus-mushroom-guide/) covers a gray hoof without the same red belt, and the [artist's conk guide](/mushrooms/artists-conk-mushroom-guide/) covers a broad *Ganoderma* complex with a bruise-reactive pore surface. Neither silhouette alone is decisive.

## Begin with the whole wood unit

Photograph the trunk, log, stump, or dead branch before approaching the bracket. Record whether the wood is standing or fallen, bark retention, diameter, ground contact, fracture, visible roots, and surrounding trees. Name a host only when leaves, cones, bark, wood anatomy, or site records support it.

Map every fruit body along a measured length. Separate old blackened brackets, actively growing pale margins, broken shelves, and other fungi. Several species can share one log. A close-up of the most colorful belt loses the decay and substrate evidence needed to interpret the record.

## Read the upper surface as a growth history

Measure projection, width, thickness, attachment width, and height above ground. Older zones may become gray, brown, or nearly black and develop cracks, algae, or moss. The red to red-brown band is usually most evident behind a pale active margin, but rain, age, resin, and exposure alter its saturation.

Photograph the same bracket with diffuse light and a scale. Avoid increasing saturation until the belt becomes diagnostic by editing. Record whether the crust looks matte, varnished, sticky, or dry and whether gentle warmth affects resinous areas. Do not use a flame test on a living tree or protected site.

## Inspect the pore surface without confusing droplets and bruises

The underside bears fine pale pores rather than gills. Photograph a clean area at right angles with a millimeter scale and record pore density in several zones. Note cream, yellowed, stained, insect-damaged, and actively growing tissue separately. The [pore-surface guide](/identification/mushroom-pore-surface-guide/) provides a repeatable documentation method.

Fresh margins may exude clear droplets in wet growth conditions. Droplets are physiological context, not a species test. Check whether touching produces a persistent color change and record the time course; dirt transferred from a finger is not a bruise reaction.

## Preserve the broad attachment and annual tube layers

Where collection is lawful and justified, photograph a detached fallen-wood bracket before cutting a radial section from upper crust through context and tubes. Measure crust, flesh, each visible tube layer, and total thickness. Perennial brackets may add successive pore layers, but compressed or damaged zones can make age counts unreliable.

Keep a slice that includes the wood interface. Record whether attachment tissue enters bark, sapwood, or an old wound and whether insect galleries or another fungus cross the same area. A section documents local anatomy; it does not map the entire decay column.

## Keep the species-complex boundary visible

The name *F. pinicola* has been applied broadly across the Northern Hemisphere. Regional lineages and revised names mean a photograph from one continent should not be used as a universal reference. Record country, coordinates at an appropriate privacy level, elevation, host evidence, collection date, and voucher repository.

Microscopy should include spores and the tissue system required by the current regional key. Sequence evidence is useful only with a traceable voucher, suitable loci, quality control, and comparison to curated material. A search-engine image match cannot resolve a complex.

## Separate the nearest perennial brackets

Tinder fungus is commonly gray and hoof-shaped without a red resinous belt, though weathered specimens can lose obvious color. Artist's conk often forms broad brown shelves whose pale pore surface marks dark when scratched. Other *Fomitopsis* species can share brown rot, pale pores, and zoned crusts.

Compare host, upper crust, active margin, pore density, context color, tube layering, hyphal system, spores, geography, and current nomenclature. Reject the record at complex level when key characters are missing rather than turning uncertainty into a precise species name.

## Document brown rot without overclaiming cause

Members of the complex are associated with brown rot, which preferentially removes cellulose and leaves brown, cubical, brittle residue. Photograph wood texture at increasing distances from the fruit body and record moisture, cracking, compression, and other decay signs. Do not infer internal extent from the cap's size.

A conk on a dead log is evidence of decomposition, not proof that the fungus killed the original tree. On a standing tree, fruiting may indicate established decay, but hazard depends on location, residual sound wood, crown condition, loading, targets, and species-specific structure. A qualified arborist should evaluate risk in context.

## Run a fixed-zone margin record

Select one accessible bracket on fallen wood and mark three non-damaging photographic zones: old crust, red belt, and pale margin. At regular intervals, record zone width, surface moisture, droplet presence, insect damage, and a color reference under similar light. Do not paint or cut a live margin merely to create landmarks.

The record can show visible seasonal change on that bracket. It cannot convert zone width into a universal age or growth-rate estimate because temperature, wood moisture, genetics, and prior damage differ. Report missing intervals and measurement uncertainty.

## Compare pore density across age zones

On a lawful detached specimen, choose equal-area fields near the newest margin, middle pore surface, and an older zone. Count complete pores intersecting a defined grid and record blocked, torn, or insect-damaged areas separately. Repeat counts before averaging.

This can reveal within-bracket variation and prevent one close-up from standing for the whole hymenium. It does not by itself distinguish species. Preparation, pore stretching on curved surfaces, and senescence must accompany the numbers.

## Make a tube-layer chronology with explicit uncertainty

On a lawful detached bracket from fallen wood, sand or cut one radial face cleanly enough to see stacked tube layers without polishing away boundaries. Photograph with a scale and mark clear, ambiguous, merged, and insect-damaged intervals. Measure layer depth at several points because curvature and partial growth produce uneven bands.

Successive tube layers support perennial growth, but they are not automatically calendar years. Interrupted growth, replacement pore surfaces, weathering, and tissue loss complicate counts. Report a minimum number of visible layers and the uncertain zones instead of assigning an exact age to the conk.

## Separate active margin from a painted-looking color edge

Measure the pale rim and adjacent red-brown belt at the top, side, and lower edge under the same light. Gently inspect whether the pale tissue is soft and actively expanding or old, dry, and abraded. Record droplets, resin sheen, cracks, and algae separately.

This prevents a single bright photograph from turning any red-edged bracket into the complex. Margin activity varies around one fruit body, and an old belt can remain visible after growth slows. Color-zone geometry is supporting evidence that must agree with pores, section, wood, microscopy, and geography.

## Treat extracts and health claims as separate questions

Laboratory studies of fungal compounds do not establish that a wild conk, tea, powder, or supplement treats disease. Identity, extraction method, dose, contaminants, pharmacokinetics, interactions, and clinical endpoints are different evidence layers. This field guide provides no therapeutic recommendation.

Do not ingest a roadside, treated-timber, or uncertain bracket. If an unknown preparation was consumed and symptoms occur, contact local poison control or medical services and preserve the product, packaging, remnants, timing, and amount. Do not delay care while researching a common name.

## Build a reviewable red-belted conk record

1. Photograph the whole wood unit and map every fruit body.
2. Measure brackets and preserve natural color with a reference.
3. Record old crust, red belt, pale margin, and fine pores separately.
4. Section lawful fallen-wood material through attachment and tube layers.
5. Document host evidence and brown-rot texture without assigning tree death.
6. Apply regional microscopy and sequence-supported taxonomy when needed.
7. Keep tree-risk, food, and health conclusions outside unsupported evidence.
