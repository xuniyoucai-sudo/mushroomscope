The **brick cap mushroom** is best approached as a wood-decay evidence problem, not as a cap-color match. The name usually refers to *Hypholoma lateritium*, a ringless mushroom that forms groups on hardwood and develops dark purple-brown spores. Its cap often carries a brick-red center with a paler margin, but rain, age, shade, and camera processing can move that palette from chestnut through orange-brown.

The strongest record combines substrate, developmental changes, gill attachment and color, stem anatomy, spore deposit, season, region, and a deliberate comparison with dangerous wood-growing mushrooms. This page organizes that record. It cannot confirm a specimen for eating.

## Prove the woody attachment first

Photograph the whole stump, log, or tree base before touching the mushrooms. Show bark, exposed wood, decay, cluster position, and surrounding trees. A group that appears to rise from soil may connect to a buried root or branch, so trace one stem base carefully instead of labeling the collection terrestrial from the surface view.

Brick cap is saprotrophic: its mycelium uses dead woody tissue. In many accounts hardwood is the expected substrate. Record a host only when bark, leaves, buds, branching, or other evidence supports it. “Hardwood” is more honest than an invented oak identification, and “buried wood unknown” is more useful than “soil.”

Map the cluster. Note whether stems share a tight base, form several adjacent groups, or run along one buried root. Dense growth is common but not exclusive to *Hypholoma*. The [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps separate visible surface from the actual nutrient source.

## Build a cap series instead of matching one color

Young caps are convex, often with a pale cream to yellowish margin and a warmer orange-red or brick-red center. Fine whitish veil remnants may cling near the margin. Expanded caps become broadly convex or almost flat, and rain can wash the surface while sun and age deepen or dull the center.

Measure several caps and photograph them in the same neutral light. Include a young cap, a mid-stage cap, and a mature cap. Record whether the surface is dry, moist, or recently rained on. A phone’s automatic saturation can make red-brown appear diagnostic when the real collection is much subtler.

Do not reject a plausible group because every cap is not red. Development, exposure, and hydration create variation within one cluster. Conversely, do not accept a mushroom because its center looks exactly like a brick; many unrelated wood fungi use the same autumn palette.

## Follow gills from pale to smoky purple-brown

Turn caps at different stages and photograph the gill-to-stem junction. The gills are attached rather than free. Young gills may look pale gray, cream-gray, or faintly yellowish. As spores mature, they become smoky gray and eventually darken with a purplish-brown cast.

That progression is more useful than one old underside. Greenish-yellow young gills can push the comparison toward sulfur tuft, while rusty cinnamon spores redirect it toward *Galerina* or *Pholiota*. Lighting can distort these differences, so pair photographs with a real deposit.

The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) explains how to record attached, notched, free, and decurrent junctions. Photograph naturally attached gills before cutting; tearing a cap from its stem can create a false gap.

## Make a labeled spore deposit

Use a mature cap across white and dark nonabsorbent surfaces, cover it against drafts, and label it with the collection number. A sufficiently heavy brick cap deposit should be purple-brown to dark purplish brown. A few reflected gill colors or a wet stain are not a print.

The [spore-print method](/identification/how-to-make-spore-print/) gives a repeatable setup. Spore color is particularly important in stump clusters because it separates several high-risk pathways: white-spored *Armillaria*, rusty-brown *Galerina* and many *Pholiota*, and purple-brown *Hypholoma*.

A purple-brown print supports the genus-level direction but does not prove *H. lateritium*. Keep it attached to notes about cap development, gill color, ring absence, host, and region.

## Document the stem and fleeting veil evidence

Stems are often yellowish above and deepen to brown toward the base. Photograph the full length and every side. Brick cap lacks a persistent ring, but young caps may carry fine partial-veil remnants around the margin or an inconspicuous fibrillose zone on the upper stem.

Weather can erase veil traces. Ring absence on one soaked old mushroom is therefore weaker than a series across ages. At the same time, a distinct membranous ring should trigger comparisons with *Galerina*, *Armillaria*, and *Pholiota* rather than being explained away.

Cut one representative stem lengthwise. Record whether it is hollow or stuffed, whether colors change from apex to base, and whether the base joins wood directly. Do not use bitterness as a field test: tasting an unresolved wood-growing mushroom adds risk without replacing anatomy or spores.

## Separate the critical lookalikes

### Sulfur tuft

*Hypholoma fasciculare* can share dense stump clusters and purple-brown spores. It is usually more vividly sulfur-yellow, with young gills that may show yellow-green or olive tones before darkening. Brick cap tends toward a brick-red center and less intensely yellow flesh. These are tendencies, not a two-color switch; weathered material and regional variation require a current key.

### Deadly Galerina

The *Galerina marginata* complex is the safety-critical comparison. It also fruits on wood and may occur in groups. A rusty-brown spore deposit, ring or ring zone, and different microscopic characters distinguish it, but old rain-worn fruit bodies can lose obvious veil structures. Mixed collections can occur on one log. Evaluate each fruit body and never treat “same stump” as “same species.”

### Honey fungus

*Armillaria* can form large clusters from roots, stumps, and tree bases. Its spore deposit is white rather than purple-brown, and many species have a visible ring. The [honey fungus guide](/mushrooms/honey-fungus-mushroom-guide/) also explains why tree-health context and underground roots matter.

### Pholiota and other scaly wood fungi

Many *Pholiota* have rusty-brown spores, conspicuous cap or stem scales, and stronger veil structures. Rain can remove scales, so spore color and microscopic features still matter. A clean smooth cap after a storm is not proof that scales were never present.

## Use microscopy when a species name matters

For a formal record, measure mature spores from a deposit and document mount medium, number measured, and range. Examine cystidia on gill faces and edges, cap-cuticle construction, and veil tissues. Compare these observations with a current key for the collection’s continent.

Taxonomic sources may use the synonym *Hypholoma sublateritium*. Preserve the exact name supplied by a database or voucher and record when it was checked. A well-archived observation can survive nomenclatural change; an unlabeled photograph cannot.

Dry a lawful representative voucher with collection number, date, generalized location, substrate evidence, photographs, print, and notes. Microscopy or DNA should remain connected to that voucher rather than to a loose image of the cluster.

## Interpret fruiting as decay evidence, not a structural diagnosis

Mushrooms show that a fungus has enough established mycelium and suitable conditions to reproduce. They do not reveal the complete boundary or strength of decay inside a stump, root, or tree. Moisture, temperature, and competing organisms can produce fruiting in one year and none in the next.

On natural logs, decomposition returns nutrients and creates habitat. At a living tree base or near structures, species identification does not replace assessment by a qualified arborist or building professional. Record whether wood is dead, attached, buried, or load-bearing, and keep ecological identification separate from hazard evaluation.

## Keep food claims outside the identification shortcut

Regional field guides differ in their treatment of brick cap edibility, and common names can be applied inconsistently. Even a correctly identified collection raises separate questions about freshness, pollution, mixed species, individual intolerance, legality, and preparation. This guide does not answer those questions for a real specimen.

Never assume that prolonged cooking repairs uncertainty. It cannot turn *Galerina* into food, remove environmental contamination, or separate mixed caps after they have been chopped. For symptoms after mushroom exposure, contact emergency services or Poison Control promptly and preserve uncooked material, cooked leftovers, photographs, location, and timing.

## Field record checklist

- Cluster photographed in place with the actual stump, log, root, or buried wood
- Host evidence and living/dead status recorded without guessing
- Caps measured across young, expanding, and mature stages
- Brick-red center, paler margin, moisture, and veil remnants documented
- Gill attachment and progression from pale gray to smoky purple-brown shown
- Full ringless stem and woody base photographed
- Heavy purple-brown spore deposit linked to the collection
- Sulfur tuft, deadly *Galerina*, honey fungus, and *Pholiota* considered explicitly
- Region, date, recent weather, and microscopy limits stated

## Frequently asked questions

### Can brick caps grow from apparently bare ground?

Yes, when roots, chips, or wood are buried. Trace the base gently and describe the actual attachment. Surface soil does not prove a soil-feeding ecology.

### Is the cap always brick red?

No. Young margins may be pale, rain can wash color, and old caps can darken or fade. Use a developmental series and multiple characters.

### Does a purple-brown print rule out deadly Galerina?

It argues against a rusty-spored *Galerina* for the printed cap, but it does not prove that every cap in a mixed cluster is the same. Keep specimens separated and examine each one.

### Why avoid tasting for bitterness?

Taste is subjective and unnecessary for resolving a potentially dangerous wood-growing collection. Substrate, gills, spores, veil anatomy, microscopy, and regional keys provide safer evidence.

### What should be archived with photographs?

Keep measurements, substrate and host evidence, developmental-stage notes, spore deposit, region, weather, key used, and a voucher number when lawful.
