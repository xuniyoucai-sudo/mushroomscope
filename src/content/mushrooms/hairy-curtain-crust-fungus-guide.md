**Hairy curtain crust**, *Stereum hirsutum*, covers dead hardwood with thin, overlapping fans. Fresh growth commonly shows ochre, yellow-orange, tan, and gray zones beneath a fine hairy coating. The underside is smoother and often brighter. That last feature matters most: a colorful, hairy top can resemble several small brackets, but *Stereum* does not have the true pore layer expected in turkey tail. A smooth underside that is distinctly violet-gray follows the separate [silver leaf fungus guide](/mushrooms/silver-leaf-fungus-guide/), including its wound and plant-symptom evidence limits.

This guide is designed for a reviewable wood-decay record. It does not turn a photograph into an identification or an identification into food advice. Preserve the exact branch, complete margins, both surfaces, age range, measurements, microscopy, and regional context.

## Prove the fruit body is attached to one wood unit

Begin with a habitat frame that shows the occupied branch or log from end to end. Record whether the wood is standing or fallen, barked or decorticated, suspended or touching soil, recently cut or deeply softened. Photograph end grain, bark, buds, leaves, and nearby living trees when they genuinely help identify broadleaf wood.

Do not label a host solely because an oak or beech stands nearby. Fallen branches move, crowns overlap, and firewood is transported. If the host is uncertain, describe the substrate as dead broadleaf wood and retain a small lawful wood sample with the fungal voucher. The [habitat documentation guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) separates observed substrate from an inferred tree name.

Map fruit bodies by face of the branch: upper, side, and sheltered underside. A colony can extend from a flat crust into projecting fans where the wood angle changes. That continuity explains apparently different forms without assuming that every nearby crust belongs to the same species.

## Measure thin fans without flattening them

Choose young, mature, and weathered margins. Measure projection from wood, width along attachment, thickness near the edge and base, and the depth of each colored zone. Include a side view with a millimeter scale. The fruit body should be thin and leathery, but a number is more useful than an adjective.

Record whether neighboring fans overlap, fuse laterally, or remain separate. Avoid peeling an entire patch from the branch. One representative section that includes wood, attachment, upper surface, and hymenium provides more evidence than a bag of detached fragments.

Drying can curl margins and exaggerate thickness. Rain can flatten hairs and deepen color. Note recent weather and photograph a persistent colony at comparable angles after wet and dry intervals. Changes within one numbered patch are more informative than unmatched photographs from different sites.

## Audit hairs and color zones under neutral light

Use diffuse daylight and a neutral reference. Photograph the upper surface directly and at low raking light. Fine hairs should catch side light, especially near active pale margins. Describe whether hairs are dense, matted, rubbed away, or colonized by algae. Older zones may appear green because the hairy surface traps moisture and supports algal growth.

Divide the fan into margin, middle, and attachment zones. Record color and hair density for each. Do not raise saturation until faint orange becomes diagnostic. Sun bleaching, algae, dirt, and camera white balance can make one colony span several guidebook palettes.

A hand lens can show whether the surface is truly hairy or merely rough and radially wrinkled. Save an uncropped frame that connects the close-up to its numbered fan. Macro texture without specimen context is easy to misassign.

## Turn over several intact margins and search for pores

The most useful field question is not “Does it look like turkey tail?” but “Does the mature fertile surface contain genuine pores?” Inspect several fresh, intact undersides with a 10× lens. Hairy curtain crust has a smooth to slightly wrinkled hymenium. Turkey tail has a dense field of tiny round or angular openings.

Use side lighting and focus across the surface. Dirt pits, insect damage, droplets, and camera noise can imitate pores. A true pore field repeats consistently and connects to tubes in section. The [pore-surface guide](/identification/mushroom-pore-surface-guide/) explains how to distinguish pores, teeth, folds, and damaged tissue.

Photograph the underside from center to growing margin. Note yellow, ochre, orange, gray, or cracked areas separately. Weathered hymenia may lose their clean color, so absence of yellow on an old fan does not settle the name.

## Compare turkey tail with a surface-first table

Build a comparison with upper hairs, zoning, underside color, pore presence, pore density, thickness, attachment, host, and microscopic evidence. *Trametes versicolor* can be just as zoned and hairy above. Its pale porous underside makes it a polypore; calling every zoned bracket “turkey tail” discards the strongest structural evidence.

Measure pores over a known distance rather than declaring them “tiny.” If no openings are visible on several mature clean margins, cut a narrow radial section. A polypore shows tubes above the pore mouths. A *Stereum* section remains a thin layered crust without that tube layer.

The [turkey tail guide](/mushrooms/turkey-tail-mushroom-guide/) owns the separate species intent and describes its pore checks. Link the two records by the observed contradiction: smooth versus porous, not by color resemblance alone.

## Separate other Stereum and toothed brackets

Other *Stereum* species overlap in hardwood ecology and smooth hymenia. Some form broad resupinate sheets, some develop different hair or color patterns, and several may redden when the fresh fertile surface is scratched. Test bruising only on a small edge, record elapsed time, and include an untouched control. Lack of reddening in old dry tissue is weak evidence.

Violet-toothed polypores may look thin and zoned from above, but the underside matures from pores toward split teeth and can retain violet tones near fresh margins. The [violet-toothed polypore guide](/mushrooms/violet-toothed-polypore-guide/) shows why fertile-surface development should be sampled across ages.

Resupinate crust fungi are diverse and often need microscopy. If the collection is mostly flat, lacks fresh projecting margins, or gives contradictory reactions, keep a broad field label instead of forcing *S. hirsutum*.

## Link microscopy to the photographed fan

Make a small packet from one numbered mature fan and its attachment. Note mount medium, stain, microscope calibration, number of spores measured, full length and width ranges, and structures used by the regional key. Do not paste published measurements into a specimen record.

A spore deposit may be sparse. Suspend a mature fertile surface over clean glass in a humid but ventilated container, retain a blank control, and record orientation and duration. A white residue is meaningful only when it forms beneath the hymenium and microscopy confirms spores rather than wood dust or surface debris.

Sequence data can help with difficult complexes, but a sequence requires a documented locus, quality review, and trustworthy reference. A database name is not a substitute for morphology and voucher provenance.

## Study decay without claiming the fungus killed the tree

Hairy curtain crust decomposes dead hardwood. Finding it on a fallen branch does not prove that it caused branch death. Drought, storm damage, pruning, other fungi, insects, and prior injury may have acted before fruiting became visible.

For a small decay study, divide a branch into equal-length zones and record bark cover, hardness, moisture, ground contact, shade, and fruit-body area. Repeat after comparable rain. Include unoccupied wood on the same branch. The result can describe where visible fruiting concentrates; it cannot reveal the whole mycelium or establish a single cause of decay.

Keep management claims proportional. On structural timber or a valued tree, the visible fungus warrants inspection of wood condition, not an automatic diagnosis from an online picture.

## Keep food and exposure boundaries explicit

The thin, leathery fruit bodies have no useful culinary role in this guide. A matching image cannot exclude a different crust, contamination, or an individual reaction. Do not taste a bracket to complete a field description, and do not use folk medicinal claims as proof of identity, dose, purity, or benefit.

If illness follows ingestion of any wild fungus, contact local emergency or poison-control services promptly. Retain uncooked material, leftovers, photographs, location, amount, preparation, and symptom times. Do not delay care while seeking an online name.

## Build a reviewable hairy-curtain-crust record

1. Photograph the whole occupied wood unit before disturbance.
2. Measure young, mature, and weathered fans in several dimensions.
3. Record hairs and zones under neutral and raking light.
4. Inspect several clean undersides for true pores.
5. Section one margin when surface texture remains ambiguous.
6. Compare other *Stereum* and toothed or porous brackets.
7. Link microscopy and any sequence to the numbered voucher.
8. Keep host, decay, edibility, and causation claims within the evidence.

The durable identification lesson is structural: a hairy zoned top is shared, while a smooth fertile underside redirects the record away from true turkey tail. A useful page makes that contradiction visible instead of rewarding color matching.
