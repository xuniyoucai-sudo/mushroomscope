Jelly baby fungus is an informal name for *Leotia lubrica*, a small woodland ascomycete with an irregular moist head balanced on a paler stem. Its toy-like outline is memorable, but the nickname hides important evidence. The head is a fertile surface rather than a conventional gilled cap, colors shift as tissue wets or dries, and several small clubs occupy the same litter. A useful record joins form, texture, substrate, development, microscopy, region, and a current local key.

## Read the head and stem as separate structures

Photograph an untouched group at soil level. The head is commonly rounded, folded, or lobed rather than symmetrical. Fresh material may be olive-yellow, ochre, or greenish and can reflect light like damp wax. The stem is usually paler and more cylindrical, though it may flatten or bend. Record head width, total height, stem width, and whether the boundary is abrupt or gradual.

Do not force every specimen into one silhouette. Young heads may be nearly globose; mature ones can spread, crease, or sag. Insects and rain produce notches that are not taxonomic lobes. Measure several fruit bodies and retain the range. A cluster showing different ages is stronger evidence than a single unusually photogenic individual.

## Confirm the absence of gills, pores, and teeth

The smooth head carries the spore-producing tissue. There should not be radiating gills, a field of pores, or downward teeth beneath it. Photograph the side and underside transition with a scale. A shadow beneath a folded head can imitate a separate cap surface, so use diffuse light and more than one angle.

This distinction separates *Leotia* from ordinary agarics before color is considered. It also prevents a common caption error in which every stalked fungus is called a mushroom with a cap. The visible architecture is a club-like ascoma with a differentiated fertile head.

## Record texture before it changes

Fresh fruit bodies are flexible and moist, but “gelatinous” is not a standardized measurement. Note whether the head rebounds after the lightest permitted touch, whether its surface is sticky or merely wet, and whether the stem has the same consistency. Do not squeeze every specimen. A sectional view, when lawful and justified, should be made only after complete exterior photography.

Drying can shrink the head, dull olive color, stiffen the stem, and erase translucency. Place collected material in a ventilated container rather than sealed plastic. Record the time between collection and each photograph. Comparisons made several hours apart otherwise confuse handling history with species difference.

## Map soil, litter, moss, and buried wood

Jelly babies are generally recorded from damp woodland ground. Describe the actual emergence point: mineral soil, humus, leaf litter, moss, a bank, or a path edge. Gently tracing one base may reveal fine soil contact, but do not excavate an entire colony. If buried wood is present, record it instead of assuming the fungus is strictly terrestrial.

Use the [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) to separate substrate from nearby-tree guesses. List trees only when bark, leaves, needles, or buds support the record. Canopy type, drainage, slope, recent rain, and litter depth help another observer interpret the site without pretending that proximity proves a biological partnership.

## Build a moisture and color series

Color names alone are unreliable. Photograph the same marked group after rain and again after a dry interval, using a gray card or neutral reference when possible. Keep camera white balance consistent. Record which heads remain olive, which yellow, and which turn brown at damaged edges.

The exercise does not prove identity, but it shows how much variation belongs to one colony. It also limits overconfident comparisons with dark-headed *Leotia* concepts. Saturated phone processing can turn ochre into bright green; save an unedited frame and avoid using enhanced color as diagnostic evidence.

## Separate related Leotia and Cudonia cautiously

Names within this group are not resolved by a single field character everywhere. Material called *Leotia viscosa* is often described with a darker green or blackish head, yet age and moisture blur that boundary. *Cudonia* species may have drier, more clearly cap-like heads and different microscopic features. Small yellow clubs in other ascomycete groups can add further confusion.

Compare ascus and spore form, dimensions, septation, tissue construction, ecology, and regional literature. A field label such as “*Leotia* species” can be more accurate than a confident species name based only on yellow color. Retain mature material and precise locality data if expert study is planned.

## Use microscopy as specimen-linked evidence

Microscopy should be tied to the photographed fruit body, not to loose fragments from a mixed collection. Make mounts from a documented part of the fertile head. Record mounting medium, magnification, calibration, number of spores measured, and whether spores came from a mature ascus or free tissue.

Report a range rather than selecting one convenient measurement. Immature spores and collapsed material can distort dimensions. A white deposit may be hard to see, so failed printing does not establish immaturity or a different species. Keep slides, notes, and voucher code connected.

## Follow a small woodland plot through time

Mark a non-destructive 25-centimeter plot around a group. Count newly emerged, mature, damaged, and collapsed fruit bodies on repeat visits. Record soil-surface moisture, rainfall interval, litter disturbance, and approximate moss cover. Photograph from the same height and direction.

This produces a developmental record rather than a claim that one rainy day “caused” fruiting. Underground mycelium, temperature, moisture history, and microtopography all contribute. The plot can also reveal how quickly soft structures disappear, explaining why absence on a later visit does not mean the organism left the site.

## Keep ecological claims proportional

A fruiting body proves that the fungus produced an ascoma at that point. It does not by itself identify its full nutritional relationship or underground extent. Avoid claiming that it grows “on moss” when the base enters soil beneath moss, or that it is linked to one tree simply because that tree dominates the photograph.

Leave most fruit bodies in place. Small soft fungi are easily destroyed by repeated handling, and their site can support invertebrates and other decomposers. Where collecting is allowed, one well-documented mature specimen is more useful than a bag of unlabelled clubs.

## Food and poisoning boundaries

This page does not recommend jelly baby fungus as food. Small size, soft texture, and lack of a dramatic warning color do not establish safety. A photograph cannot exclude lookalikes, contamination, spoilage, or individual reaction. Never taste an unknown specimen as an identification test.

If symptoms follow any wild-mushroom exposure, contact local emergency or poison-control services promptly. Preserve uncooked material, trimmings, photographs, packaging, meal details, and timing. Do not wait for an online identification or induce vomiting unless a clinician instructs you to do so. The [identification safety checklist](/identification/mushroom-identification-safety-checklist/) provides a conservative evidence workflow.

## A field record that can be checked

Give the observation a unique code. Photograph the whole group, head profiles, smooth fertile surface, stem, bases, habitat, and a scale. Record date, locality at an appropriate privacy level, elevation, recent weather, substrate layers, nearby verified trees, odor without tasting, and handling time. Separate visible facts from conclusions.

If you collect legally, keep one fruit body intact and section another longitudinally. Dry material gently with its label. A useful voucher connects field images, notes, microscopy, and repository location. Uploading a cropped head without its stem and habitat discards the evidence most likely to correct a mistaken name.

## Distinguish a colony from a mixed microhabitat

Small woodland fungi often fruit centimeters apart. Before treating every yellow club in one frame as a developmental stage, compare head texture, stem color, attachment depth, and damage pattern specimen by specimen. Give a separate field number to a neighboring structure that is drier, branched, cup-shaped, or attached to wood. Do not combine it in the same envelope merely because the camera view made one cluster.

Map each fruit body on a simple grid and preserve the map with the voucher. If microscopy later shows two taxa, the grid reconnects measurements and photographs to their original positions. This modest step prevents a mixed collection from producing an impossible range of spores or a misleading description of color change.
