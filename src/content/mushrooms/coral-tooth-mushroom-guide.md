## Coral tooth is a branching tooth fungus, not a coral mushroom

*Hericium coralloides* produces an unusually architectural fruit body. A fresh specimen is white to cream and divides repeatedly into an open framework. Short teeth hang from many branch surfaces. Those teeth, not gills or pores, form the spore-bearing layer. Branching, teeth, and direct hardwood attachment establish the useful starting concept.

The common name can mislead. “Coral mushroom” is used for unrelated branching fungi, especially *Ramaria* and *Clavulina*. Those fungi do not become *Hericium* because their silhouette resembles coral. A young or damaged coral tooth may not yet show every expected character, either. Identification must follow structures, ecology, region, and developmental stage rather than one name or photograph.

This page is a species guide and documentation aid. It cannot authenticate a specimen or decide whether it is edible. If food use is contemplated, obtain in-person confirmation from someone qualified to evaluate the intact collection locally.

## Read the fruit body from attachment to tooth tips

Begin at the wood. Coral tooth has no conventional cap and stem. Its branches arise from a shared base attached to dead or declining hardwood. Photograph that base before moving anything. A specimen apparently growing from soil may connect to buried wood, but aggressive digging destroys evidence and habitat.

Follow the framework outward. Mature branches divide repeatedly and leave open spaces rather than merging into one dense pom-pom. Examine their undersides and sides for fine downward teeth. Tooth length and distribution change during development, so compare several areas instead of selecting the neatest tip. Natural-light close-ups are more useful than a saturated phone image.

Fresh tissue is generally white to cream. Age, handling, dry weather, insects, or bacterial deterioration can introduce yellow, tan, or brown zones. Discoloration is a condition record, not a species verdict. Note texture and odor without tasting. Softening, slime, widespread browning, or a sour or putrid odor should end food consideration even after identification.

The spore deposit is white, but that has low resolving power because several tooth fungi also have pale spores. The [spore-print method](/identification/how-to-make-spore-print/) records one character; it never confirms this species alone.

## Separate coral tooth from other Hericium forms

### Lion's mane forms a compact mass

Lion's mane, *Hericium erinaceus*, usually develops as a compact, largely unbranched body with conspicuous long hanging spines. Coral tooth presents a more open repeated branching system, with shorter teeth distributed along branches. The distinction is straightforward in ideal mature examples but weaker in young, weathered, or malformed fruit bodies. The [lion's mane species guide](/mushrooms/lion-s-mane-mushroom-guide/) covers that species' separate cultivated and evidence intent.

### Other branched Hericium require regional interpretation

Names applied to branched *Hericium* have not always been used consistently across continents and older field guides. *H. americanum* is common in North American comparisons, while European and Asian literature may organize records differently. Branch density, tooth placement and length, host, spores, hyphal characters, and geography can matter. A current regional key is more defensible than transferring a name from a photograph posted in another country.

### Soil corals lack a tooth-bearing surface

Species of *Ramaria*, *Clavulina*, and other coral-like genera can be repeatedly branched and pale. Their fertile surface is not made of the hanging teeth seen in *Hericium*. Many arise from soil or litter, although substrate impressions can deceive. Documenting the attachment, fertile surface, and nearby wood prevents silhouette-only comparison.

## Hardwood condition explains where fruit bodies appear

Coral tooth is a wood-decay fungus associated with dead or declining broadleaf wood, including fallen trunks, large branches, and logs. Host reports include beech, maple, and other hardwoods, but no host list is universal: tree communities, naming practice, and sampling vary by region. Identify the tree independently where possible from bark, leaves, buds, and stand context.

Record whether wood is standing or fallen, barked or bare, solid or advanced in decay, shaded or exposed. Note whether the fungus emerges from a wound, broken end, underside, or buried portion. These details remain useful if the fungal name later changes. GBIF provides occurrence context, but mapped points inherit the quality and historical names of their source datasets.

Dead wood is habitat for insects, mosses, fungi, and cavity-dependent organisms. Avoid peeling bark or breaking logs to search for more. Where collecting is permitted, take only what a documented purpose needs and leave representative material to mature and release spores.

## Season follows local weather rather than a fixed calendar

Temperate records often cluster in late summer and autumn, when moisture and temperature align, but fruiting shifts with latitude, elevation, rainfall, and the log's thermal behavior. A month list copied between regions is weaker than local dated observations. Record recent rainfall, temperatures, canopy exposure, and wood condition with the date.

Repeat observations are especially useful. Photographing the same fruit body from a safe distance over several days can show tooth development, color change, insect damage, and collapse. Do not repeatedly handle it just to create a time series.

Phenology records become more comparable when observation effort is recorded too. A ten-minute trail walk cannot be compared directly with a three-hour survey across several stands. Note the distance searched, number of suitable logs examined, and whether the site was visited after the same weather event. An absence record is meaningful only when method and habitat opportunity are visible.

The fruit body is temporary evidence of a longer wood-decay process. It does not reveal how far mycelium extends, when colonization began, or whether another fungus occupies adjacent wood. Avoid turning one fruiting into a precise claim about tree death or structural stability. On a standing tree near people or property, document the location and seek a qualified arborist's assessment; the image is not a remote hazard rating.

## Food reports do not clear a wild collection

Coral tooth has a food-use reputation, particularly while young and fresh, but edibility statements assume correct identification and legal collection from an uncontaminated site. A roadside log, treated timber, industrial property, sprayed landscape, or flood-debris pile adds exposure questions cooking cannot resolve. A known edible species can also spoil.

If qualified local expertise confirms a legal collection, retain an uncooked portion and photographs, cook it thoroughly, and begin with a modest portion. Allergy or gastrointestinal intolerance remains possible. Never serve a first-time wild collection to someone unable to communicate symptoms clearly. The FDA source below supplies general produce hygiene, not wild-mushroom certification.

For symptoms after an unidentified or wild mushroom, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Preserve uncooked material, trimmings, packaging, photographs, location, and the time eaten. Do not wait for social-media identification or induce vomiting unless a medical professional directs it. NAMA's syndrome overview explains why symptom timing helps responders but is not a home diagnostic tool.

## Cultivation starts with authenticated culture

Some *Hericium* strains grow on sterilized hardwood-based blocks, but “grows like lion's mane” is not a complete method. Strain, substrate, sterilization, gas exchange, colonization temperature, humidity, fresh air, and harvest timing interact. A culture supplier's protocol is safer than universal numbers copied from another species or system.

Label species and supplier lot, separate clean inoculation from fruiting and waste areas, and record dates and abnormalities. Unexpected green, black, orange, or pink growth, persistent foul odor, slime, or unidentified fruit bodies are reasons to isolate and discard a food block. Do not clone an uncertain wild collection for eating; cultivation reproduces the wrong organism as readily as the intended one.

Compare batches by changing one variable at a time. Record substrate dry weight and water addition, inoculation percentage, container type, filter specification, colonization time, pin-set position, room readings, and harvest mass. One successful block does not establish an optimal recipe, while repeated failures cannot be diagnosed from room humidity alone. Sensor placement, calibration drift, internal block temperature, and carbon-dioxide accumulation may separate recorded conditions from those at the fruit body.

Harvest before teeth discolor or the structure collapses, using clean tools and cooling promptly if intended as food. Cultivation notes show what happened to one authenticated batch; they do not prove that a visually similar wild collection is the same organism.

## Build a record another person can audit

Use the [mushroom photo checklist](/identification/mushroom-photo-checklist/) to capture habitat, attachment, overall form, branch junctions, and tooth close-ups in natural light. Add measurements from several branches, a written color description, host evidence, odor, date, weather, and region. If lawful, preserve a small dried voucher linked to the field notes.

Compare the complete record with a current regional key and local occurrence data. Image recognition can assemble candidates, but cannot inspect microscopy or repair missing habitat evidence. The [identification safety checklist](/identification/mushroom-identification-safety-checklist/) explains why agreement among observations is still not remote food clearance.
