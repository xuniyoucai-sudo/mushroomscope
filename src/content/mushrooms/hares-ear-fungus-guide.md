Hare’s ear fungus turns a simple common-name resemblance into a demanding identification problem. The fruit body often stands upright like a narrow ear, with one side split nearly to the base. That architecture is a useful entry point, but several *Otidea* species overlap in color and form, and old, crowded, or damaged cups can lose their most familiar outline.

The name in this guide refers to *Otidea onotica*. A defensible record combines cup geometry, both surfaces, complete base, grouping, substrate, maturity, region, and microscopic characters. The page does not treat a yellow ear shape as permission to eat a wild fungus.

## Start with the group before isolating one cup

Photograph the entire patch from above and from soil level. Mark whether fruit bodies are solitary, loosely grouped, or crowded from a common area. Include an undisturbed frame showing litter type, moss, bare soil, roots, and nearby trees. Closely packed cups can deform one another, so group structure helps explain shapes that look atypical in isolation.

Assign a field number to several cups across the size range. Measure total height, maximum width, cup depth, slit length, and distance between neighbors. Record whether the cup stands upright, leans, curls inward, or has collapsed after rain. These observations separate developmental change from species claims.

## Describe an ear without relying on resemblance

An ear-shaped cup is asymmetrical, elongated, and open along one side. Replace “looks like an ear” with measurable geometry: height-to-width ratio, how far the slit reaches toward the base, whether the two margins overlap, and whether the apex is rounded or pointed.

Young cups may be narrow and nearly closed. Expanding cups reveal more of the inner surface, while older specimens can spread, split further, or become irregular plates. Photograph at least one cup in profile and one looking into the opening. A single frontal image makes a broad cup and a narrow folded cup appear deceptively similar.

## Compare inner and outer surfaces

The inner face is the fertile hymenium. It is generally smoother and may be warmer yellow, ochre, buff, or faintly pink compared with the exterior. The outside is often paler and can look finely granular or downy through a hand lens. Record these surfaces under the same light and white balance.

Do not create false texture with aggressive sharpening. Include a scale and a neutral color reference when practical. If the inner face is wet and glossy, note the rain or condensation rather than interpreting shine as a stable character. If soil has splashed across the outer wall, photograph a clean upper section before handling.

## Expose the base without turning it into a stem

Hare’s ear narrows into a short attachment, sometimes partly buried in litter and humus. It does not have a conventional cap balanced on a long stem. Brush aside only loose material and photograph how the cup enters the substrate. Do not pull several fruit bodies merely to find a cleaner base.

Record whether adjacent cups appear fused, simply crowded, or separated below the surface. A section through one lawful representative can show the thin continuous cup wall and narrowed base. Keep the section linked to the field photograph; detached fragments cannot preserve orientation.

## Test the substrate description

“Woodland floor” hides useful information. Record leaf species where recognizable, litter depth, moss cover, mineral soil exposure, compaction, drainage, slope, and distance to a path or decaying wood. Photograph roots or buried woody fragments without assuming the fungus grows from them.

Nearby trees provide context, not automatic host proof. List the trees observed within a defined radius and state whether roots were visible. Repeated fruiting at the same microsite can be mapped across seasons, but a recurrence does not by itself establish mycorrhizal or saprotrophic function.

## Separate orange peel fungus by posture and ground

The [orange peel fungus guide](/mushrooms/orange-peel-fungus-guide/) covers *Aleuria aurantia*, which commonly forms bright orange shallow cups, saucers, or flattened torn patches on disturbed bare soil. Hare’s ear is usually more upright, buff-yellow, elongated, and split along one side in woodland litter.

The boundary is not “yellow versus orange.” Weather, cameras, and maturity shift color. Compare cup posture, slit, outer surface, substrate profile, grouping, and spores. A damaged *Otidea* can flatten, while a young *Aleuria* can form a deep cup, so preserve several stages rather than selecting one idealized specimen.

## Separate scarlet cups by color and attachment

Scarlet elf cups have a vivid red fertile interior, paler exterior, and a relationship with buried or exposed dead wood. Their cup is broader and typically lacks the long unilateral ear slit expected here. The [scarlet elf cup guide](/mushrooms/scarlet-elf-cup-guide/) provides an explicit wood-attachment workflow.

Trace substrate carefully. A cup apparently on soil may connect to a buried twig, and a woodland photograph may contain unrelated sticks. Color attracts attention, but attachment and architecture determine which comparison deserves further work.

## Separate saddle fungi by stem architecture

A white saddle fungus carries folded lobes on a conspicuously ribbed, fluted stem. Hare’s ear is a cup that narrows directly into a short base. The [white saddle guide](/mushrooms/white-saddle-fungus-guide/) shows the chambered stem section and cap-stem relationship that distinguish that body plan.

This comparison is especially useful for pale damaged material. Ask whether the visible folds belong to one cup wall or to a cap supported above the ground. Photograph the complete lower half before trimming it. Missing bases cause avoidable category errors.

## Treat the Otidea name as a testable hypothesis

Species in *Otidea* can overlap in yellow, buff, brown, or pinkish colors. Names in older regional books may not reflect later microscopic or molecular revisions. Preserve the source, publication date, region, and characters used rather than copying a label without its evidence.

When a current key is unavailable or the material is immature, report *Otidea* species or an ear-fungus group. A provisional name attached to a complete record is scientifically stronger than a precise name attached to one cropped photograph.

## Measure mature spores with a documented method

Collect microscopic material from a mature numbered cup. Record whether spores were naturally discharged or removed from tissue, the mount, stain, calibration, magnification, and number measured. Report length and width ranges, shape, internal droplets where visible, surface ornamentation, and any paraphysis characters the regional key uses.

Do not mix measurements from adjacent cups unless each has its own number. A clustered patch can contain more than one cup fungus. Immature tissue may yield undersized or incomplete spores, while old wet material can be contaminated. Microscopy must remain traceable to the photographed fruit body.

## Build a developmental series

Select a small fixed plot and photograph it from the same position after rainfall intervals. Without moving the fungi, record new cups, height, opening width, slit extension, collapse, insect damage, and disappearance. A transparent scale placed beside rather than on a cup reduces disturbance.

The series can answer whether apparently different shapes are stages of one local population. It cannot prove that every cup belongs to the same genetic individual or species. That boundary should be stated in the observation notes.

## Keep collection proportional to the question

Photography, measurements, and one mature voucher are often enough for a reviewable record. Before collecting, check land rules and whether the population can tolerate removal. Avoid stripping a small patch simply because cups are fragile and convenient to gather.

Dry a voucher promptly, preserving its field number and a note about fresh colors. Thin cups shrink and twist, so fresh photographs are essential. Store location at an appropriate privacy level, habitat notes, microscopy, identifier comments, and any name revisions with the specimen record.

## Do not turn uncertainty into a cooking experiment

This page classifies hare’s ear conservatively as inedible. Even if a regional source reports a historical use, that does not verify the identity, freshness, contamination, or safety of a particular collection. Small brittle cup fungi also offer little reason to accept an avoidable identification risk.

Never taste an unknown cup fungus to identify it. If someone has eaten a wild fungus and symptoms or uncertainty exist, contact local emergency services or a poison center. Retain uncooked material, photographs, preparation details, amount, and time of ingestion.

1. Photograph the whole patch before touching litter.
2. Number young, mature, and old cups across the shape range.
3. Capture inner surface, outer surface, slit, margin, and complete base.
4. Measure height, width, depth, slit length, and grouping distance.

This method produces information another person can check. It also prevents the ear metaphor from replacing the anatomical and ecological evidence required for a species-level determination.

## Frequently asked field questions

### Are the cups always yellow?

No. Fresh color can range through lemon-yellow, ochre, tan, buff, and faint pinkish tones, then fade or darken with age and moisture. Use color with structure and microscopy.

### Does every fruit body have a long side slit?

The slit is characteristic of the ear-like form, but its apparent length changes with development, crowding, damage, and camera angle. Photograph several intact cups from the side.

### Can a patch contain multiple species?

Yes. Similar cup fungi can share a microsite. Keep each measured or sampled cup tied to its own field number and do not pool spores without evidence.

### Why is a low side photograph important?

It shows posture, cup depth, slit, base, and substrate contact. An overhead view compresses all of those into a colored outline and can hide the character that separates an ear cup from a flat saucer.

### What label is appropriate without microscopy?

Use the strongest supported level, such as *Otidea* species or hare’s-ear group, and list the missing evidence. A provisional genus-level record remains useful for phenology and habitat observations.
