**Stump puffball**, *Apioperdon pyriforme* (widely published under *Lycoperdon pyriforme*), is a pear-shaped puffball that commonly fruits in crowded groups from decaying wood. Its most useful starting clue is not the outline but the attachment: a real record connects each fruit body to a stump, log, buried root, or woody fragment.

This page is distinct from the [common puffball guide](/mushrooms/common-puffball-mushroom-guide/), which follows a usually soil-growing, prominently spiny species, and the [pestle puffball guide](/mushrooms/pestle-puffball-guide/), which covers a larger elongated form with a conspicuous sterile base. The goal is a traceable wood-decay record, never food permission from a photograph.

## Prove the cluster is attached to wood

Photograph the colony before touching it. Include the entire stump or log surface, the cluster edge, nearby bark, exposed decay, and enough habitat to show whether the wood is standing, fallen, or buried. Count fruit bodies by size class and map whether they emerge from cracks, cut faces, bark seams, or soil overlying roots.

“On the ground” is not an adequate substrate description. Follow one cluster downward with a narrow excavation. Buried roots and decomposed boards can disappear under a thin mineral or litter layer. Photograph the continuous connection between the narrowed base, mycelial tissue, and recognizable wood fibers.

Record wood diameter, decay class, bark persistence, hardness, moisture, and any defensible host clues. Do not name the tree from one weathered bark fragment. The fungus is saprotrophic; a nearby living tree does not prove a living-host relationship.

## Measure shape without trusting the common name

Number young, middle-aged, and mature fruit bodies. Measure maximum height, width, the width of the narrowed base, and the ratio of fertile upper body to sterile base. Crowding can flatten one side, create angular shoulders, or make a nominally pear-shaped fruit body look round.

Photograph each numbered body in profile and from above. Young specimens are often cream to pale buff; mature ones become ochre, tan, or brown. Record color in consistent diffuse light and distinguish true surface pigmentation from mud, algae, leaf stain, and spore dust transferred by an older neighbor.

The surface is finely granular or minutely spiny rather than covered with the larger detachable pyramidal spines typical of common puffball. Use raking side light and a millimeter scale. Rubbing the surface before photography destroys one of the comparison features.

## Section every developmental stage vertically

Cut each selected specimen from apex through its base with a clean blade. An immature puffball should show a continuous, uniform white interior without an outlined cap, gills, or stem. This section is a structural record: it distinguishes an internal spore mass from a young enclosed agaric, but it does not by itself prove the species.

As stump puffballs mature, the fertile gleba shifts from white through yellowish or olive stages to dry olive-brown powder. The lower sterile tissue can remain firmer and differently textured. Record the boundary height, color, moisture, cavities, insect damage, and whether maturation is even across the cluster.

Never infer the interior of uncut neighbors from one white specimen. Mixed fruitings occur, and age varies within a colony. A dark, marbled, or structured interior belongs on a different risk path, especially when earthballs or unopened gilled mushrooms are possible.

## Track how the apical pore forms

Young fruit bodies lack an open mouth. With maturity, tissue at the apex thins and breaks into an ostiole through which spore powder escapes. Photograph the apex square-on at daily intervals where access is permitted. Measure opening diameter and note whether the margin is torn, round, or enlarged by weather and animals.

Do not squeeze an unknown fruit body toward the face. Mature spores and substrate dust can irritate airways, and force destroys the natural release evidence. A remote camera can record raindrop impacts or gentle branch movement without manufacturing a theatrical cloud.

Link the pore to the sectioned interior. A hole made by insects or physical damage does not prove mature puffball release. The relevant chain is an apical opening above a dry powdery gleba in a correctly constructed fruit body attached to decaying wood.

## Separate spores from a cap-style print

Puffballs do not make a gilled-mushroom cap print. Collect mature powder only where legal and safe, using a clean container tied to the specimen number. Record powder color under neutral light and avoid mixing it with brown rotten wood dust.

The [spore-color guide](/identification/mushroom-spore-colors/) explains why deposit color is one character rather than a verdict. For difficult material, microscopy can compare spore dimensions, ornamentation, and capillitium, but measurements require calibrated optics, mature material, and a preserved voucher.

Report a wet, compacted interior honestly. Moist olive tissue may not release a visible cloud even when maturation has started. Drying a sample can support examination, but it changes texture and cannot recreate the condition in which it was found.

## Compare common puffball and pestle puffball

Common puffball often grows on soil and bears conspicuous detachable spines, which can leave a reticulate scar pattern. Stump puffball is more consistently tied to wood, frequently crowded, and more finely granular. A common puffball beside a buried root is still possible, so verify both surface and substrate.

Pestle puffball is generally taller and larger, with an elongated sterile base that can persist after the upper spore-bearing portion breaks down. It tends to occur on soil, leaf litter, or grassland rather than forming dense colonies directly on a stump. Compare measured proportions instead of selecting specimens that best match their names.

Small earthballs may occupy woody or disturbed sites. Their tougher rind and early dark interior redirect the identification immediately. Do not use the vague label “puffball” until a vertical section rules out internal gill development and documents the gleba state.

## Reconstruct decay ecology over time

Mark a fixed photo point and revisit the wood after rain cycles. Record first emergence, expansion, browning, pore opening, collapse, and the persistence of sterile bases. Measure wood moisture and hardness at the cluster and at an uncolonized comparison zone without damaging the main fruiting area.

One fruiting does not quantify decay rate or prove that stump puffball caused every visible feature of the wood. Multiple fungi and invertebrates share a log. White mycelial cords, softened fibers, and fruit bodies support an association; culture, microscopy, or sequence-linked sampling is needed for stronger attribution.

Keep intervention minimal. Peeling bark or breaking the stump to find more mycelium changes moisture and can destroy future observations. A repeatable surface map usually adds more ecological value than an uncontrolled destructive search.

## Handle naming and distribution carefully

Many reliable sources use *Lycoperdon pyriforme*, while current treatments may use *Apioperdon pyriforme*. Record the accepted name and synonym attached to each source rather than assuming every old record has been re-evaluated. Search both names when reviewing literature and collections.

Occurrence portals are starting points. Check voucher institution, identifier, date, coordinates, substrate notes, and taxonomic backbone. Photo records that omit wood attachment or sectioned interiors cannot carry the same weight as preserved, reviewable material.

Regional range statements should reflect the evidence actually reviewed. Similar pear-shaped wood puffballs and changing generic concepts make a global-looking map less certain than its dots suggest.

## Keep eating claims outside identification

Some sources discuss young white puffballs as food. That rule is unsafe when detached from complete identification, vertical sectioning of every specimen, local expertise, site contamination, freshness, and individual reaction. A white interior is a necessary observation for an immature puffball hypothesis, not remote food clearance.

Decaying urban timber may contain treatments, paint, heavy contamination, or animal waste. Cooking does not repair a wrong identification or remove every environmental hazard. MushroomScope therefore gives no harvesting or preparation instruction for this wild species.

If illness follows ingestion, retain uncooked and cooked remains, packaging, photographs, and meal timing. Contact local poison-control or emergency medical services promptly and do not induce vomiting unless directed by a professional.

## Build a reviewable stump-puffball record

1. Map the whole cluster before disturbance.
2. Prove continuous attachment to stump, log, buried root, or woody debris.
3. Measure young, mature, crowded, and isolated fruit-body proportions.
4. Photograph granular surface texture before handling.
5. Vertically section every specimen used in a safety-relevant comparison.
6. Link apical pore development to a dry mature gleba.
7. Compare common puffball, pestle puffball, earthballs, and unopened agarics.
8. Preserve a voucher where legal and state uncertainty explicitly.
