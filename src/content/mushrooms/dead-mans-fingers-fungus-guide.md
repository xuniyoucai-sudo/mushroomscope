Dead man’s fingers is a memorable common name for a developmental series. Young stromata may be gray-white and powdery, while mature ones become black, hard, and finely dotted. Without that time dimension, observers often assume the pale and black structures are different fungi.

The visible clubs are stromata: organized tissues containing or supporting reproductive structures. This guide explains their stages, wood origin, perithecia, microscopy, and tree-assessment boundary without turning a horror-themed resemblance into the identification.

## Map the cluster before selecting a specimen

Photograph the whole stump, log, root zone, or wood fragment. Record whether clubs form a tight group, line, fused mass, or scattered emergence. Measure the tallest and shortest stromata and include the surrounding substrate.

The human-finger analogy exaggerates scale and regularity. Real stromata can be only a few centimeters tall, bent, flattened, branched, or fused.

## Follow the young conidial stage

Developing clubs often carry a pale gray or whitish powdery surface. This layer produces asexual propagules and can be uneven, leaving darker bases or patches. Rain and handling remove it.

Photograph the powder without blowing or rubbing it. A pale tip is a developmental feature, not mold growing on top of a separate black fungus.

## Recognize the mature black stroma

As sexual structures develop, the surface darkens to charcoal or black and becomes firmer. Small bumps or dots can mark perithecial openings. The interior remains organized tissue rather than the hollow spongy column of a stinkhorn.

Section one legal mature stroma lengthwise. Photograph the dark rind, paler interior, and tiny embedded cavities. Avoid interpreting insect tunnels as perithecia.

## Understand perithecia and ascospore release

Perithecia are flask-shaped chambers beneath the surface. Each contains asci that produce sexual spores, which exit through small openings. This differs from exposed gills, pores, or a gleba smeared on a stinkhorn.

Mature ascospores can be measured microscopically. Record color, dimensions, germ slit where relevant, mounting medium, calibration, and number examined.

## Trace apparent soil growth to buried wood

Clubs at the base of a tree may arise from a buried root, stump, or wood fragment. Gently expose only enough substrate to document attachment. Widespread excavation damages roots and destroys the decay context.

The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps distinguish soil-fruiting earth tongues from wood-origin *Xylaria*.

## Record hardwood identity conservatively

Identify the substrate from bark, wood anatomy, attached leaves or twigs, and site history when possible. A rotten stump may be too decomposed for a confident host name. “Decaying hardwood” is better than an invented oak record.

Keep host confidence separate from fungal confidence. One can be strong while the other remains broad.

## Separate decomposition from tree-risk prediction

*Xylaria* on a stump or dead root indicates use of woody material. On a living tree, visible stromata do not reveal the full decay column, root plate, residual wall, loading, or likelihood of failure. The USDA Wood Handbook explains why strength depends on species, grain, defects, moisture, and loading.

For a tree near people or property, consult a qualified arborist. Do not cut roots, inject chemicals, or remove the fungus as a substitute for assessment.

## Compare X. longipes with proportions and host

*Xylaria longipes* is often more slender and has a clearer sterile stalk, with host records that may emphasize sycamore or maple in some regions. However, proportions overlap, and old literature may use names differently.

Record total height, fertile length, width, branching, host, and spores. A long club is not automatically *X. longipes*.

## Compare candle-snuff fungus

*X. hypoxylon* often forms slimmer branched or antler-like stromata with pale powdery tips. Mature black bases can occur beside younger white branches. Its general color sequence overlaps but architecture is usually different.

Search for multiple stages and avoid identifying fragments. Microscopy and regional keys complete the comparison.

## Separate earth tongues from wood clubs

Species of *Geoglossum*, *Trichoglossum*, and related earth tongues tend to fruit from soil, grassland, or moss and have different tissue and spore characters. Their black clubs may look similar in a cropped photograph.

Actual attachment is decisive for routing the key. Do not place a detached specimen on wood for photography.

## Avoid confusion with finger-like molds and roots

Slime molds, plant roots, charred wood, and insect structures can produce dark projections. Look for repeated organized stromata, developmental stages, perithecial texture, and internal fungal tissue.

An image classifier may respond strongly to the silhouette while ignoring substrate and section. Treat its result as a hypothesis.

## Build a developmental time series

1. Photograph the colony and exact wood origin.
2. Mark a few stromata without touching them.
3. Record pale conidial coverage and dimensions.
4. Repeat images through darkening and perithecial development.
5. Section one mature legal specimen.
6. Collect mature spores for calibrated microscopy.
7. Compare host, shape, anatomy, and spores with a regional key.

The [season guide](/identification/mushroom-season-guide/) explains why persistent fruit bodies should be labeled by condition, not merely date.

## Preserve carbonaceous tissue properly

Dry stromata thoroughly and keep them with the substrate note and collection number. Their hard exterior can conceal internal moisture. A sealed damp sample may mold and lose diagnostic surfaces.

Store photographs of young and mature stages together. A mature voucher alone cannot reconstruct the earlier conidial appearance.

## Test maturity before investing in measurements

Not every black stroma contains mature ascospores. Examine several perithecia from a minimal section and record whether asci and pigmented spores are present. Measurements from immature hyaline spores should not be compared with a key written for fully mature material. A failed collection may need a later revisit rather than more pressure on the microscope.

When discharge is occurring, a clean slide positioned near—not pressed against—the stroma can collect material while preserving surface structure. Document duration, humidity, and which stroma supplied the deposit. Keep contaminating soil and conidial powder separate from the mature ascospore preparation.

## Map wood position below the surface

Where clubs arise along a buried root line, mark the visible distribution without trenching. Site plans, old stump records, and shallow non-destructive observation may explain the pattern. A line of stromata does not prove that a root is continuously decayed or mechanically ineffective.

In parks and gardens, note grinding, soil addition, irrigation, construction, and prior tree removal. These changes can bury woody material and create apparent soil fruiting years later. The record should separate known site history from inference.

## Keep conservation and curation proportional

Common-looking black clubs may still document an unusual host, date, or regional taxon. Consult a recorder before collecting heavily. One mature stroma, one young stage where abundant, and representative wood can be more useful than the whole cluster.

If sequencing is planned, use clean internal tissue and preserve a separate morphological voucher. A sequence without photographs and anatomy can be difficult to audit, while a name based only on shape may conceal a species complex.

## Do not infer medicinal use from metabolite research

Laboratory studies of *Xylaria* cultures or isolated compounds can address chemistry and biological activity under defined conditions. They do not demonstrate that chewing a wild stroma treats disease. Species identity, strain, culture medium, purification, dose, toxicity, and human trials are separate steps.

This page makes no supplement, wound-care, antimicrobial, cancer, or veterinary recommendation.

## Keep ingestion outside the guide

Stromata are tough, wood-associated, and not presented as food. Substrate may include pollutants, treated wood, animal waste, or unknown landscape chemicals. Similar black clubs and unresolved species add further uncertainty.

If ingestion causes symptoms, contact local emergency services or a poison center and retain specimens, photographs, timing, and exposure details.

## Culture work requires verification

An isolate from a black stroma can be useful for research, but fast-growing contaminants may overtake plates. Link culture to a voucher, use clean transfers, maintain controls, and verify identity molecularly or through authenticated fruiting where appropriate.

Do not release research cultures onto landscape trees or claim a metabolite profile from visual plate morphology.

## Observe without damaging roots and wood

Leave most stromata in place for maturation and wildlife interactions. Avoid stripping bark or excavating structural roots. Use repeat photography and a minimal voucher strategy.

Where the substrate is a heritage tree, coordinate sampling with the land manager and arborist. Scientific curiosity does not override tree care or site rules.

## Frequently asked field questions

### Are pale and black clubs the same age?

Usually they represent different developmental states, though weather can leave patchy pale coatings on otherwise darkening stromata.

### Can a club branch?

Yes. Shape is variable, and adjacent stromata may also fuse. Record the base before deciding whether one structure branched.

### Are the dots on the surface pores?

They are openings associated with embedded perithecia, not a polypore tube layer.

### Does the fungus grow on conifers?

The focal species is principally associated with hardwood in common descriptions. An unusual substrate record requires careful host and species verification.

### Can dead stromata persist into winter?

Yes. Hard black tissue may remain after active spore production, so condition should accompany occurrence dates.
