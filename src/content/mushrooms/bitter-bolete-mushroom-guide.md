Bitter bolete, *Tylopilus felleus*, is a classic porcini lookalike because a young specimen can combine a brown cap, a thick pale stem, and a nearly white pore surface. The resemblance weakens as spores mature: the pores often take on a flesh-pink cast, and the raised net on the stem is commonly darker and coarser than the fine pale reticulation expected on many porcini. Those patterns are useful, but no single color patch identifies a specimen.

## Confirm pores before comparing bolete names

Turn the cap without tearing it from the stem. The underside should contain a continuous layer of tiny pores, not radiating gills, folds, or teeth. Photograph it perpendicular to the surface with a ruler. Record pore color at the margin, midpoint, and close to the stem because maturity and handling can make the surface uneven.

The [pore surface guide](/identification/mushroom-pore-surface-guide/) explains how tubes terminate in pores and how to distinguish that structure from thick folds. If the underside is damaged, section a small edge to show the tube layer. Do not begin with “brown bolete”; begin with observable construction.

## Follow the pore color through maturity

Young bitter boletes may have white or cream pores. A pinkish cast often develops as the spores mature, sometimes first in protected areas. Old surfaces can become dull flesh-brown, stained, or insect-damaged. Camera white balance can push cream toward pink, especially under green canopy light.

Photograph a developmental series at the same exposure with a neutral card beside, but not touching, each cap. Compare sheltered and exposed areas. A real pink maturation pattern across several bodies has more weight than a single warm photograph.

## Map the raised net on the stem

Reticulation is a network of raised ridges, not merely streaked pigment or cracking. Use side light and macro focus. In typical bitter bolete material, brown ridges can form a conspicuous coarse net across much of the pale stem. Record its color, ridge height, mesh size, and how far it extends from apex to base.

Porcini often shows a finer pale or whitish net concentrated toward the upper stem, although variation exists within the *Boletus edulis* group. Soil can darken a pale net, and abrasion can flatten ridges. Clean only a small window with a soft brush after documenting the untouched surface.

## Section the flesh and time changes

Make one vertical cut through cap, tube layer, and stem after exterior photography. Record flesh color immediately, then at one, five, and fifteen minutes. Typical bitter bolete flesh remains white to cream rather than rapidly turning blue. Mark insect galleries and water-soaked zones separately; damaged tissue does not represent the intact reaction.

Absence of blue staining narrows some comparisons but does not identify *T. felleus*. Many boletes remain pale. The useful result is a matrix: pore maturation, reticulation, flesh response, spore deposit, host context, and microscopic evidence where necessary.

## Bitterness is famous but not a safe shortcut

Many accounts emphasize an extremely bitter taste capable of spoiling an entire dish. Yet perceived bitterness varies among people, specimens, and preparation. Advice to chew and spit raw tissue creates avoidable exposure and contamination risks and can encourage tasting of the wrong species.

This guide does not use taste as a required character. If a specimen was already cooked into food and an intensely bitter flavor is noticed, stop eating rather than using more bites to test it. Retain uncooked material and document all mushrooms used.

## Habitat describes a tree relationship

*Tylopilus felleus* is ectomycorrhizal: its underground mycelium associates with living tree roots. Fruiting bodies may appear near conifers or broadleaf trees, sometimes from moss, litter, or soil rich in decayed wood. A cap beside a stump is not evidence that it decomposed that stump.

Record trees within a measured radius, noting which are living and mature enough to contribute roots. Photograph needles, leaves, bark, and the wider stand. Do not assign a host from the closest trunk alone; roots overlap and can extend beyond the canopy.

## Geography changes the comparison set

The name is broadly reported across the Northern Hemisphere, but regional *Tylopilus* diversity differs. A pink-spored bolete in eastern North America, Europe, or Asia may face a different set of similar species. Global occurrence portals are starting points to records, not substitutes for local keys.

Retain coordinates at an appropriate privacy level, elevation, soil context, forest type, and date. A regional expert can interpret a coarse dark net more effectively when locality and nearby trees are known.

## Obtain and interpret a spore deposit

Place a mature cap section pore-side down across white and dark clean surfaces and cover it against drafts. Record duration, humidity, and whether the cap was already old or dry. A pinkish-brown or flesh-colored deposit supports *Tylopilus* placement, but several members share it.

Microscopy may compare spore size and shape, pileipellis, tube structures, and clamp features using a current treatment. A deposit color should be described under neutral light and kept as a photograph with the specimen number.

## Run a no-taste comparison protocol

Select three fruit bodies spanning apparent ages. For each, score the pore surface on a neutral scale from cream through pink-brown, photograph a one-centimeter stem window under raking light, and count reticulation intersections within that window. Section one representative and time its color response.

Then compare the set with regional descriptions for bitter bolete and porcini. The protocol produces repeatable evidence without tasting. It also detects mixed collections: one cap with olive pores among pink-pored neighbors may be a different species rather than simple age variation.

## Food and exposure boundary

## Preserve the characters that disappear first

Fresh pore color, stem reticulation, and bruising timing deteriorate quickly after collection. If a scientifically useful voucher is justified and lawful, photograph the intact specimen from cap, underside, full stem, and base before transport. Wrap bodies separately in paper so spores and pigments from different boletes do not transfer. Never mix an unidentified voucher with food mushrooms.

At the work surface, record cap width, stem dimensions, pore density, tube depth, odor without inhaling closely, and the timed section response. Make a spore deposit before drying if maturity allows. Dry thin representative sections promptly with ventilated warm air and retain the separately labeled deposit. A sealed refrigerator bag may preserve bulk for a short time but also encourages condensation, bacterial change, and misleading discoloration.

The label should include coordinates at an appropriate privacy level, elevation, date, nearby living trees, soil and litter context, collector, and field number. Photographs and notes remain linked to that number. A dried brown cap lacking its fresh pore color and host context is far less useful for resolving a pink-spored bolete.

## Treat mixed baskets as separate identification problems

Boletes growing within a few meters can belong to different species. Before any culinary decision, place each fruit body into a group based on pore color, reticulation, bruising, and attachment—not cap color alone. If a single member differs, isolate the entire group until it is independently evaluated. One bitter bolete can affect a dish, but bitterness is not the only concern: a mistaken assumption of uniform identity can conceal a more consequential lookalike.

Market or cultivated provenance also does not validate a wild specimen placed beside it. Maintain separate containers, knives, boards, and labels for food and study material. This simple chain-of-custody rule prevents a field comparison from becoming an accidental exposure.

Bitter bolete is rejected as food because bitterness can remain after cooking and may make a mixed dish inedible. Gastrointestinal reactions are also possible. More importantly, a webpage cannot determine whether a collected bolete is this species, another inedible taxon, or a specimen affected by decay, pollution, or improper storage.

If illness follows mushroom ingestion, contact local emergency services or a poison center. Preserve uncooked specimens, cooked leftovers, photographs, timing, quantities, and packaging. Do not wait for an online identification before seeking care.

## Evidence limits and editorial method

## Frequently asked questions

### Can the pore surface stay white?

Yes, especially in young material. That is why mature fruit bodies, stem netting, spore color, and a regional comparison are important.

### Is the dark stem net always present?

Its prominence varies with age, growth, dirt, and abrasion. Examine the full stem under side light rather than relying on one smooth or damaged patch.

### Does cooking remove the bitterness?

It may remain intense and can spread through a dish. Cooking is not an identification method and does not make an unknown bolete safe.

### Can bitter bolete grow from a stump?

It is associated with living tree roots. Fruiting beside or through rotten wood does not by itself establish wood-decay nutrition; trace the actual emergence point and record nearby living trees.

### Is a pink spore print enough?

No. It supports a *Tylopilus*-type comparison, but several species share pinkish deposits. Structure, microscopy, hosts, and geography remain relevant.
