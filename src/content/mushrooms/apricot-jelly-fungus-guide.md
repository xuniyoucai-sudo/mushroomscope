## Define the body plan before naming the color

Apricot jelly fungus has no ordinary cap-and-stem architecture. Each fruit body rises as a gelatinous tongue, spoon, ear, or narrow funnel, often split from the rim toward the base. The two sides can curl apart, producing a flame-like profile. That longitudinal slit and upright posture are more useful than “orange jelly,” which could describe many unrelated fungi after rain.

Photograph a cluster from ground level, directly above, and from the side. Include a ruler without pressing the lobes flat. Record total height, widest lobe width, tissue thickness at an undamaged edge, and the fraction of fruit bodies that show a slit. A single perfect funnel selected from a variable colony can misrepresent the population, so count intact, split, collapsed, and insect-damaged forms along a short transect.

## Test texture without destroying the record

Hydrated tissue is translucent to semi-translucent and gelatinous but usually resilient enough to hold an upright form. A gentle bend of one sacrificial edge can document whether it springs, tears, or crumbles. Orange peel fungus, by contrast, is cup-like and more brittle. Do not repeatedly squeeze the whole collection: pressure removes surface evidence, mixes soil into tissue, and makes a naturally thin lobe appear amorphous.

Weather changes the phenotype. After rain the lobes may be swollen, glossy, and paler at thin edges. During drying they shrink, darken, wrinkle, and become tougher. Mark one fruit body and make photographs at a fixed angle over several hours if conditions permit. A fresh-to-dry sequence is more informative than assuming two hydration states are different taxa.

## Show that the fertile surface is smooth

The exposed lobe lacks true gills, pores, and teeth. Use soft raking light from the side to show the smooth surface and any natural folds without inventing relief through sharpening. Photograph both faces and the cut edge. A fold is a change in geometry, not a gill: it does not repeat as a regular blade running toward a stem.

A pale spore deposit may be difficult to collect from small, immature, or weathered material. Place a mature lobe over contrasting surfaces in a humid but not sealed chamber, preserve orientation, and inspect for an organized deposit rather than condensation. Microscopy can add basidial and spore characters, but measurements need a documented sample size, calibration, and regional literature. Do not copy dimensions from a guide into an observation as if they were measured.

## Trace the apparent soil fruiting to wood

Many records appear to arise from bare soil, moss, or needle litter. Examine the attachment gently and look for buried roots, stump fragments, chips, or sawdust. Photograph the base in place before removing soil. The association with coniferous wood or wood-rich disturbed ground can be stronger evidence than the visible surface suggests, yet it should be recorded as observed or inferred rather than declared from habitat alone.

Map a one-meter circle around the cluster. Note tree species where known, live versus dead trunks, cut stumps, trail surfacing, old log landings, mulch, and drainage. A line of fruit bodies along a buried root or rotten log gives a different substrate hypothesis from a scattered distribution across mineral soil. The [habitat documentation guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) provides a repeatable way to separate direct attachment from proximity.

## Distinguish it from orange cups

The [orange peel fungus guide](/mushrooms/orange-peel-fungus-guide/) covers a frequent visual confusion. *Aleuria aurantia* makes shallow cups, saucers, or irregular flat patches, often on disturbed bare soil. Its vivid inner surface and paler outer surface form a cup fungus architecture, and the tissue is not the same translucent resilient jelly. Apricot jelly tends to stand taller as tongue-like or split funnel lobes and is associated more clearly with woody material.

Compare whole colonies rather than isolated fragments. A trampled orange cup can look folded; a collapsed jelly lobe can look like a cup. Texture, thickness, attachment, repeated form across the colony, and microscopy resolve more than saturation. Camera auto white balance can shift salmon tissue toward yellow or red, so include a neutral card when color will carry taxonomic weight.

## Separate other jelly fungi by attachment and architecture

Wood ears attach laterally to exposed wood and usually show an ear-like upper and lower surface. Witch's butter makes brain-like yellow folds, while many *Dacrymyces* begin as small cushions or pustules directly on wood. Coral-like *Calocera* branches rather than opening into broad split funnels. The [jelly tooth guide](/mushrooms/jelly-tooth-mushroom-guide/) adds a distinctly toothed fertile underside absent here.

These contrasts organize observation; they do not guarantee a species name. Tiny or dried material can lose its characteristic profile, and several jelly fungi require microscopic study. Keep the substrate fragment with a numbered specimen rather than combining multiple colonies in one bag.

## Build a hydration and recurrence record

Choose three representative lobes and measure height, width, and edge thickness after rain, then again during drying without uprooting them. Note air temperature, recent precipitation, canopy exposure, and whether the colony receives seepage or trail runoff. The purpose is not to derive a universal shrinkage percentage. It is to distinguish reversible hydration change from decay, grazing, or growth at one site.

Return after the next wet period and photograph from the same marked viewpoint. Record whether fruit bodies rehydrate, whether new lobes appear, and whether the visible woody substrate has changed. Repeated local observations can reveal a fruiting window and substrate relationship, but they should not be generalized to the species' global range without occurrence data.

## Preserve a useful voucher

Select intact lobes at more than one developmental stage, retaining the narrowed base and a small attached substrate sample. Photograph them fresh beside the field number because drying radically changes shape and color. Dry promptly with moving warm air appropriate for fungal vouchers; do not seal wet gelatinous tissue in plastic for extended transport. Store the dried packet with location, date, habitat, substrate confidence, measurements, photographs, and collector notes.

For a consequential range or species claim, compare the voucher with current regional keys and curated collections. GBIF occurrence maps are discovery tools, not proof that every plotted identification is correct. Name databases help track accepted combinations and synonyms, but nomenclature and morphology answer different questions.

## Keep edibility outside the identification shortcut

Some regional sources may discuss culinary history, but this guide makes no food recommendation. A correct name does not resolve contaminated substrate, spoilage, local regulation, allergy, or individual response. Jelly texture and attractive color are not safety signals. Never use a small taste as an identification method.

If symptoms follow any mushroom exposure, retain specimens, food remnants, photographs, location, amount, and timing, and contact local poison or medical services. This page has editorial review only and no named mycologist or clinician reviewer. Its purpose is to improve the quality of a field record, not to clear a collection for consumption.

## Create a lobe-by-lobe colony table

Number at least ten visible fruit bodies in a wide photograph and record height, maximum width, slit present or absent, number of major lobes, attachment type, hydration state, and damage. This exposes whether the colony consistently produces the split-funnel architecture or whether one selected specimen is carrying the identification. Include collapsed and grazed bodies instead of removing inconvenient variation.

Repeat the count at the next wet visit from the same viewpoint. New small lobes, expanded marked lobes, and vanished lobes should be recorded separately. Do not assume every orange structure at the site belongs to the same organism; give a second field number to cushions, cups, or branched forms and compare them independently.

## Keep microscopy tied to measured fresh anatomy

Microscopy should answer a defined question rather than decorate a field guess. Select a mature lobe whose fresh profile, surface, dimensions, and substrate attachment are already documented. Mark which surface supplied the section, use calibrated optics, record mount medium and magnification, and report the number and range of spores measured. Preserve images that show measurement endpoints rather than only the most typical spore.

If a DNA barcode is pursued, keep extraction identity linked to the same voucher and report locus, sequence quality, database, search date, coverage, and competing matches. A close database match can support a hypothesis, but mislabeled references, incomplete taxon sampling, and changing names remain limitations. Deposit sequences and vouchers where they can be reassessed rather than treating an app result as permanent proof.

## Interpret occurrence records by evidence class

Map points from GBIF and similar portals mix preserved specimens, observations, literature records, and datasets with different verification. Filter by basis of record and inspect the underlying institution or image when making a range statement. A nearby point raises plausibility; it does not identify a new collection. Conversely, the absence of a point may reflect limited sampling or data sharing.

State the geographic scope of the field guide or key used. A European name applied to a North American collection may be correct, historical, or a placeholder for a related concept. Keeping region and evidence class visible prevents an attractive global map from flattening unresolved taxonomy.

## Frequently asked questions

### What shape is apricot jelly fungus?

Fruit bodies are upright, gelatinous, and spatula-, tongue-, ear-, or funnel-like, commonly with a longitudinal slit that leaves two uneven lobes.

### Does apricot jelly fungus grow on soil or wood?

It can appear terrestrial but is often associated with buried woody debris, roots, stumps, or wood-rich disturbed ground, especially in conifer settings.

### Does it have gills or pores?

No. Its spore-bearing surface is smooth rather than arranged as gills, pores, or teeth. Raking light helps document the surface honestly.

### Can dried apricot jelly fungus be identified from color?

Not reliably. Dehydration changes color, translucency, thickness, and shape, so rehydration response, substrate, microscopy, and a fresh-stage record matter.

### Is apricot jelly fungus edible?

This page makes no food recommendation. Regional identity, substrate exposure, condition, and lookalike risk cannot be resolved from a web comparison.
