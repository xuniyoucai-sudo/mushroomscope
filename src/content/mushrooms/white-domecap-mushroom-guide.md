**White domecap**, *Leucocybe connata* (often published as *Lyophyllum connatum*), is a pale mushroom of damp, disturbed ground that commonly produces dense clusters with stems joined at the base. The shared base is more informative than white color alone, but it is not exclusive. A reviewable record must keep cap development, gill attachment, complete basal construction, spore color, site history, and dangerous white lookalikes tied to numbered specimens.

This species page does not cover every clustered white mushroom. The [clustered bonnet guide](/mushrooms/clustered-bonnet-mushroom-guide/) follows a smaller wood-growing *Mycena*, while the [destroying angel guide](/mushrooms/destroying-angel-mushroom-guide/) explains why a complete white *Amanita* base is a high-consequence finding. Here the intent is evidence separation, not food permission.

## Map the cluster before separating it

Photograph the entire group from above and from ground level. Count stems, measure the cluster footprint, and mark isolated fruit bodies nearby. Record which caps overlap, which stems curve away from crowding, and whether smaller mushrooms emerge from the same basal mass. A harvested pile cannot reconstruct this architecture reliably.

Trace the site boundary. White domecaps are associated with damp, nutrient-rich disturbed places such as path edges, parks, gardens, roadside banks, woodland margins, and ground containing decomposing plant debris. Note recent soil movement, mowing, mulch, buried wood, drainage, and vegetation rather than reducing the habitat to “woods.”

Do not assume that every cap in a tight group is one species. Mixed fruitings occur where litter, soil, and buried roots meet. Number representative fruit bodies and preserve their connection from field photograph through section and spore work.

## Recover the conjoined basal mass

Excavate one group as a block with a margin of soil. Brush only enough material away to show whether several stems truly merge or merely press against one another. Photograph the bases from multiple sides with a scale before separating them. Record attached litter, fine roots, woody fragments, cords, and any membrane.

True connate growth means the stem bases share tissue. The feature can be lost when pickers snap mushrooms at ground level. Conversely, roots, compacted soil, and white mycelium can bind unrelated stems into an apparent cluster. A clean longitudinal cut through the shared base helps distinguish tissue continuity from surface contact.

Absence of a volva matters only with a complete excavation. A white *Amanita* broken above its basal sac or bulb cannot be dismissed because the remaining stem looks plain. Keep high-consequence alternatives open until the whole base is visible.

## Measure cap development under neutral light

Select young, mid-stage, and expanded caps without blending their traits. Measure diameter, height, margin position, and central profile. Young caps are domed to convex; older ones broaden and may become irregular where neighbors collide. White, ivory, cream, and pale gray-buff zones should be described separately.

Use diffuse light and protect highlights from clipping. A pure white cap can lose surface texture in a phone photograph, making fine silkiness, moisture, bruising, and dirt impossible to judge. Include a neutral gray reference and repeat one view after a measured drying interval.

Crowding produces asymmetry. A flattened edge pressed beneath another cap is developmental evidence, not necessarily a species trait. Measure an outer uncrowded fruit body alongside a central crowded one to quantify the effect of the cluster.

## Document gills and attachment in section

Take a square-on underside photograph of an intact cap. Record spacing, short gills, forks, edge condition, color, and attachment. White domecap gills are crowded and pale, attached to slightly descending. An upturned cap can make gills appear more decurrent than they are.

Cut through cap center and stem. The section reveals cap thickness, the gill-to-stem junction, stem interior, and any hidden structure. Use the [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) to keep attachment, spacing, and branching as separate observations.

Free gills redirect the comparison toward *Amanita*, *Agaricus*, *Leucoagaricus*, and other groups. Strongly decurrent gills may support a funnel comparison. Do not force an intermediate attachment into a preferred label; preserve the section and describe exactly what is visible.

## Make a controlled white-spore deposit

Place a mature cap section over adjoining black and white nonabsorbent surfaces. Cover against drafts while allowing limited ventilation, and label specimen, time, temperature, and duration. A white deposit will be clearest on the dark side, while the light side helps expose contamination.

White spores do not establish *L. connata*. Destroying angels, white funnels, bonnets, and many unrelated mushrooms overlap. The deposit mainly rejects mature pink-, rust-, chocolate-, purple-brown-, and black-spored alternatives when it is sufficiently dense and unambiguous.

If the print is weak, repeat it with another mature cap. Condensation, gill fragments, and reflected light are not spores. Where a defensible identification is needed, retain dried material and calibrated microscopy linked to the same specimen record.

## Compare white funnels without shortcut rules

Several small white *Clitocybe*-like mushrooms contain toxins and occur in grass or litter. They may form arcs, rings, or troops and often have gills descending the stem. White domecap’s dense connate cluster and disturbed-ground ecology help structure the comparison, but neither provides safety clearance.

Measure cap-to-stem ratios, gill descent, spacing, odor, and basal fusion across matched mature specimens. Do not compare one waterlogged old domecap with a pristine young funnel. Regional keys and microscopic characters may be required when gross morphology converges.

Any rule that treats “growing in a cluster” as edible is unsafe. Honey fungi, sulfur tufts, bonnets, funnels, and many other mushrooms cluster for different ecological reasons.

## Exclude white Amanita construction first

Destroying angels are high-consequence comparisons because white caps, pale gills, and white spores overlap superficially. Excavation must reveal the complete base. Look for a sac, rim, bulb, zones of universal-veil material, a ring or ring remnants, and genuinely free gills.

Rain, animals, age, and handling can erase veil tissue. A missing ring in a damaged specimen is not proof of absence. If the base is cut, the gills are not clearly attached, or the collection is mixed, stop at an unresolved white agaric.

The risk boundary is deliberate: no website image, chemical spot, smell, or single feature can convert incomplete material into food clearance.

## Use site history as ecological evidence

Record soil texture, moisture, slope, compaction, vegetation, litter depth, and recent disturbance. Ask whether mulch, compost, road fill, excavation, mowing, or flood deposition changed the site. Repeat photographs after rain and through the fruiting period to see whether clusters recur along the same buried organic zone.

This evidence supports a saprotrophic disturbed-ground interpretation but does not prove the exact food source. A cluster beside a stump may be using buried wood, rich soil, or mixed debris. Culture or sequence-linked substrate sampling would be needed for a stronger causal claim.

Avoid destructive trenching. A small basal block and a fixed photo transect usually preserve more long-term value than excavating every cluster.

## Reconcile Leucocybe and Lyophyllum names

Older and regional sources commonly use *Lyophyllum connatum*; other current treatments use *Leucocybe connata*. Preserve the name used by each source and search both when reviewing specimens, papers, and occurrence portals. A database synonym does not guarantee that every historical identification was re-examined.

For distribution records, check voucher institution, identifier, locality precision, habitat notes, and the taxonomic backbone date. Photographs of detached white caps carry less evidence than specimens with conjoined bases, sections, spores, and reviewable microscopy.

State geographic limits honestly. A map assembled from mixed names and unvouchered observations should not become a claim of confirmed global distribution.

## Keep chemistry and food claims bounded

Some field treatments have changed their advice about this species, including concern over chemical findings and uncertainty about relevance. A compound detected under particular experimental conditions is not automatically a clinical outcome, yet uncertainty is also not permission to recommend eating a remotely identified collection.

MushroomScope therefore makes no edibility claim. Identity, white-gilled lookalikes, site contamination, freshness, preparation, dose, and individual reaction cannot be resolved here. Do not taste an unknown mushroom as an identification test.

If illness follows ingestion, retain uncooked and cooked remains, photographs, packaging, and meal timing. Contact local poison-control or emergency medical services promptly and do not induce vomiting unless directed by a professional.

## Build a reviewable white-domecap record

1. Map the complete group before stems are separated.
2. Excavate and section one shared basal mass.
3. Measure young, expanded, central, and uncrowded caps.
4. Photograph gills square-on and confirm attachment in vertical section.
5. Make a labeled two-color white-spore deposit.
6. Record disturbed-ground history and exact substrate separately.
7. Exclude complete white *Amanita* construction and regional white funnels.
8. Preserve a voucher where legal and state uncertainty explicitly.
