## Record the whole emergence sequence

Common stinkhorn, *Phallus impudicus*, can move from a partly buried egg to a tall mature fruit body within hours. Its final outline is conspicuous, but the useful evidence lies in the transition: egg tissue ruptures, a porous white receptacle extends, an olive spore mass coats the pitted cap, insects remove that mass, and the fragile structure collapses. Treat each stage as a linked record rather than naming an isolated object.

Photograph the undisturbed site widely enough to show woodland litter, garden mulch, compost-rich soil, hedge base, or other organic context. Map eggs and mature fruit bodies, give each a number, and note whether they form a line, ring, or scattered group. Record rain, air temperature, substrate moisture, and time. Do not excavate every egg; repeated non-destructive views reveal emergence rate better than a tray of detached specimens.

## Section one egg only when collection is justified

An unopened stinkhorn egg is whitish to cream, sometimes stained by soil, with rootlike mycelial cords at the base. Its outside is not enough for a reliable decision. If collection is legal and enough material remains at the site, cut one numbered egg longitudinally from apex through base. Photograph the intact exterior first and then the section immediately with a scale.

The section should show an outer covering, gelatinous layer, compressed porous stem, and folded cap structure. Trace each internal part to the mature fruit body rather than memorizing a decorative cross-section. Note egg height, width, wall thickness, gel depth, internal colors, odor before and after cutting, and attachment cords. Keep the image tied to the specimen number and site map.

The safety reason for sectioning is important. Young *Amanita* buttons can also be enclosed by universal-veil tissue, but their internal architecture develops a conventional cap and gills rather than a stinkhorn receptacle and gleba. A badly cut, decayed, or photographed egg may remain unresolved. Never turn a remote image of a white ball into permission to eat it.

## Measure the porous stem and pitted cap

The mature stem is white, hollow, coarsely porous, and easily crushed. Measure total height, stem width at three levels, wall thickness, cavity width, and the dimensions of the ruptured volva. Photograph the complete base before cleaning. Soil around the cup is ecological evidence, while a trimmed base hides one of the most useful connections to the egg stage.

The cap is conical to bell-shaped with a pitted or honeycomb-like surface. Fresh olive-brown gleba can fill the pits and soften the outline. After flies remove the sticky mass, the underlying pale cap becomes much clearer. Photograph the same cap before and after insect activity when possible. A clean pale cap is not necessarily a different species, and a uniformly glossy brown cone may be too fresh to show its underlying architecture.

Record whether a pale perforated disc or opening is visible at the apex, but do not make one camera angle decisive. Measure cap height, basal width, pit size across a defined line, and gleba coverage as an approximate percentage. These repeatable observations communicate stage more honestly than “typical stinkhorn shape.”

## Treat odor and insects as ecological evidence

The gleba's strong carrion-like odor attracts flies and other insects. They walk over the sticky spore mass, feed, and transport spores away. This is an active dispersal system rather than incidental decay. Record the first time odor is detected at a stated distance and wind direction, then list observed visitors without claiming they are identified beyond the available photograph.

Odor is subjective and changes with temperature, wind, rain, age, and gleba removal. The [odor documentation guide](/identification/mushroom-odor-identification/) recommends neutral language, separate observers, and no close inhalation of unknown material. “Carrion-like at one meter downwind” is more reviewable than “unmistakably disgusting.”

Insect activity can erase diagnostic material quickly. Photograph the gleba, cap pits, visitor abundance, and exposed cap at timed intervals. Do not spray or trap insects for a routine record. A mature fruit body with most gleba removed can still be documented through the remaining cap architecture, porous stem, volva, nearby eggs, and site history.

## Map habitat without confusing soil with nutrition

Common stinkhorn is saprotrophic and occurs in humus-rich woodland ground, gardens, hedges, mulch, compost-rich sites, and other places with decomposing organic matter. The visible fruit body may arise from soil while the mycelium exploits dispersed woody debris. Record mulch type, litter depth, buried roots or chips, irrigation, recent soil disturbance, and whether fruiting recurs after rain.

Do not infer a partnership with the nearest living tree merely because the fungus stands inside its canopy. A substrate core is rarely justified for a public observation. Instead, document what is visible and label buried material as inferred. The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) provides an observation hierarchy that keeps “on soil,” “in wood-rich mulch,” and “on a named host” from collapsing into one claim.

Garden occurrence does not establish that a purchased mulch bag carried the organism, nor does it imply garden harm. If removal is desired because of odor, lift mature structures and eggs with ordinary hygiene, avoid spreading gleba indoors, and address excess buried woody material only if it conflicts with site management. Fungicide use is not an identification method.

## Compare common stinkhorn with close forms

Dog stinkhorns such as *Mutinus caninus* are usually slimmer and smaller. Their spore mass is carried near a pointed colored tip rather than across a distinct broad honeycombed cap. The [dog stinkhorn guide](/mushrooms/dog-stinkhorn-guide/) shows how stem color, tip structure, size, egg anatomy, and habitat should be compared together.

The dune stinkhorn, *Phallus hadriani*, often has a pinkish to violet egg or volva and a stronger association with sandy habitats. Color fades, soil stains, and habitat overlap prevent a one-feature decision. Document the fresh egg, mature base, cap, spores, region, and substrate, then use a current regional treatment.

Other *Phallus* species occur in different regions and may share a white porous stem and gleba-coated cap. Introduced populations and garden materials complicate range assumptions. Preserve a voucher when expert review is realistic and include microscopy or DNA only with traceable specimen handling.

False comparisons can begin at the egg stage. Puffballs have a different internal spore-bearing organization, and *Amanita* buttons show developing gills and cap tissue when properly sectioned. A superficial white exterior does not join them biologically. If the interior is damaged, immature, or unclear, retain an unresolved label and wait for another egg to mature naturally.

## Keep food traditions outside identification clearance

Some sources discuss consumption of very young stinkhorn eggs. That cultural fact does not solve identity, site contamination, freshness, microbial condition, allergy, or individual gastrointestinal response. The egg stage hides many of the mature structures that make the group recognizable. This guide therefore classifies common stinkhorn conservatively as inedible and gives no preparation instructions.

Never sample a wild egg to test its identity. Cooking cannot correct an *Amanita* mistake, and odor absence in an unopened egg is not reassuring evidence. Specimens from treated mulch, roadsides, pet areas, or polluted soil have additional site hazards independent of taxonomy.

If illness follows any wild mushroom ingestion, contact the relevant poison center or medical service. Save uncooked material, leftovers, photographs of the intact site and sections, preparation details, quantities, and symptom timing. Do not induce vomiting unless directed by a medical professional.

## Build a reviewable common stinkhorn record

A strong record combines site overview, mapped eggs and mature structures, one justified egg section, emergence timing, complete volva, porous stem measurements, gleba coverage, pitted cap after partial gleba removal, insect visits, odor notes, weather, and substrate context. Store the files under specimen numbers so that an egg section cannot accidentally be paired with another species fruiting nearby.

Run a contradiction audit before choosing the name. A solid rather than porous stem, conventional gills, no gleba at any mature stage, absence of a volva, a slender colored *Mutinus*-type tip, or strongly colored egg tissue should redirect the comparison. Missing evidence is not a contradiction, but it must not be silently filled from a field guide.
