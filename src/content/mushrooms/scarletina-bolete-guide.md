## Red pores are a warning to slow down

Scarletina bolete, *Neoboletus luridiformis*, is a striking woodland mushroom: a dark cap above a yellow-to-red pore layer, a heavily red-dotted stem, and flesh that can turn deep blue after damage. Those features make it memorable, but none is unique. Red-pored, blue-bruising boletes include several difficult and high-consequence comparisons. The responsible goal is a complete, timed record, not a fast edible-or-poisonous label.

Photograph the fruit body before handling. Show its position relative to trees, moss, litter, and soil, then capture the cap profile, pore surface, full stem, and intact base. If several ages are present, keep them in one contextual frame. Young pores can differ substantially from mature pores, and weathered specimens may have lost the cap texture or stem color emphasized in field guides.

GBIF currently accepts the name *Neoboletus luridiformis*. Older sources may place the species in *Boletus* or use names whose application varies by region. Record the source and date of the taxonomic concept you follow. Close species can require regional literature or microscopy, so a name copied from an older photograph is not enough.

## Measure the cap and pore development separately

The cap begins domed and can be dark brown, chestnut, or nearly black-brown, often with a finely velvety surface. With age it broadens and may become smoother. Rain can make it appear polished, while drought may crack or fade the surface. Record cap width, margin shape, surface texture, and color under neutral daylight for at least three stages.

Turn one specimen only after its original position has been documented. The fertile surface is made of tubes ending in pores, not gills. Very young pores may be yellow before developing orange-red or red tones. Bruising can rapidly obscure the original color, so photograph an untouched area first. Then press one defined spot gently and photograph at 0, 30, 120, and 600 seconds under constant light.

The tube layer and cap flesh should be distinguished in a lengthwise section. Note tube depth, attachment near the stem, flesh color before oxidation, and the order in which areas turn blue. A single final photograph of a fully blue cut surface erases that sequence. Timing creates repeatable evidence and makes comparison with a regional key more reliable.

## Dots and reticulation are different structures

Scarletina's stem usually has a yellow ground densely covered with separate red dots or granules. A reticulation is a connected raised mesh. This distinction is central because several red-pored comparisons show a net, especially toward the upper stem. Clean loose soil gently with a dry brush, then photograph the upper, middle, and lower stem with side lighting. Do not scrub away the very ornament being studied.

Use a macro view sharp enough to follow individual marks. If the red elements repeatedly connect into polygons, record reticulation rather than calling them dots. If they remain discrete even where dense, record granulation. Avoid deciding from color alone; a heavily dotted stem can look uniformly red at normal viewing distance.

Keep the base intact. Record whether it is tapered, swollen, rooting, or damaged by insects. A cross-section through cap and stem can show internal larval channels and oxidation, but make that cut only after all external characters are photographed. Link every image to one specimen code so that features from different mushrooms are not accidentally combined.

## Map the blue reaction through time

Blue bruising is an oxidation reaction, not proof that a mushroom is poisonous or edible. Numerous boletes blue, with different starting colors, locations, speeds, and intensities. Create two controlled tests: a gentle pore bruise and a clean longitudinal section. Photograph both on the same schedule with a color reference.

Record where blue first appears: pores, tube layer, cap flesh, stem cortex, or stem base. Note whether the reaction becomes navy, blue-green, or nearly black and whether it later fades. Pressure strength and tissue age affect the result, so compare similar fresh tissues. The [bruising-reactions guide](/identification/mushroom-bruising-reactions/) explains why a timed sequence is stronger than a memory-based adjective.

Do not taste a red-pored bolete to add another character. Taste tests in older keys assume an already narrowed, appropriate group and trained practice; they are unsuitable for a public safety workflow. Odor may be noted without prolonged inhalation or handling, but it rarely overrides anatomy and ecology.

## Host evidence is part of the specimen record

This bolete forms ectomycorrhizal relationships with living trees. Records can involve beech, oak, spruce, and other hosts depending on region. Photograph trunks, bark, leaves or needles, and canopy position for plausible nearby partners. A fallen leaf beside the mushroom is weak evidence because wind and litter movement can mix species.

Map the distance from the fruit body to the nearest mature trees. Record soil character, slope, drainage, moss cover, and whether the setting is broadleaf, conifer, or mixed woodland. Repeated fruiting near the same root zone across years supports a host hypothesis more strongly than one visit. The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) offers a structured record for direct and indirect host evidence.

Range must be checked with a current regional source. Database maps contain historical names, coordinate uncertainty, and records made under earlier species concepts. Use locality as a plausibility filter, not as a substitute for anatomy. Where a close relative is known locally, document the characters that separate it rather than treating the familiar European name as default.

## Build a red-pored bolete comparison table

*Suillellus luridus* is a prominent comparison and often shows a connected stem reticulation. Sectioning and the relationship between tube layer, cap flesh, and stem tissue also matter. *Rubroboletus satanas* is poisonous and often has a pale cap, a bulbous stem, and reticulation, but weather and age can blur simple color summaries. Other *Rubroboletus*, *Suillellus*, and *Neoboletus* species may enter the key by region.

## Treat culinary claims as a separate decision

Some regional sources describe Scarletina bolete as edible only after thorough cooking, while raw or undercooked material can cause illness. That history does not make an uncertain specimen safe. Confusion among red-pored boletes, variation in names, site contamination, spoilage, and individual intolerance all remain relevant. This guide therefore does not recommend collecting it for food.

Cooking cannot correct a mistaken identification. A blue reaction cannot clear a specimen, and a lack of symptoms in one person does not establish safety for another. Local expert confirmation must precede any food consideration, and applicable regional guidance should govern preparation. Children, pregnant people, people with underlying conditions, and anyone taking medication should not be used as a test of wild-mushroom tolerance.

If symptoms follow ingestion, contact a poison center or emergency medical service promptly. Retain raw material, cooked leftovers, photographs, location, and timing. Do not delay while trying to settle the species name online, and do not induce vomiting unless directed by a clinician.

## Produce an auditable field record

A publication-quality record includes the undisturbed habitat, plausible host evidence, three developmental stages where possible, measurements, untouched pores, a controlled pore bruise, the complete dotted stem, an intact base, a timed longitudinal section, spore deposit, region, and any microscopy. It also states which lookalikes were considered and which characters remain unresolved.

Before leaving, verify that the image set preserves the original pore color and the oxidation sequence. Confirm that the close view can distinguish discrete dots from a connected net. If those decisive observations are missing, use a broader label such as red-pored, blue-bruising bolete pending expert review. Precision about uncertainty is more useful than an attractive but unauditable species name.

## Separate weather damage from diagnostic color

Rain, slugs, and repeated handling can create dark or blue-black zones that no longer reveal the first oxidation response. Mark each damaged area on a simple specimen diagram before starting a controlled test. Use untouched tissue on the opposite side for the pressure mark and section, then keep the naturally damaged and experimentally changed regions separate in captions. This prevents an old bruise from being reported as an immediate reaction.

Dry weather creates a different problem. A faded pore surface may appear ochre rather than red, while a cracked cap and dehydrated stem exaggerate texture. Record recent rainfall and compare sheltered and exposed fruit bodies from the same site without pooling their measurements. If only weathered material remains, say so and avoid reconstructing fresh colors from a guide.

Insect tunnels also oxidize along their margins. A cut face with scattered blue channels is not equivalent to a clean, evenly timed section. Photograph larval galleries, reject decomposing tissue from food consideration, and use an intact fresh specimen for taxonomic comparison when collection rules permit. Condition is evidence, not an inconvenience to crop out.
