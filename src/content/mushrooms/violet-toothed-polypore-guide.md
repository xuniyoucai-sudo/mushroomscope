The **Violet-toothed polypore** (*Trichaptum biforme*) is best treated as a field-evidence problem, not a photograph-matching exercise. Thin semicircular brackets on dead hardwood, with gray-brown hairy zonation above and a fresh violet to lilac pore surface below that soon tears into small teeth and fades with age. A useful record connects the full colony, exact wood attachment, both surfaces, scale, developmental stage, region, and—where the question requires it—microscopy. It cannot authenticate a specimen, diagnose a tree, or authorize eating.

## Photograph the whole log before the purple detail

Begin with an environmental frame that shows whether the fungus occupies a fallen trunk, branch, stump, or standing dead section. Record bark, probable host, diameter, decay class, soil contact, and the position of each cluster. Violet color can attract attention, but substrate is part of the identification. A hardwood record supports this species concept more strongly than an unidentified or conifer substrate. If host evidence is absent, say so. Do not transfer the name of a nearby living tree to a decayed log without a physical connection.

## Measure how thin the brackets really are

Photograph a side profile with a ruler. The brackets are typically thin and leathery, forming overlapping fans rather than thick fleshy shelves. Measure cap width, projection from the wood, thickness near the attachment, and margin thickness. Note whether individual brackets fuse laterally. Macro lenses can make a millimeter-scale edge look substantial, so a scale prevents misreading. Record fresh pliability separately from waterlogged softness or old brittleness; texture changes with condition and is not an edibility test.

## Read the gray zoned upper surface carefully

The cap can show pale gray, buff, or brown concentric zones with fine hairs. Side light reveals texture, while wet direct flash may erase it. Photograph young margins and weathered central areas under the same light. Cap zonation overlaps with turkey tail, Stereum, and many other bracket fungi, so it begins the comparison rather than ending it. The [turkey tail guide](/mushrooms/turkey-tail-mushroom-guide/) demonstrates why upper-surface bands must be paired with an underside examination.

## Find fresh violet at the growing margin

Turn naturally exposed brackets and compare multiple ages. Fresh fertile tissue may be distinctly violet or lilac, often strongest toward an active margin. Older areas fade to buff, gray, or brown after sun, frost, rain, and abrasion. Write a color description before editing photographs and include a neutral reference when practical. Absence of purple on one old bracket does not automatically exclude the species, but a colony with no visible fresh color needs stronger anatomical and microscopic support.

## Trace pores as they tear into teeth

Use magnification to inspect the transition from small pores to irregular torn walls and short teeth. Photograph a sequence from the margin inward. This developmental gradient explains why references may describe the surface as poroid, irpicoid, or toothed. Count pores or teeth across a measured distance rather than estimating from a screen. Avoid scraping the surface merely to make structures dramatic. A naturally broken edge or a lawful voucher can show thickness without damaging an entire colony.

## Separate turkey tail using the fertile surface

Turkey tail may share thin form, dense tiers, hairy color bands, and hardwood habitat. Its underside, however, remains pale and distinctly poroid rather than violet and torn into teeth. Always turn over several brackets, because algae, dirt, and shadow can hide the surface. The [pore-surface guide](/identification/mushroom-pore-surface-guide/) provides a measurement workflow. If a log carries brackets with two underside types, treat it as a mixed collection instead of averaging the evidence.

## Use substrate to separate related Trichaptum

Related species can overlap in appearance. Host and substrate are therefore operational evidence: verify hardwood versus conifer with bark, wood anatomy, leaves, needles, cones, or a connected branch when possible. Do not call heavily decayed wood by color alone. Record region and current nomenclature because older sources may apply names broadly. Where host evidence, fertile-surface development, and microscopic characters disagree, retain Trichaptum at genus level until a specialist or sequence-supported reference resolves it.

## Distinguish smooth false turkey tails

Stereum species can make thin colorful shelves, yet their fertile undersides are smooth rather than constructed from pores and torn teeth. Use a hand lens and grazing light; do not infer smoothness from a blurry photograph. Other polypores may have elongated pores, but lack the fresh violet phase or differ in cap texture and host. A useful comparison table records cap hairs, margin color, underside architecture, pore or tooth density, substrate, and thickness for the exact specimens in hand.

## Document aging as a colony timeline

A single log often carries fresh margins, mature violet surfaces, faded teeth, algae-darkened caps, and crumbling old brackets. Mark representative fruit bodies in photographs and record their stages. Revisit after a rain interval without claiming that different shelves are the same individual. The timeline teaches which characters persist and which disappear. It also reduces false negatives created by comparing a weathered colony with a reference photograph of fresh spring growth.

## Use microscopy to resolve the remaining uncertainty

Retain a lawful voucher containing cap, fertile surface, and wood attachment. Record the microscope preparation, calibration, number of spores measured, range rather than one value, basidia, cystidial or hyphal structures required by the chosen regional key, and image scale. White spores alone are not diagnostic among bracket fungi. Microscopy cannot rescue a sample detached from its host evidence and photographs. If tissue is contaminated, immature, or badly weathered, acknowledge that limitation rather than reporting false precision.

## Interpret white rot without making a tree diagnosis

The fungus decomposes lignified hardwood and contributes to nutrient cycling. Visible brackets indicate established fungal activity, but they do not quantify remaining wood strength or prove that a nearby living tree is unsafe. Record cavities, cracks, root condition, crown symptoms, targets, and loading separately. For a tree near people or structures, consult a qualified arborist. Scraping off brackets does not remove mycelium inside the wood and should not be presented as treatment.

## Keep the use boundary clear

These thin tough brackets are not presented as food or medicine. Do not taste an unresolved polypore, and do not infer safety from a shared common name or colorful photograph. Wild material may also carry molds, insects, pollutants, or multiple fungal species. If a person or animal becomes ill after mushroom exposure, contact the relevant poison center, veterinarian, or emergency service promptly and retain photographs, leftovers, location, and timing. A webpage match should never delay professional care.

## Archive evidence so another observer can check it

A strong record links overview, upper and lower surfaces, side profile, fresh and old margins, scale, host evidence, locality, date, and voucher under one identifier. Store unedited originals as well as cropped images. Note the guide or key used and the date accessed. If collection is prohibited, record non-destructively and leave the colony intact. Good provenance allows later taxonomic corrections without inventing certainty that the original observation did not support.

## Score a mixed-age colony without averaging it

Create a row for each numbered bracket rather than one description for the log. Record cap width, thickness, upper-zone colors, hair visibility, underside color, pore-tooth stage, margin condition, and exact position. Fresh lilac growth and old buff teeth can then be connected as developmental evidence without pretending every bracket has every feature. If one shelf has a smooth underside or persistent round pores, separate it as a possible second fungus and collect or photograph it under a new identifier.

This specimen-level table also makes absence meaningful. “No violet observed” should specify whether the entire underside was visible, whether fresh margins existed, and whether rain, frost, abrasion, or age could have removed color. A conclusion based on five fully documented brackets is stronger than one based on a hundred unturned caps. Photograph the completed field sheet or transcribe it promptly so measurements do not become reconstructed memories.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is the underside violet only sometimes?

The strongest violet is usually fresh growth; sunlight, rain, drying, and age fade it toward cream, tan, or brown.

### Does it have pores or teeth?

Fresh tissue begins poroid, but the walls split and tear during development, producing the characteristic tooth-like surface.

### How is it separated from turkey tail?

Turn every bracket over: turkey tail has a pale round-pored surface, while fresh violet-toothed polypore shows violet pores becoming torn teeth.

### Does a hardwood log prove Trichaptum biforme?

No. Hardwood is important supporting ecology, but anatomy, age, region, and sometimes microscopy must also agree.

### Is violet-toothed polypore edible?

This guide does not present the thin tough brackets as food and cannot clear any wild collection.
