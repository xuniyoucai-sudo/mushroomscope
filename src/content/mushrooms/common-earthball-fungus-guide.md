The **common earthball** (*Scleroderma citrinum*) should be documented as a complete organism in context, not matched from one dramatic feature. Document common earthball by its coarse scaly rind, soil attachment, dark maturing gleba, woodland ecology, puffball differences, toxicity, and safe field recording. The workflow below connects habitat, development, internal or fertile structures, microscopy limits, and conservative safety decisions. It cannot authenticate a specimen or authorize eating.

## Treat every round fungus as an unopened structure

A ball-shaped fruit body hides the features that ordinary mushroom guides place on the cap, gills, and stem. Begin by photographing the undisturbed group, soil, litter, nearby trees, spacing, and attachment. Then, where lawful, cut one representative specimen vertically from top through base. A complete section can reveal a rind and gleba, or expose the miniature cap, gills, and stem of an immature Amanita hidden inside a veil.

Do not rely on the folk rule that every white-centered ball is edible. Developmental stage matters, and several unrelated fungi converge on a rounded young form. The [identification safety checklist](/identification/mushroom-identification-safety-checklist/) explains why structure and provenance must precede any food discussion.

## Read the rind as architecture, not decoration

Common earthball develops a thick, tough outer wall colored ochre-yellow to yellow-brown. The surface often breaks into coarse, irregular polygonal scales with darker centers. Photograph texture with side lighting and include a ruler; harsh sharpening can invent cracks, while wet glare can erase them. Measure wall thickness on a clean vertical section rather than describing it only as “thick.”

Scale pattern varies with age, abrasion, rain, and soil. Fine, regular areoles may suggest another Scleroderma, while a smoother or spiny skin can redirect the comparison toward true puffballs. No surface character should be used without the interior, base, habitat, region, and microscopy when species-level certainty matters.

## Track the gleba from pale to purple-black

The internal spore-bearing mass is called the gleba. In common earthball it can begin pale but soon darkens through gray-violet and purple-black, later becoming brown and powdery. Photograph freshly cut young, intermediate, and mature specimens from the same site. Record whether the color is uniform, marbled, chambered, or organized into recognizable mushroom structures.

A mature dark interior is a strong warning against confusing the specimen with a culinary puffball. However, cutting is not a license to taste. An immature earthball may still be pale, and contamination, misidentification, or a different enclosed fungus remains possible. Use the cut as documentation, not as a kitchen test.

## Document the buried base and tree context

Common earthball has no ordinary stem. It narrows into a short sterile base and can be strongly anchored by thick mycelial cords. Clear only enough loose soil to show that connection, then replace disturbed litter when appropriate. Photograph whether fruit bodies are solitary, clustered, or arranged along a bank, path, root zone, or compacted edge.

The fungus is associated with tree partners and often occurs on acidic, nutrient-poor ground in woodland and heath. Nearby oak, beech, birch, pine, or other trees may support an ecological hypothesis, but proximity alone does not prove the partner. Record tree evidence and soil context separately. The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps prevent a generic “on soil” description from replacing useful ecology.

## Separate earthballs from true puffballs

True puffballs are not one simple comparison. Giant puffballs, pear-shaped puffballs, gem-studded puffballs, and others differ in size, outer ornament, sterile base, habitat, and how the spore mass opens. Common earthball combines a notably thick tough rind, coarse polygonal scales, a dark-maturing gleba, and strong soil attachment.

Cut every specimen selected for study. Never infer the interior of a second ball from the first, because mixed species can grow together. The [giant puffball guide](/mushrooms/giant-puffball-mushroom-guide/) shows why a uniform white interior is only one part of a much larger identity and condition check.

## Exclude an Amanita button explicitly

An immature Amanita can be enclosed in a universal veil and resemble a pale ball. A top-to-base section may show a miniature cap, tightly folded gills, and stem. Those organized structures are fundamentally different from a homogeneous gleba. Photograph the section before it dries or discolors.

If any internal cap, gill, stem, chamber, or layered organization is visible, stop using puffball or earthball assumptions and move to a regional key with expert support. Some Amanita species are deadly. A partial slice or a photograph of the outside cannot exclude them.

## Keep poisoning guidance direct

Common earthball is considered poisonous and has been associated with gastrointestinal symptoms. It should not be eaten. Folk preparation, removal of the rind, cooking, or selecting only pale interiors does not make an uncertain specimen safe. Pets and children should not be encouraged to handle or kick mature fruit bodies where exposure is a concern.

After suspected ingestion, contact a poison center, veterinarian, or emergency medical service as appropriate. Retain intact specimens, cross-sections, meal remnants, photographs, site details, and timing. Do not wait for symptoms or an online identification before seeking professional advice.

## Use microscopy and regional records at species level

Several Scleroderma species overlap in color and scaling. Spore size and ornament, wall structure, base, ecology, and region may be required to distinguish them. Mature spores are useful, but opening a fruit body releases a dense cloud; work outdoors or in controlled laboratory conditions and avoid inhalation.

Occurrence databases help establish where named records exist, yet records vary in verification quality. Confirm the current name, voucher, identifier, coordinates, and geographic plausibility. If the evidence supports only Scleroderma, a careful genus-level record is better than a forced species name.

## Create a repeatable earthball field record

Number each fruit body before cutting. Record maximum width and height, rind thickness, scale dimensions, internal color, odor without tasting, base form, cord thickness, soil, slope, drainage, tree evidence, weather, and maturity. Photograph an untouched group, a cleaned base, a full vertical section, and a surface macro under neutral light.

The AI cover is an editorial reconstruction, not a voucher or proof that the species occurs at a location. A publishable observation should connect images, notes, specimen, microscopy when needed, and a regional identification path.

## Section the rind on a measured axis

An earthball's outer wall is not just “thick.” Photograph the intact fruit body with its base, then cut one mature specimen from apex through attachment. Measure wall thickness at the top, side, and basal third with a millimeter scale. Record whether the interior is firm white, marbled, purple-black, powdery, or chambered. Link every section photograph to the intact specimen number so an attractive cross-section is not detached from surface and habitat evidence.

Repeat the section on three size classes from the same colony when collection rules permit. A developmental grid can show the gleba changing from pale and solid to dark and powdery while the rind cracks or opens. Do not infer edibility from a white interior: several hazardous or unresolved puffball-like fungi are pale when immature, and an Amanita button may also be white inside but reveals organized cap, gill, and stem tissue on sectioning.

## Record how deeply the base enters the substrate

Clear soil from one side of the base and measure the depth to firm attachment. Note compact mineral soil, moss, litter, rotting wood, turf, or buried debris. Earthballs may have rootlike mycelial cords or a narrowed sterile base, but they do not have the cap, free gills, ring, and central stem of an Agaricus. Restore the site after the minimal inspection.

This distinction is especially useful near lawns and paths where closed Agaricus buttons can appear ball-like. The [yellow stainer guide](/mushrooms/yellow-stainer-mushroom-guide/) follows gill development, basal chrome-yellow bruising, phenolic odor, and a chocolate-brown spore deposit. Cutting through the vertical center exposes that internal organization; a superficial slice across the cap may miss it.

## Separate weather cracks from diagnostic surface texture

Photograph a sheltered and exposed fruit body under the same diffuse light. Dry weather can exaggerate polygonal cracking, while rain and abrasion can smooth or darken the surface. Record scale height, crack depth, loose soil, and recent precipitation rather than treating every wart as permanent anatomy. Use raking light gently and avoid aggressive sharpening that invents texture.

If the identification will support a range record, retain a mature dried voucher with rind, gleba, base, and habitat photographs. Microscopic spore ornamentation should be measured from mature material with calibrated optics and a stated sample size. An immature white gleba will not provide the same spore evidence, so developmental stage belongs beside every measurement.

## Frequently asked questions

## Measure rind and gleba development by maturity class

Divide a local group into externally intact, beginning-to-crack, and openly powdering stages without assuming they are identical. For a lawful representative from each class, measure maximum width, fruit-body height, rind thickness at the top and side, scale width, base depth, and the proportion of pale versus dark gleba. Photograph every section with one color card and scale. These measurements turn “thick skinned” and “dark inside” into observations that can be compared with a regional key.

The sectioning knife must be cleaned between specimens if material will be retained for study; otherwise dark spores from a mature earthball can contaminate a pale section. Label halves immediately. A young fruit body that shows organized gills or a stem is removed from the earthball series and treated as an enclosed agaric, with Amanita safety precautions.

Drying changes both rind and gleba. Note fresh measurements before preserving a voucher, then state the drying method and final mass if that information matters. Never describe a collapsed dried section as if it retained fresh wall thickness.

## Record spore release without creating an exposure cloud

Mature earthballs release fine spores when the rind ruptures or is compressed. Do not kick, squeeze, or blow into fruit bodies for a photograph. That behavior destroys the spatial record and can create avoidable inhalation exposure. Photograph natural cracks and surrounding deposition from a comfortable distance, particularly around children, pets, or people with respiratory vulnerability.

Laboratory examination should use appropriate containment and a small labeled sample. Record whether spores came from a naturally mature gleba or surface contamination, calibrate the microscope, and measure an adequate series. Ornament and size can help separate *Scleroderma* taxa, but published ranges must come from a treatment relevant to the region and species concept.

## Interpret woodland distribution with soil context

Occurrence near oak, beech, birch, or pine is meaningful only when the tree and soil evidence are recorded. Note dominant trees, distance to trunks, litter, visible roots, slope, drainage, compaction, and any acidic-soil indicators available from site records. A path bank can expose mineral soil and concentrate fruit bodies without representing the wider stand evenly.

Map specimens rather than reporting a vague colony. Repeated fruiting in the same root neighborhood can support an ecological association, while one earthball transported in mulch cannot. Public distribution maps are shaped by observer effort, access, and taxonomic revision; absence of a dot is not proof of biological absence, and presence of a dot is not confirmation of a new specimen.

### What separates an earthball from a puffball?

Common earthball has a thick coarse scaly rind and an interior that becomes dark purple-black; true puffballs differ in skin, internal organization, opening, and microscopic traits.

### Is common earthball edible?

No. It is considered poisonous and can cause gastrointestinal illness; this page does not provide food clearance for any round fungus.

### Why cut a round fungus from top to base?

A full vertical section shows the rind, internal color, sterile base, and any hidden cap, gills, or stem that would indicate an immature gilled mushroom.

### Does a white center prove a safe puffball?

No. Immature earthballs and enclosed gilled mushrooms can contain pale tissue. Identity, structure, maturity, habitat, and expert confirmation all matter.

### Where does common earthball grow?

It often occurs on acidic woodland or heath soil near trees, with a short base and mycelial cords anchoring it to the ground.
