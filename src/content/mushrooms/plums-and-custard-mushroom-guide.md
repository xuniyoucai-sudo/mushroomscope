## Start with the color pattern, then test it

Plums and custard, *Tricholomopsis rutilans*, has one of the most memorable palettes in temperate woodland: wine-purple scales lie over a custard-yellow cap, while the gills and stem remain yellow. That contrast is a useful search image, not a verdict. Weather can bleach the yellow, rain can flatten scales, and camera processing can turn red-purple into brown. A defensible record joins surface structure to gills, stem, spores, decaying wood, region, and age.

Photograph the entire group before handling it. Include the stump or log, bark remnants, cut end, needles, and nearby trees. Give each fruit body a number and show at least one young convex cap, one expanded cap, an underside, and the complete base. If mushrooms seem to arise from soil, test gently for buried wood without excavating living roots or dismantling the site.

## Measure cap scales across a developmental series

Young caps are convex and may carry a dense dark purple-red covering. As the cap expands, the yellow ground color opens between fibrous scales. The center often stays more densely colored while the margin becomes yellower. Measure cap width, scale-patch width at center and margin, and the proportion of visible yellow background on several ages.

Use a hand lens to determine whether the purple material is attached fibrils or loose debris. Photograph in open shade with a neutral color reference. Wet scales can look nearly brown-black; dry caps may look pinker or more ochre. Preserve original files instead of pushing saturation until the common name appears obvious.

Check the margin for veil remnants but do not assume every fibrous edge predicts a ring. Record whether the cap is dry, tacky after rain, cracked, insect damaged, or abraded. A single worn cap may have lost much of the pattern, while a sheltered young neighbor preserves it.

## Confirm yellow gills and their attachment

Turn one intact cap far enough to show the gills. They are crowded and yellow, often with shorter gills between those running from margin to stem. Photograph the stem junction directly so attached, notched, or slightly decurrent interpretations can be reviewed. The [gill-types guide](/identification/mushroom-gill-types/) explains why an oblique underside image is insufficient.

Record gill color across age and after bruising. The important comparison is not simply “yellow”: it is whether mature gills stay yellowish or acquire a rusty-brown cast from maturing spores. Several unrelated wood agarics begin yellow and later reveal a brown-spored identity.

Make a spore deposit from a mature, undamaged cap on half black and half white nonabsorbent surfaces. *T. rutilans* should give a white to pale cream mass. A thin deposit can borrow color from paper, so record time, cap maturity, condensation, and deposit density. The [spore-color guide](/identification/mushroom-spore-colors/) provides a controlled method.

## Preserve stem fibers and the wood attachment

The stem is yellow and can carry wine-red or purple-red fibrils, particularly toward the lower portion. It lacks a persistent ring and volva. Measure length, width at top and base, surface color, fiber density, interior condition, and bruising. Preserve the whole base: trimming it can hide both a veil clue and the direct wood connection.

Section one numbered specimen vertically. Photograph cap flesh, gill depth, stem interior, insect channels, and the interface with rotten wood. Fresh flesh is yellowish; waterlogging, larvae, freezing, and decay can change texture. Do not use a generic word such as “firm” without noting specimen condition.

If several stems arise from one stump, map their spacing rather than assuming a fused cluster. Document whether bases share the same crack, bark pocket, or buried fragment. This makes the saprotrophic relationship reviewable and distinguishes direct wood growth from a merely nearby tree.

## Read conifer wood as evidence, not proof

Plums and custard is most often associated with decaying conifer wood. Stumps and fallen logs are straightforward when bark, cones, needles, branch arrangement, or forestry records support the wood identity. Advanced brown rot may erase those clues. In a mixed woodland, call the substrate “heavily decayed wood, probably conifer” unless the evidence is stronger.

Record log diameter, decay class, bark coverage, moisture, ground contact, shade, and surrounding tree species. A fruit body on a saw-cut stump has different host evidence from one apparently emerging two meters away through litter. Revisit the same wood after rain and map new fruit bodies; recurrence supports a persistent colony but does not independently confirm the species name.

Managed timber, old steps, buried boards, and woodchip paths can also support wood decayers. Note treatment, paint, road runoff, or other contamination. Even a correct identification does not turn a fruit body from treated wood into food.

## Separate similar yellow wood agarics

*Tricholomopsis decora* is an important close comparison. It is often smaller and carries darker olive-brown to blackish scales on yellow, but size, scale color, and density overlap. Compare multiple ages, substrate, spore dimensions, cap-surface structure, and a current regional key. Do not force a purple-versus-black choice from a processed photograph.

Wood-growing *Gymnopilus* species can be yellow, orange, or scaled. Their spore deposits are rusty brown, and mature gills commonly shift toward rusty or orange-brown tones. Some contain psychoactive or toxic compounds. A pale-looking young gill surface cannot substitute for a mature spore record.

Scaled *Pholiota* species also occur on wood and generally produce brown spores. Veil tissue, a ring or ring zone, cap viscosity, scale architecture, gill maturation, and host all matter. The [shaggy scalycap guide](/mushrooms/shaggy-scalycap-mushroom-guide/) demonstrates why a dramatic scaled cap is a starting point for comparison rather than edible clearance.

Yellow *Tricholoma* species may occur on soil in mycorrhizal relationships rather than directly on a rotten stump. Buried wood can blur that distinction, so retain the entire attachment. A white spore deposit alone will not separate every pale-spored agaric.

## Keep microscopy linked to the voucher

When macroscopic evidence is incomplete, microscopy should remain traceable to the photographed fruit body. Report equipment calibration, mount, number of spores measured, length and width ranges, shape, and any cap-surface or cystidial characters used by the chosen key. Do not paste measurements from a reference into a field record.

Dry representative cap, gill, stem, and basal tissue promptly with the collection number. Store the substrate description, coordinates at appropriate privacy, date, collector, image filenames, and provisional determination. A clean voucher allows a specialist to revisit an old name if regional taxonomy changes.

GBIF occurrence maps are useful for locating documented records, but a map point does not prove presence at a new site. Check the underlying record, identification basis, date, and geographic precision. An introduced or overlooked population should be documented, not rejected solely because a simplified range map lacks a dot.

## Apply a conservative food boundary

Sources differ on edibility and often describe this mushroom as poor, bitter, or not worthwhile. Those statements cannot authenticate a collection or predict individual tolerance. Similar-looking wood agarics include brown-spored and potentially toxic taxa, and old specimens deteriorate rapidly. This guide therefore treats plums and custard as inedible and gives no preparation advice.

Do not taste unknown mushrooms to resolve a name. If illness follows wild mushroom ingestion, contact the relevant poison center or medical service and save uncooked material, leftovers, site photographs, preparation details, amount, and symptom times. Do not induce vomiting unless directed by a professional.

## Build a contradiction-led record

A strong record contains the undisturbed group, confirmed or qualified wood substrate, cap measurements across ages, scale close-ups in neutral light, yellow gills and attachment, complete stems and bases, section, pale spore deposit, and microscopy when required. Run a contradiction audit: rusty spores, a persistent ring, a volva, soil attachment without wood, gills that mature brown, or a smooth unscaled cap should redirect the identification.
