Eyelash cup fungus turns a wet log into a miniature landscape. Each fruit body is an orange to scarlet disc only a few millimeters across, edged with stiff dark hairs. Magnification makes the rim look unmistakable, yet the name *Scutellinia scutellata* should not be assigned from “orange cup plus eyelashes” alone. The genus contains close lookalikes whose spores and hairs must be measured.

## Find scale before interpreting shape

Make the first photograph wide enough to show the log, branch, bark fragment, or plant debris. Then add a millimeter scale beside the colony without crushing it. Phone macro modes can make a three-millimeter cup look as large as a saucer, erasing one of the most useful facts.

Young fruit bodies may be nearly spherical before opening. Mature ones become shallow cups or flat discs; crowded individuals can distort one another. Measure several rather than reporting the largest. Show the attachment edge because these cups are effectively sessile and should not have the conventional stem suggested by some image-search results.

## Examine the orange disc and dark rim separately

The upper fertile surface is smooth and orange-red to scarlet. Color can fade toward orange-brown with age or drying. The exterior and margin carry brown to nearly black hairs, most conspicuous as a fringe around the rim. Photograph them in side light and with magnification; a bright overhead reflection can hide the hairs against wet wood.

Record whether the hairs are evenly distributed, longer at the margin, straight or curved, and whether they appear rooted deeply in the tissue. These observations guide microscopy but do not settle the species. Dirt fibers and moss rhizoids can mimic a fringe in a low-resolution photograph.

## Confirm the decaying substrate

Eyelash cups are frequently recorded from very damp, decayed wood. The wood may be so soft and partly buried that the cups appear to grow from soil. Trace the substrate beyond the colony. Record bark presence, wood texture, diameter, contact with running water, shade, and whether the material is hardwood or conifer when supportable.

Do not invent a host from nearby trees. A fallen branch may have moved, and advanced decay removes bark characters. The [habitat identification guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) provides a way to label wood identity as confirmed, probable, or unknown. Substrate uncertainty is useful data, not a defect to conceal.

## Why “eyelash fungus” is a genus-level clue

Multiple *Scutellinia* species produce bright discs with marginal hairs. Some differ mainly in spore ornamentation visible under suitable microscopy, plus spore size and the length, width, septation, wall thickness, and rooting pattern of hairs. A global photograph gallery flattens these distinctions and often carries older determinations forward.

Treat *S. scutellata* as a hypothesis. Preserve locality, substrate, dimensions, and a small lawful sample if specialist review is warranted. Use a current regional key and compare its measurement protocol. A cautious record of *Scutellinia* species is better than a precise name unsupported by the necessary characters.

## Microscopy connects the name to evidence

The exposed upper surface is an apothecium: asci line the disc and release spores. A conventional spore print under a cap is not the right workflow. A mature cup can be mounted or positioned to capture discharged spores, while sections allow examination of asci, paraphyses, outer tissue, and hairs.

For spores, record length and width from an adequate sample, shape, oil droplets when relevant, and ornamentation using the preparation specified by the key. Ornament can appear as warts, ridges, or a partial network and may require the correct stain, focus, and optical setup. For hairs, measure several intact examples from known positions. One broken hair or immature spore should not drive the determination.

## Separate other orange cup fungi

The [scarlet elf cup guide](/mushrooms/scarlet-elf-cup-guide/) covers much larger stalked or short-based cups on buried sticks, without the same obvious black eyelash fringe. The [orange peel fungus guide](/mushrooms/orange-peel-fungus-guide/) covers larger ground-dwelling cups shaped like torn orange peel. Scale, attachment, exterior texture, and habitat divide those search intents before microscopy.

*Cheilymenia* species can be small, orange, and hairy, and some favor dung or enriched soil. Hair color and structure, substrate, spores, and excipular anatomy help separate them. Other tiny discomycetes may be yellow, red, or orange without a conspicuous dark fringe. Never use brightness alone as a genus character.

## Build a colony map

Select a fixed section of log, such as ten centimeters, and count cups by developmental class: closed, cupped, flat, collapsed, or damaged. Record diameter for a representative sample and note their position relative to bark cracks, exposed fibers, moss, standing water, and shaded undersides. Repeat after rain or a drying interval without moving the substrate.

This map reveals how hydration affects apparent abundance. A dry colony can shrink until it is nearly invisible, then reopen after moisture returns. It also stops a photographer from claiming that every red dot seen on a different date is the same fruit body.

## Moisture, decay, and microclimate

Fruit bodies occur where the surface remains moist enough for development and spore discharge. “Wet habitat” is still too broad: distinguish splash zones, saturated wood, humid hollows, shaded log undersides, and temporary rain films. Measure wood-surface temperature and local humidity only if the instruments are calibrated and their placement is recorded.

The fungus participates in a community of decomposers rather than acting alone. Mosses, algae, bacteria, insects, and other fungi occupy the same fragment. Visible cups demonstrate reproduction at that moment; they do not quantify decay rate or prove that *Scutellinia* caused every structural change in the wood.

## Photographing hairs without creating artifacts

Use a stable camera support, diffuse natural light, and a scale. Focus stacking can improve coverage but must be disclosed when used, because merging frames can duplicate or erase hairs. Keep an unstacked context frame. Avoid sharpening halos, aggressive saturation, and black-point adjustments that turn every rim fiber into a diagnostic hair.

Photograph the same cup from above and at substrate level. The top view records disc shape and crowding; the side view records hairs, cup depth, and attachment. Include one frame that shows the entire substrate so a reviewer can check that the colony was not staged.

## Collection and contamination controls

When collection is lawful and justified, remove a tiny substrate chip bearing several maturity stages and keep it humid rather than submerged. Assign one field number, record coordinates and date, and prevent contact with other collections. Minute cups detach easily, so a hard container is safer than a folded paper packet during transport; drying or preservation then follows the receiving herbarium's instructions.

Clean tools between colonies. A microscopy slide containing spores from a previous orange cup creates false precision. Keep raw images, measurement sheets, slide labels, and any voucher identifier connected so the species name can be revised without losing the observation.

## Food and exposure boundary

Tiny size does not equal harmlessness, and bright color does not equal poison. The sensible conclusion is narrower: this is not a food target, and species-level identification can require microscopy. Do not taste unknown cup fungi or encourage collection for novelty cooking.

If a child or animal may have ingested an unknown fungus, do not rely on a web image match. Contact the appropriate poison service, veterinarian, or medical provider, retain material and photographs, and record timing and symptoms. Emergency guidance takes priority over completing an identification.

## A field workflow for checkable records

1. Photograph the colony and whole substrate before moving anything.
2. Add a millimeter scale and record cup diameters.
3. Make top, side, fringe, and attachment views.
4. Record wood identity only to the confidence supported by evidence.
5. Sample multiple maturity stages when permitted.
6. Measure spores, ornamentation, asci, and hairs with a regional key.
7. Preserve the record at genus level when decisive characters are missing.

The workflow protects the two things most often lost online: true scale and the link between microscopic evidence and a particular colony.

## Evidence limits and image disclosure

Taxonomic databases help reconcile names and locate records; they do not authenticate a photograph. Forest-fungi literature supplies ecological context but does not make one substrate universal. Toxicology guidance supports conservative response after exposure rather than a claim about edibility.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are the black hairs visible without a microscope?

They can be visible with a hand lens or strong macro photograph, especially around a mature rim. Species-level hair measurements still require microscopy.

### Why do cups disappear during dry weather?

Thin fruit bodies shrink, darken, or collapse as surface moisture changes. Repeat photographs can distinguish temporary contraction from loss or decay.

### Can I identify the wood from the fungus?

No. Identify the substrate from its own evidence. Advanced decay may justify recording only “decayed wood” rather than guessing a tree species.

### Does the common name refer to one species worldwide?

Not reliably. The name is widely applied to *S. scutellata*, but similar members of the genus require regional and microscopic comparison.

### Is a phone macro image enough for a record?

It is valuable when paired with scale, substrate, locality, and multiple angles. It normally cannot resolve spore ornamentation or hair anatomy.
