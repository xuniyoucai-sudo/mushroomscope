**Crystal brain fungus**, *Myxarium nucleatum*, forms small translucent whitish jellies on dead broadleaf wood. Fresh lobes may contain opaque white cores or granules that look suspended inside clear tissue. Those inclusions inspired the common name and provide a useful clue, but a clear blob with white material is not a complete identification.

The page’s purpose is to build a specimen-linked record: exact wood, scale, lobe architecture, section, hydration sequence, microscopic structures, regional literature, and nomenclatural history. It does not provide food clearance from an image.

## Find the attachment before handling the jelly

Photograph the occupied branch in place, including bark, exposed wood, nearby leaves, and its position above or against soil. Then trace each jelly to its attachment. Rain droplets, snail eggs, sap, ice, and other fungi can occupy the same wet wood; the context frame prevents a macro close-up from becoming detached from its source.

Record whether the branch is broadleaf or conifer, standing or fallen, barked or decorticated, firm or deeply softened. Do not infer a host from the nearest living tree. Transported firewood and crossed crowns are common sources of error. When host evidence is weak, “dead broadleaf wood” is more honest than an unsupported species name.

Number separate colonies along the wood. A millimeter scale should sit beside, not on, the fruit body. Note total occupied length and whether bodies follow bark cracks, cut ends, or the sheltered underside. The [habitat guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps keep wood observations separate from assumptions about the surrounding forest.

## Measure a water-rich body in three dimensions

Choose several intact lobes. Measure maximum width, projection from wood, height, and thickness at a visible edge. Include one direct view, one profile, and one oblique frame. “Small blob” hides the dimensions needed to compare other jelly fungi.

Describe lobe boundaries: rounded, folded, fused, cerebriform, cushion-like, or spreading. Record whether adjacent bodies coalesce after rain. A large wet mass may be several merged fruit bodies rather than one oversized individual.

Handle minimally. Pressure changes shape and can push inclusions through the tissue, creating structures that were not present in situ. If collection is lawful, remove one representative body with a sliver of attached wood and keep untouched neighbors for follow-up.

## Photograph transparency and white cores honestly

Use diffuse daylight, a neutral background card placed behind but not touching the specimen, and exposures that preserve highlights. Make both reflected-light and transmitted-light frames. The outer tissue may range from nearly clear to milky white, while internal inclusions remain opaque.

Record the number, size, position, and hardness of visible white cores. Section one mature body with a clean blade and photograph the cut face immediately. Determine whether the white material is truly enclosed, attached at the base, or merely wood debris seen through transparent tissue.

Do not call every pale center a “crystal.” The inclusions are a macroscopic clue, not a home mineral test. Their visibility changes with hydration, age, exposure, and section angle. Use a current key and microscopy to interpret them.

## Build a marked wet-to-dry sequence

Jelly fungi can appear to vanish as water leaves their tissues. Mark one colony with a nearby removable scale and photograph it from a fixed position after rain, during drying, and after natural rehydration. Record temperature, rainfall interval, exposure, and elapsed time.

Measure the same lobes where possible. Note whether they shrink into a translucent film, a whitish crust, or an inconspicuous nub. Do not pour water onto the fungus to manufacture recovery. Natural sequences avoid changing the wood moisture and washing contaminants across the hymenium.

Rehydration supports recognition of jelly-like tissue but is not species-specific. The value of the sequence is specimen continuity: it shows that a dry remnant and a wet lobe belong to the same marked patch.

## Separate snow fungus by scale, architecture, and ecology

Snow fungus, *Tremella fuciformis*, can be white and translucent, but it commonly develops larger, more elaborate leaf-like fronds. It also has a parasitic relationship involving another wood-decay fungus rather than functioning as a simple visual twin.

The [snow fungus guide](/mushrooms/snow-fungus-guide/) covers that separate organism and its cultivated-food context. Compare occupied area, lobe thickness, branching, internal white cores, associated fungi, and microscopic structures. Do not merge the two because both photographs look like white jelly.

White brain fungus and other *Tremella*, *Exidia*, and *Myxarium* species can also overlap. Some contain a firm internal host-derived core; others differ mainly under the microscope. Section position, host association, spore and basidial characters, and regional taxonomy matter more than the common name “brain.”

## Treat the historical name as traceable evidence

Many sources and records use *Exidia nucleata*. Current treatments may place the species in *Myxarium*. Preserve the name exactly as it appears in each source, together with author, date, identifier, and database access date. This lets a reviewer reconcile older observations without pretending that names never change.

Search occurrence databases under both combinations, then open underlying records. Check whether they have images, vouchers, coordinates, collection dates, and determiner information. A map dot is a lead, not proof that the species occurs on a new branch.

For sequence records, document locus, read quality, reference voucher, and the taxonomic concept used by the reference. A top database match cannot repair a contaminated sample or a misnamed reference.

## Make microscopy reproducible

Tie every slide to a numbered photographed fruit body. State whether tissue came from the fertile surface, interior, or attachment. Record mount medium, stain, calibration, magnification, and number of structures measured. Report full ranges rather than only an average.

Jelly-fungus identification may require basidial form, septation, spores, hyphae, and other specialized structures specified by a regional key. Mature tissue matters. A collapsed old lobe may provide poor material even when it once looked characteristic.

Attempting a white spore deposit is possible but often unrewarding on a small wet body. Suspend the fertile surface over clean glass without crushing it, use a blank control, and protect the chamber from dripping condensation. Confirm any pale residue microscopically.

## Map moisture without confusing visibility and abundance

Divide the branch into equal zones and record bark cover, decay hardness, ground contact, shade, moss, and surface moisture. Count visible fresh, shrinking, and dry remnants after comparable weather intervals. Include an unoccupied section on the same wood.

The result measures visible fruiting, not the whole organism. Wet weather may make existing tissue conspicuous without creating a new colony. Internal moisture pathways, wood structure, competing fungi, and microtopography can explain clustering. Report association rather than claiming that moss, shade, or one rainfall event caused fruiting.

Finding the fungus on dead wood also does not prove it killed a branch or tree. Separate decomposition after death from the cause of death.

## Keep collection, food, and poisoning limits conservative

Crystal brain fungus is small, water-rich, and easily confused. This page does not recommend it as food. A historical edibility note, absence of a known toxin, or similarity to a cultivated jelly does not authenticate a specimen or address contamination and individual response.

Do not taste an unknown jelly fungus. Collect only where lawful and proportionate, leaving enough material for ecology and future observation. Wet wood beside roads, treated timber, and contaminated sites adds exposure questions even when a name is correct.

If symptoms follow ingestion of any wild fungus, contact local emergency or poison-control services promptly. Save uncooked material, leftovers, photographs, site details, preparation, amount, and symptom times. Do not delay care for an online identification.

## Build a reviewable crystal-brain record

1. Show the whole wood unit and exact attachments.
2. Measure multiple bodies in direct, profile, and oblique views.
3. Photograph transparency with reflected and transmitted light.
4. Section one body to locate the opaque white material.
5. Follow a marked colony through natural drying and rehydration.
6. Compare white *Tremella*, *Exidia*, and *Myxarium* candidates.
7. Link microscopy and nomenclature to a numbered voucher.
8. Keep decay, distribution, and food conclusions within the evidence.

The useful search image is not simply “white jelly.” It is a small hardwood-attached, translucent, lobed body whose internal opaque inclusions, hydration behavior, microscopy, and regional name treatment agree without contradiction.
