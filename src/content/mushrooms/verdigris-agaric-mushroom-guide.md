The **verdigris agaric**, *Stropharia aeruginosa*, combines an unusual blue-green cap with white veil fragments and dark spores. Those features look decisive in a fresh textbook specimen, yet rain can wash away scales, drying can fade color, and age can darken the gills. The reliable subject is therefore a developmental sequence, not a turquoise thumbnail.

This guide separates observed cap surface, veil anatomy, gill maturation, spores, habitat, region, and lookalikes. It treats the mushroom as not for food and does not infer psychoactive chemistry from a photograph or genus name.

## Photograph the cap before rain evidence disappears

Begin with the undisturbed group in diffuse light. Fresh caps may be viscid or sticky, blue-green to verdigris, and decorated with irregular white veil scales. Photograph the surface at a shallow angle so moisture and texture remain visible. Record whether it has rained, whether litter adheres to the cap, and whether the center is already fading toward yellow-green or ochre.

Avoid boosting cyan saturation. Include a stable gray reference and repeat the frame after the cap surface dries. Verdigris color often looks stronger at the margin or in younger tissue, while exposed centers fade first. A moisture series distinguishes a biological transition from camera processing.

## Map veil remnants across several ages

Young caps can carry conspicuous white scales, especially near the margin. These are remnants of a veil, not permanent spots painted into the cap. Expansion separates them; rain and abrasion remove them. Photograph a button, an expanding cap, and a mature cap from the same group before requiring every specimen to share one pattern.

Turn attention to the stem as well. A fragile ring or ring zone may catch falling spores and become dark above. Fibers or scales can occur below. The [veils and rings guide](/identification/mushroom-veils-rings-guide/) explains how to record a complete membrane, a collapsed ring, and a mere color boundary without treating them as interchangeable.

## Follow gills from pale gray to purple-brown

Photograph intact undersides across maturity stages. Young gills may be pale gray; mature spores shift the visible surface through violet-gray to purple-brown. Pale or whitish gill edges can be useful in comparison with the blue roundhead, but the observation requires a sharp macro image and should not override all other evidence.

Record attachment before pulling cap and stem apart. The gills are attached, and damage can create a false gap. Count full-length and shorter gills across a fixed arc, note mottling from uneven spore maturation, and keep each underside linked to its cap and stem.

## Make a heavy, labeled dark spore deposit

Set a mature cap across white and dark nonabsorbent surfaces, cover it against drafts, and label collection number and time. A good deposit should be purple-brown to purple-black. Brown gills seen through reflected light are not a print, and a black-looking smear without known cap origin is not useful evidence.

Use the [spore-print method](/identification/how-to-make-spore-print/) to preserve provenance. Dark spores support a *Stropharia* direction but do not separate *S. aeruginosa* from every relative. Microscopic spore dimensions, germ pore, cystidia, cap cuticle, and veil tissues may be necessary in a current regional key.

## Record the complete stem and base

Lift one legal specimen with the base intact and photograph all sides. The stem is pale and may carry blue-green tones, a ring or ring zone, and scattered fibrils below. Soil, mycelium, and torn litter can imitate scales; brush only a small comparison area and preserve the untouched surface in another image.

There should be no cup-like volva. Nevertheless, absence of a volva in one broken specimen proves little. Document several intact bases and keep unexpected structures as reasons to reopen the identification rather than explain them away.

## Separate humus from visible wood

Verdigris agaric is saprotrophic in humus-rich ground, litter, grass, and decaying organic debris. Record whether fruit bodies arise from mineral soil, thick leaf litter, buried chips, a decayed log, or parkland turf. Apparent soil growth can conceal woody debris, while proximity to a log does not prove direct attachment.

Photograph the substrate at the base, surrounding vegetation, canopy, slope, and moisture. First Nature notes woodland and pasture contexts in its European account. Local habitat frequency varies, so a familiar British setting should not be projected automatically onto a North American record.

## Distinguish the blue roundhead with a character matrix

*Stropharia caerulea* is the closest common source of confusion. It is often paler blue-green; scales may be evident mainly when young; and mature gills are described without the same contrasting white edges. None of these is an infallible one-feature rule because weather erases scales and image exposure changes edge contrast.

Build a small matrix for cap viscosity, scale persistence, gill-edge color, ring development, spore measurements, habitat, and region. Score unknown or missing features honestly. If the matrix remains mixed, preserve a voucher and use microscopy or specialist review rather than choosing the more photogenic name.

## Exclude aniseed funnels by more than color

The aniseed funnel can be blue-green, but it lacks the slimy scaled *Stropharia* cap and ring, produces a pale rather than purple-black deposit, and usually has a strong anise-like odor. Its mature profile becomes more funnel-shaped. The [aniseed funnel guide](/mushrooms/aniseed-funnel-mushroom-guide/) keeps that odor-and-gill intent separate.

Odor is supportive, not permission to taste. Record it by wafting air near a fresh section and note observer agreement. Colds, contamination, age, and individual perception can make an absent odor weak evidence.

## Keep large wine caps in a different scale class

Wine caps share the genus and a dark deposit but are substantially larger and more robust, commonly burgundy to brown, with a strong wrinkled or cogwheel-like ring and frequent wood-chip habitat. The [wine cap guide](/mushrooms/wine-cap-mushroom-guide/) documents that cultivated and garden context.

Measure before using adjectives such as small or robust. A rain-faded wine cap and an old verdigris agaric can both lose their headline color, while cap diameter, stem thickness, ring construction, substrate, and microscopy preserve the distinction.

## Report distribution with record-quality limits

GBIF treats *Stropharia aeruginosa* as an accepted name and aggregates occurrence records. Use those records to bound a question, not answer it automatically. Note whether a point has a preserved specimen, image, coordinate uncertainty, and recent taxonomic review. Museum and citizen-science records carry different evidence.

First Nature provides a detailed European comparison with *S. caerulea*. NCBI supplies a lineage checkpoint. A collection in an undersampled region needs local literature and a voucher; a global map alone cannot resolve a species pair that has changed in historical treatments.

## Avoid unsupported toxicity and chemistry claims

Online accounts disagree about edibility and sometimes repeat chemical claims without specimen-linked analyses. This page does not state that every fruit body contains a particular psychoactive compound, nor does it translate absence of a confirmed compound into food safety. Species identity, chemistry, dose, contaminants, co-ingestants, and individual health are separate questions.

Treat the mushroom as not for food. If ingestion produces symptoms, contact local emergency services or poison control promptly. Keep uncooked specimens, leftovers, photographs, location, timing, and other exposure details. Do not induce vomiting unless a clinician directs it.

## Build an auditable verdigris agaric record

1. Photograph the group before handling, with color reference and weather notes.
2. Record sticky cap texture and scale distribution across several ages.
3. Document the ring or ring zone and the full stem below it.
4. Photograph gill attachment and color maturation under neutral light.
5. Make a labeled purple-brown to purple-black spore deposit.
6. Describe humus, litter, grass, or buried wood at the actual base.
7. Compare blue roundhead, aniseed funnel, and wine cap in a character matrix.
8. Preserve a voucher and state uncertainty when microscopy is unavailable.

The [identification safety checklist](/identification/mushroom-identification-safety-checklist/) is the stopping rule when essential evidence is missing.

Archive raw photographs as well as edited copies so later reviewers can inspect the original color, exposure, scale, and edge detail.
