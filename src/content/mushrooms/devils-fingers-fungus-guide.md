**Devil's fingers**, *Clathrus archeri*, is a stinkhorn whose fruit body changes so completely that the egg and mature stages can look unrelated. A pale rounded structure first pushes through litter. It splits, and several red-orange arms unfold, spreading apart while irregular patches of olive-brown spore slime produce a strong carrion-like odor. The useful field record is therefore a sequence—egg, split volva, expanding arms, gleba, insect removal, and collapse—not a single dramatic photograph.

This page owns the species-level record for *C. archeri*. The [common stinkhorn guide](/mushrooms/common-stinkhorn-guide/) covers a single white column and conical head, while the [dog stinkhorn guide](/mushrooms/dog-stinkhorn-guide/) covers a slender column with gleba near its tip. A photograph cannot provide food clearance. Do not taste an unknown egg-stage fungus, because many unrelated fungi begin as pale enclosed structures.

## Establish the substrate before naming the fungus

Take a wide photograph before moving litter. Record whether the fruit body emerges from leaf mould, commercial wood chips, grass over buried debris, compost, or visibly decayed wood. Devil's fingers is saprotrophic, but a single view can hide the material it is decomposing. Measure litter depth and note nearby mulch boundaries, irrigation, drainage, recent rain, and disturbance.

Map every fruit body in a fixed area. Count unopened eggs, splitting eggs, upright arms, fully spread bodies, and collapsed remains. A mixed-stage group is more informative than one perfect mature specimen because it connects the egg to the red receptacle. Mark the positions without pulling all the eggs; retaining the site matters more than making a collection look complete.

Use the [habitat-recording guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) to separate visible substrate from inferred ecology. Nearby trees describe the setting but do not imply a mycorrhizal partner. In parks and gardens, imported mulch can explain a record better than the nearest trunk.

## Record the egg without treating it as diagnostic

Measure the egg above and below the litter line. Photograph its outer surface, attachment, and any pale root-like mycelial strands. The outside may be whitish, grayish, or dirt-stained and can feel firm over a gelatinous layer. Those features identify a developmental enclosure, not *C. archeri* by themselves.

If collection is legal and justified, section one representative egg from top to base with a clean blade. Photograph the cut surface immediately beside a scale. A developing devil's fingers egg can show folded red receptacle tissue inside translucent gel and an outer membrane. Keep the section tied to the numbered site record. Do not section every egg or assume that any red pigment proves the species.

The egg is the highest-risk stage for casual food claims. Puffballs should have a uniform interior when correctly mature and identified; stinkhorn eggs contain differentiated structures and gel. Amanita buttons can also be enclosed by universal veil tissue. None of these comparisons turns a web image into an edible decision.

## Count arms and document how they join

Photograph a newly opened body from the side and directly above. Count the arms, measure their length and width at base and midpoint, and record whether tips are upright, arched, or touching the ground. Four to eight arms are a practical field expectation, but count is variable and damaged bodies can lose tissue.

The structural question is whether the arms remain separate above a short shared base. Trace each arm visually to the split volva. In *Clathrus ruber*, branches reconnect to form a lattice; in devil's fingers, the mature outline is more like a star, hand, or octopus. A crowded or partly opened specimen may temporarily conceal that distinction, so revisit the same body after several hours if possible.

Use side light to show the porous, spongy surface without excessive sharpening. Record both inner and outer faces. Generated images and phone processing can exaggerate holes, color, and symmetry; actual specimens are often bent, torn, insect-visited, and unequal.

## Map gleba rather than describing a black fungus

Fresh gleba is a sticky olive-brown to nearly black-looking mass carried in irregular zones, especially on the upper inner arm surfaces. Photograph its position before insects remove it. Record color with a neutral reference, gloss, thickness, odor distance, and the proportion of each arm still covered.

Return at fixed intervals—such as 30 minutes, two hours, and the following morning—and photograph from the same position. Flies may create clean islands or trails through the gleba while transferring spores. A late specimen can retain only stains, so absence of abundant slime after insect activity is not the same as absence at emergence.

Do not make close contact merely to test odor. Describe when it becomes noticeable while approaching from downwind, and avoid transferring gleba to skin, clothing, pets, or equipment. Odor is an insect-attraction mechanism, not a calibrated species test.

## Preserve the complete volva and attachment

The torn pale egg remains as a cup or irregular lobes around the common base. Clear only enough litter to expose its outline. Photograph the arms entering the volva, the volva entering the substrate, and any attached cords. Measure the shared column before the arms separate.

A cropped photograph that omits the base loses the evidence connecting red arms to the egg. It also makes damage hard to interpret. If an arm has been broken or eaten, record the wound rather than reconstructing an ideal count from memory.

## Separate the principal lookalikes by architecture

The [common stinkhorn](/mushrooms/common-stinkhorn-guide/) has one porous white stem supporting a single conical or bell-shaped head. Its gleba covers that head rather than lying in patches along several red arms. The [dog stinkhorn](/mushrooms/dog-stinkhorn-guide/) is slimmer and columnar, with olive gleba concentrated near a pointed orange-red tip.

Red cage fungi require a connectivity test. Trace whether red branches reconnect into closed windows. *Clathrus ruber* creates a lattice; related columned stinkhorns may form joined arches. Devil's fingers should show free tapering arms above the basal union, though a body that has not finished expanding may require time before that pattern is clear.

Sea-anemone stinkhorns and other phalloid fungi vary by region. A record outside the familiar introduced range deserves particular care: retain multiple views, exact locality at an appropriate privacy level, stage sequence, measurements, and a specimen where regulations allow. Regional experts may need microscopic or molecular evidence for unusual records.

## Interpret distribution as a dated record

*Clathrus archeri* is native to Australia and New Zealand and has become established in parts of Europe, with scattered reports elsewhere. Distribution maps combine observations made under different names and verification standards. Treat the map as a history of records, not proof that a new photograph must or cannot be this species.

For a potentially new county, province, or national record, document date, coordinates, habitat, substrate, stage sequence, and introduced mulch pathways. Avoid claiming ecological invasion from one fruit body. Persistence across seasons and verified collections are stronger evidence than a viral photograph.

## Separate local persistence from one imported fruiting

When the fungus appears in a recently mulched bed, record the mulch supplier, material type, delivery date, storage history, and the boundary of the spread if those facts are available without invading privacy. Search the surrounding unmulched litter with the same effort and map both positive and negative plots. A fruit body beside a delivery pile supports a transport hypothesis; it does not prove where the underground culture originated.

Repeat the survey after comparable wet periods for at least one further season. Use the same plot dimensions and record maintenance such as mulch replacement, digging, irrigation, and fungicide use. Recurrence beyond the imported layer is more informative about local establishment than a single spectacular emergence. Conversely, failure to refind it is not proof of absence because fruiting is episodic.

Submit unusual records to a regional fungal recording scheme with photographs of the egg, free-arm architecture, volva, gleba distribution, substrate, and a legally retained voucher where requested. Keep public coordinates coarse for private gardens or sensitive sites while preserving precise coordinates in the expert record.

## Keep the safety boundary conservative

This guide does not recommend devil's fingers as food. The mature body is foul-smelling and easily contaminated by insects and substrate, while unopened eggs create a more consequential identification problem. Do not use appearance, odor, folklore, or a generated illustration to authorize ingestion.

If someone has eaten an unknown fungus, preserve a sample and photographs, record time and amount, and contact local poison-control or medical services. Do not wait for a webpage to match symptoms and do not induce vomiting unless a qualified professional instructs it.

## Build a repeatable developmental record

Choose one undisturbed egg and place a scale outside the fruiting path. Photograph at consistent height, direction, and focal length. Log temperature, recent rainfall, shade, and elapsed time. The valuable output is not a beauty sequence but measured change: membrane splitting, arm emergence, arm separation, gleba exposure, insect removal, loss of turgor, and collapse.

Number files by specimen and time. Keep raw images, field notes, and edits distinguishable. This creates a record another observer can audit and prevents photographs from separate bodies being presented as one developmental series.
