**Yellow stagshorn**, *Calocera viscosa*, makes small forest antlers from wet, decaying conifer wood. Its upright yellow-orange branches divide repeatedly and end in blunt, rounded tips. The surface looks gelatinous, yet the fruit body is tougher and more elastic than many jelly fungi. That combination supports a hypothesis; bright color alone does not.

This guide treats the fungus as a wood-decay record, not a food target. A trustworthy observation connects the entire branching body to its substrate and tests texture, architecture, microscopy, season, and region against similar *Calocera* and true coral fungi.

## Expose the substrate connection before naming it

Photograph the whole cluster in place, then trace one basal trunk to the wood. Conifer needles nearby do not prove conifer substrate, and a fruit body that appears soil-borne may arise from a buried root. Record whether the wood is a stump, log, root, or fragment; whether bark remains; its decay softness; and any visible tree characters.

Use a millimeter scale beside the cluster. Measure total height, basal width, maximum branch width, number of major forks, and occupied wood area. Map separate clusters instead of assuming all yellow branches belong to one individual. The [habitat documentation guide](/identification/mushroom-habitat-identification/) helps distinguish observed substrate from inferred host.

## Describe a branching sequence, not a silhouette

Select young, intermediate, and mature fruit bodies on the same wood unit. Photograph each from front, side, and above. Note where the first fork occurs, whether subsequent forks divide evenly, how often three-way branching appears, and whether tips remain rounded or become flattened with age. Include damaged tips separately.

True architecture is easy to distort when branches overlap. A single frontal image can turn two bodies into one impossible network. Rotate the camera, not the fungus, and use focus stacking only when the final image remains honest about edges. Do not count crossing branches as junctions.

## Record color under controlled light

Make one frame in diffuse daylight with a neutral gray reference. Record surface moisture and time since rain. Yellow may deepen toward orange with age or drying, while strong automatic saturation can create a neon color absent in the field. Describe the basal, middle, and terminal zones independently.

Repeat the frame after a natural weather change if the cluster persists. A color series can reveal drying and rehydration, but it does not identify the species by itself. Avoid destructive wetting, chemical tests, or flash settings that erase the translucent surface.

## Test texture without tasting

When collection is permitted, remove one proportionate branch with its base. Bend it gently and record whether it flexes, springs back, tears, or snaps. Cut a clean transverse section and photograph surface and interior. *Calocera* tissue is typically gelatinous-tough rather than the brittle, chalky, or fibrous texture associated with many superficially similar clubs and corals.

Use consistent words tied to a method. “Rubbery” without a bend distance or recovery observation is subjective. Never taste a wild branched fungus to complete a description, and do not squeeze every fruit body on the log.

## Separate true coral fungi by ecology and tissue

Yellow *Ramaria* and related coral fungi can produce complex branching, but many arise from soil or litter and have a different, more brittle flesh. The visible wood connection, rounded branch ends, gelatinous elasticity, and microscopic structures move a collection toward *Calocera*. None should be used alone.

Build a table with substrate, height, branching order, tip form, bruising, texture, spore deposit, spore measurements, basidia, and region. Mark uncertainty explicitly. A hidden piece of wood can make a wood decomposer appear terrestrial, while a coral growing beside a stump can appear wood-borne.

## Compare smaller Calocera species carefully

*Calocera cornea* is generally smaller and less branched and is commonly associated with broadleaf wood. Small or damaged *C. viscosa* can overlap that appearance. Measure several complete bodies rather than choosing the largest, and preserve the exact wood fragment with the voucher when lawful.

Other yellow clubs may be simple, sparsely forked, or grassland-associated. Regional keys differ in the limits they give to macroscopic characters. If the collection lacks a secure substrate and mature microscopy, retain *Calocera* or a broader descriptive label instead of forcing a species.

## Collect a deposit and a microscopic packet

Stand a mature branch over clean glass in a humid but ventilated chamber and protect it from drafts. Label collection number, orientation, duration, and temperature. A sparse pale-yellow deposit can be confused with surface fragments, pollen, or condensation residue, so keep a blank control and photograph the undisturbed result.

Microscopy should state mount medium, scale calibration, magnification, number measured, and full ranges. Record spores and the forked basidia characteristic of the family as required by the chosen key. The [spore-print guide](/identification/how-to-make-spore-print/) explains contamination controls, though branched fungi often demand a modified setup.

## Map decay zones without claiming causation

Divide the occupied log into fixed zones based on bark cover, softness, moisture, sun exposure, and ground contact. Count fresh, mature, and collapsed fruit bodies in each zone after comparable rain intervals. Include an adjacent unoccupied zone as a reference.

This map can show where visible fruiting concentrates. It cannot prove that one factor caused the pattern or reveal the full mycelial territory. Other decomposers, internal moisture pathways, wood chemistry, and previous damage remain possible explanations. Report correlations as observations.

## Measure recovery after a controlled bend

On one lawfully collected mature branch, mark a 20 mm segment above the base and photograph it against a grid. Deflect the tip gently by a recorded distance for five seconds, release it, and photograph immediately and after one minute. Record whether the branch returns, remains curved, tears, or leaks fluid. Run the same observation on a naturally damaged branch when available rather than consuming another intact specimen.

Recovery documents the elastic-gelatinous quality more clearly than the word “rubbery,” but it is not a diagnostic stress test. Temperature, water content, age, and prior frost can change the response. Pair the bend record with a clean section, branching map, conifer-wood attachment, spores, and basidia. Stop at the first sign of tearing; repeated flexing adds damage without useful taxonomic information. The purpose is a reproducible texture description, not a pass-fail value for *C. viscosa*.

## Use occurrence and sequence databases as leads

GBIF occurrence records can guide a search for regional vouchers and seasonality, but coordinates, identifiers, dates, and name revisions need checking. NCBI taxonomy and sequence-linked records help locate molecular context; a sequence match is only as useful as the locus, read quality, reference annotation, and species concept.

First Nature provides a European field account emphasizing the conifer substrate, branching, and gelatinous texture. Elsewhere, use local floras or an herbarium. A distribution map cannot authenticate a new photograph, and an old name should be reconciled with current taxonomy before publication.

## Keep food and safety boundaries explicit

Yellow stagshorn is small, tough, and easily confused with other branched fungi. This page does not recommend collecting it for food. Familiar color, lack of a dramatic warning feature, or a matching app result cannot establish edibility or exclude contamination and individual reactions.

If illness follows ingestion of any wild fungus, contact local emergency services or a poison center promptly. Retain uncooked material, leftovers, photographs, location, and timing. Do not delay care while seeking an online identification. Follow landowner and protected-area rules, and leave enough material for ecological function and later observation.

## Build a reviewable yellow-stagshorn record

1. Photograph the cluster and exact wood before disturbance.
2. Measure complete bodies and map separate bases.
3. Document successive forks and rounded tips from several angles.
4. Record color with neutral light and moisture context.
5. Test elasticity on one proportionate voucher without tasting.
6. Compare soil corals and smaller *Calocera* species.
7. Add a controlled deposit and calibrated microscopy.
8. Keep the name broad when substrate or microscopic evidence is missing.

A strong record connects the conspicuous yellow antlers to quiet evidence: one wood unit, measured architecture, repeatable texture observations, mature microscopic structures, and a regional key. That chain matters more than color recognition.
