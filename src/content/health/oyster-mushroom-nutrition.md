# Oyster Mushroom Nutrition: Food Data in Context

> **Safety scope:** This editorial guide explains evidence and documentation. It cannot confirm that a wild mushroom is edible, and it has no named expert or clinical reviewer.

## Define the food before comparing numbers

Oyster mushroom nutrition depends on species, raw or cooked state, serving mass, and recipe. Pleurotus ostreatus is the usual reference, but retail clusters may include other cultivated Pleurotus species. Database values describe sampled foods, not every package. Compare entries on the same basis—preferably per 100 grams and per realistic serving—before drawing conclusions.

Cooking drives off water, so nutrients may appear more concentrated per 100 grams even when no nutrient was added. Oil, butter, sauces, and salt can change energy, fat, and sodium more than the mushroom itself. Use a cooked database entry that matches the method rather than applying raw figures to a sautéed dish. Drain loss and cooking time also make exact home values uncertain. For the broader workflow, use [this related MushroomScope guide](/mushrooms/oyster-mushroom-guide/).

### A practical decision point

Oyster mushrooms can contribute B vitamins and minerals, while vitamin D varies especially with ultraviolet exposure and product handling. A package claim or matched laboratory entry is more useful than assuming every cluster has the same vitamin D. Beta-glucans are components of fungal cell walls, but the presence of a compound does not prove a clinical outcome from an ordinary serving. Oyster mushrooms provide both protein and dietary fiber, but they are not a concentrated protein food comparable gram-for-gram with legumes, eggs, fish, or meat. Their high water content keeps calories and macronutrients modest in a fresh serving. Calling them only fiber or only protein hides their actual role: a low-energy whole food contributing texture, fiber, some protein, and several micronutrients within a varied meal.
