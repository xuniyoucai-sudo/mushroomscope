## The practical answer

Mushrooms are not automatically a dependable source of vitamin D. The meaningful distinction is whether a **specific product** was deliberately exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light and whether its label states a vitamin D amount per serving. UV treatment can convert mushroom ergosterol to vitamin D2; the USDA Agricultural Research Service has documented that the amount can vary greatly between commercially treated samples. A mushroom type, a marketing photograph, or a home sunlight experiment is not a substitute for the package's nutrition information.

For everyday meal planning, use the label on the package you bought. For a health concern such as suspected deficiency, symptoms, an abnormal test, kidney disease, pregnancy, or a prescribed regimen, use a clinician's advice rather than trying to calculate treatment from foods.

## Why UV treatment changes the question

Mushrooms contain ergosterol, which can be converted to vitamin D2 after appropriate UV exposure. That is why an untreated retail mushroom and a UV-treated version of the same general type may have very different vitamin D values. USDA research on UV-exposed portabellas found wide lot-to-lot variation, which is a useful warning against assigning one fixed number to all “vitamin D mushrooms.”

The form matters, too. UV-treated mushrooms are generally a food source of vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol), while foods and supplements may supply different vitamin-D forms. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements discusses vitamin D2 and D3 together in dietary guidance. This page does not rank one product as right for every person; it explains how to decide whether the food in hand has a stated, relevant amount.

## What the human evidence supports—and does not

A small randomized controlled trial in adults with low baseline 25-hydroxyvitamin D tested UV-B-irradiated button mushrooms against comparator groups over five weeks. It is useful evidence that vitamin D2 from a specifically UV-treated mushroom preparation can be bioavailable. It is not evidence that every fresh mushroom, powder, extract, or recipe will have the same amount or effect.

That boundary matters. The trial involved a defined preparation, a defined dose, a short duration, and a small group of participants. It did not establish a home UV protocol, replace clinical assessment, or prove that a person with a medical condition should self-manage vitamin D with mushrooms. A food can contribute a nutrient without becoming a treatment.

## How to read a package before you buy it

Use this four-point check for a fresh, dried, powdered, or prepared mushroom product:

1. **Find the vitamin D line.** On a U.S. Nutrition Facts label, look for the amount and percent Daily Value per serving. If vitamin D is not listed or the producer provides no dependable value, do not assume UV treatment.
2. **Check the serving basis.** A claim about a cup, a whole package, or dry powder can describe very different quantities. Compare the stated serving with the amount you intend to eat.
3. **Look for the product description.** “UV-treated,” “UV-exposed,” or comparable wording may explain the nutrient value, but the listed amount is still the usable number.
4. **Keep the product identifiable.** Save a label photo, brand, lot, and best-by date if vitamin D is important to your diet. FoodData Central can provide useful reference entries, but it cannot certify the contents of an untested package in your refrigerator.

FDA explains how the Nutrition Facts panel presents nutrients per serving. Use it to compare products, not to turn a percent Daily Value into a personal diagnosis or a dosing plan.

## What not to infer from a mushroom label

- A product described as “mushroom” is not necessarily UV-treated or high in vitamin D.
- A vitamin D value for one brand or lot does not apply to another brand, species, recipe, or supplement.
- A study of UV-treated whole mushrooms does not validate mycelium powders, multi-ingredient blends, or an unlabeled extract.
- Sunshine on a countertop does not give a measured, food-safe, or repeatable vitamin D amount. Do not create a home treatment protocol from research conditions.
- A food contribution does not tell you whether your blood level is adequate or whether a supplement should be changed.

## A sensible way to use the information

Buy identified culinary mushrooms from reputable food sellers and follow ordinary storage and cooking guidance. If vitamin D is a purchasing goal, choose a product with a clear amount per serving and incorporate it as one food in a varied diet. Do not assume every serving provides the same amount after substitutions, different portion sizes, or a switch in brand.

Speak with a qualified clinician or pharmacist about vitamin D supplements and fortified foods if you have a diagnosed condition, take medicines, are pregnant or breastfeeding, are preparing for surgery, or are following treatment for a deficiency. Bring the exact product label. That conversation is safer and more useful than a general claim that mushrooms “fix” vitamin D.
