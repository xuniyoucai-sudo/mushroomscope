## Start with what is actually in the bottle

A mushroom supplement label should identify the scientific species, fungal part, amount per serving, extraction method, other ingredients, manufacturer, and lot number. Common names can refer to multiple species. “Fruiting body,” “mycelium,” “8:1,” “dual extract,” and “full spectrum” are manufacturing descriptions—not proof of identity, purity, dose, or benefit.

In the United States, FDA does not approve dietary supplements for safety and effectiveness before marketing. Market availability and a Supplement Facts panel therefore do not mean that a product has passed the kind of premarket effectiveness review required for a drug.

## A practical label checklist

1. **Identity:** accepted scientific name and the tested fungal material.
2. **Dose:** amount of extract or material per serving, not only capsule weight.
3. **Extraction:** solvent and ratio with enough information to interpret them.
4. **Composition:** a validated assay for a relevant constituent; “total polysaccharides” can include starch.
5. **Lot testing:** identity, microbes, heavy metals, pesticides where relevant, and other contaminants from an independent laboratory.
6. **Traceability:** lot number, date, manufacturer contact, and a certificate matching that lot.

A certificate of analysis is useful only if the laboratory, method, sample, units, acceptance limits, and lot are clear. A generic marketing certificate or a result for a raw ingredient does not necessarily describe the finished bottle.

## Match the claim to the study

Check whether a cited study tested the same species, fungal part, extract, dose, duration, population, and outcome. Cell or animal work cannot establish a human treatment benefit. A trial of one standardized product does not validate every retail item that shares a common mushroom name.

Be cautious with claims to diagnose, prevent, cure, or treat disease; FDA explains that such intended uses may make a product a drug. Testimonials, white coats, “clinically researched” wording, and a long reference list do not replace product-matched clinical evidence.

## Interactions and adverse events

Supplements can alter drug absorption, metabolism, laboratory tests, bleeding, anesthesia response, or immune effects. Risk depends on the exact ingredients and the person. Discuss products before use when taking medicines, receiving cancer or immune therapy, preparing for surgery, pregnant or breastfeeding, or managing liver, kidney, bleeding, allergy, or immune conditions.

Do not stop prescribed care. If a serious reaction occurs, stop the product, seek medical help, retain the label and lot number, and use the relevant adverse-event reporting system.
